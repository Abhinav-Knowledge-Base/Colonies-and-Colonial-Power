## Before

Episode 31 showed that empire did not need an ocean. By the late 19th century, two non-European powers — Japan and the United States — were building overseas empires alongside the Europeans.

## The turn

After the Meiji Restoration (1868), Japan incorporated Hokkaido (1869) and colonized Ainu lands through settlement and assimilation law (the Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act, 1899). It annexed the Ryukyu Kingdom as Okinawa Prefecture (1879). After defeating the Qing in 1894–95, it took Taiwan. After defeating Russia in 1904–05, it made Korea a protectorate and annexed it in 1910. It created the puppet state of Manchukuo in 1932. Japan ruled others while claiming to be Asia’s liberator from the West (Episode 61).

The United States expanded across the continent (Episode 46) and then overseas. In 1893 American businessmen, backed by US Marines, overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani of Hawaii. The US annexed Hawaii in 1898. In the same year it defeated Spain and took Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, and made Cuba a protectorate. Filipino revolutionaries had already declared independence. The Philippine-American War (1899–1902, with fighting continuing in the south for years) killed about 4,200 US soldiers and an estimated 20,000 Filipino combatants. Estimates of Filipino civilian deaths from violence, famine, and disease range from about 200,000 to much higher (Episode 60). The Insular Cases (from 1901) held that the Constitution did not fully apply in the new territories.

## Acts

### 1. Hokkaido and Ryukyu

Ainu lands and the Development Commission (*Kaitakushi*). Assimilation law and Ainu resistance and survival; Japan recognized the Ainu as an Indigenous people in 2019. The Ryukyu Kingdom, its tributary relations with China and Satsuma, and annexation in 1879.

### 2. Taiwan, Korea, Manchuria

Taiwan after 1895, including resistance and the Wushe (Musha) uprising of 1930. Korea’s protectorate and annexation. Manchukuo. A preview of Episode 61’s detailed treatment.

### 3. Hawaii

The Kingdom of Hawaii as a recognized sovereign state. The 1893 overthrow and Liliʻuokalani’s protest. The 1897 anti-annexation petitions signed by tens of thousands of Native Hawaiians. The US Apology Resolution (1993).

### 4. 1898 and the Philippine-American War

The war with Spain, the Treaty of Paris, and the war against the Philippine Republic. Death tolls shown as estimates. Documented atrocities (the Balangiga retaliation on Samar, 1901; the “water cure” investigated by the Senate’s Lodge Committee, 1902) are named without methods.

### 5. Peers, not students

Japanese and American imperial ideologies — pan-Asianism and “benevolent assimilation” — beside European ones. The Anti-Imperialist League in the United States. The act closes on the point that non-European and anti-colonial origins did not prevent these states from becoming colonial powers.

## Comparative note

The United States was a postcolonial state and a colonial power at the same time (Episode 46). Japan was a non-Western state that escaped formal colonization and colonized its neighbors. Both complicate a simple “West versus rest” story.

## How we know

Japanese Government-General records for Taiwan and Korea. Ainu and Ryukyuan sources. US congressional records, including the Lodge Committee hearings. The Hawaiian anti-annexation petitions (US National Archives). Scholarship by Alexis Dudden, Paul Kramer (*The Blood of Government*, 2006), Noenoe Silva (*Aloha Betrayed*, 2004), and Daniel Immerwahr (*How to Hide an Empire*, 2019).

## Do not claim

- That Japan and the United States only imitated Europe.
- That US expansion was not colonial because the US was once a colony.
- That Filipino war dead are known precisely.
- That Hawaii was annexed with Native Hawaiian consent.
- That Japanese rule was liberation for other Asians.
