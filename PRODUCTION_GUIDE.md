# Production guide

Handbook for *Colonies and Colonial Power* (*The Colonial Age*). This guide belongs to this series only. *Discovery of India*, *Cradles of Civilization*, *600–500 BCE*, and *The Last Five Hundred Years* have their own guides. Nothing here overrides them, and nothing there is assumed here.

Series bible: [`README.md`](README.md). Packaging: [`SEASONS_AND_PATHS.md`](SEASONS_AND_PATHS.md). Evidence: [`SOURCE_CATALOG.md`](SOURCE_CATALOG.md). Comparison: [`COMPARATIVE_MATRIX.md`](COMPARATIVE_MATRIX.md). Clearance: [`RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md`](RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md).

## Governing principle

The series films a system and the people inside it, not voyages and heroes. Most of its evidence was written by the people who ruled. The camera therefore always shows who made a record, for whom, and when, before it shows what the record says. The viewer should always be able to tell whether they are looking at a document of the period, a colonial photograph, a later painting, a living community’s own account, an estimate, a model, or a reconstruction.

## On-screen claim labels

Episode 6 introduces the labels. Every factual caption that could be misread carries one in small type.

| Label | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **EV** — evidenced | Supported by documents or material evidence of the period | The 1661 Barbados slave code; von Trotha’s order of 2 October 1904 |
| **CL** — claim | Statement by an interested party, usually the colonizer | Cortés’s letters; Spanish accounts of Cajamarca; a district officer’s report of “unrest” |
| **INF** — inferred | Historians’ reasoned conclusion from indirect evidence | The role of the reparto in the Túpac Amaru rising |
| **MEM** — later memory | Oral tradition, commemoration, or national narrative | Nanny of the Maroons’ exploits; Bois Caïman details |
| **EST** — estimate | Number built from partial records and stated assumptions | 12.5 million embarked (Slave Voyages) |
| **MOD** — modeled | Number produced by demographic projection | Pre-contact population of the Americas |
| **CON** — contested | Figures or interpretations with serious scholarly disagreement | Congo Free State deaths; Algerian war dead; Kenyan detention totals |

- A number is never shown without a label and a source.
- Where two figures compete (Amritsar 1919; Sétif 1945; Kenya’s detainees), both appear together every time.
- Where no defensible number exists, the caption says “unknown.”

## Violence

The series is about systems that killed at scale. It shows that honestly and does not make it a spectacle.

- **Establish the analytical need.** Every depiction of violence must answer: what does the viewer need to understand about the system? If a document, an empty site, or a sentence of narration does the work, use it.
- **No reenactment** of massacre, torture, mutilation, flogging, execution, drowning, or death by famine or disease. Episode 34 does not stage the Middle Passage; Episode 29 does not recreate mutilation; Episode 69 does not film torture.
- **No lingering on bodies.** Colonial photographs of the dead, the executed, and the mutilated are used only when their existence is itself the evidence, briefly, with provenance, and after consultation (see `RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md`).
- **Methods are not described.** Torture in Algeria and Kenya, the enfumades, and chemical weapons in Ethiopia are named as documented practice. How they were done is not explained.
- **Battles are logistics, not ballet.** Conquest is shown through maps, supply lines, records, and landscapes (Episode 53), not choreographed combat.
- **Named perpetrators** at the scale evidence allows: von Trotha, Coen, Bugeaud, Eyre, Dyer, Graziani, Leopold II, a company, a government, a unit, or “unknown.” Present-day nations are not addressed as if their living people were a colonial office.

## Sexual violence

- Sexual violence under slavery (Episode 36), in colonial conquest, and in the Japanese military’s system of sexual slavery (Episode 63) is stated as a legal, demographic, and state fact.
- **No reconstruction.** No actors, no suggestive staging, no voiceover imagining the act.
- Survivor testimony is used only from public record and with the consent of survivors or their organizations.
- Owners’ records that document their own crimes (Thistlewood’s diaries) are shown as documents, never read aloud at length.
- Relationships between owners and enslaved people are never framed as romance.

## Music and sound

- No music that sells horror: no swelling strings over a slave ship, no drums over a massacre site.
- No triumphant anthems over conquest, “discovery,” or independence ceremonies. Independence flags rise to room tone or archival sound.
- Music from colonized and descendant communities is licensed from its makers and used in its own context, not as exotic texture.

## No nostalgia lighting

- No sepia washes, golden-hour governor’s lawns, pith-helmet romance, or steam-train glamour.
- Colonial architecture is filmed in the same neutral light as everything else, with its labor history captioned: “Built with repartimiento labor,” “Built with sugar wealth.”
- Colonial-era footage (British Pathé, Istituto Luce, Colonial Film Unit, INA) is always labeled with its producer and purpose. The series may analyze nostalgic imagery (Episode 24) but never adopt its grammar.

## No “discovery” framing

- The word “discovery” appears only in quotation marks and only as a historical term.
- Inhabited lands were reached, entered, invaded, claimed, or settled.
- Explorers are shown with their guides, porters, and interpreters (Episode 51).
- No caravel-at-dawn shots under a heroic score. Ships are filmed as technology and as claim (Episode 3).

## Maps

Episode 3 introduces the rule that a claim is not control. Every map in the series is versioned and carries a source note.

- **Pre-colonial maps** show polities as centers and zones, with shaded uncertain edges. No modern national borders.
- **Colonial claims** are drawn as dashed lines labeled “claimed.” Areas of effective control are shaded separately. The gap between the two is the point.
- **Colonial borders**, when they are the subject (Episodes 52, 68, 71), are drawn as drawn: straight lines appear with the treaty that made them and its date.
- **Contested territories** (Palestine 1947–49 in Episode 68; Western Sahara in Episode 72) carry a note on sources and on what each line means (a UN plan, an armistice line, a claim). No map pretends neutrality by omitting its sources.
- **Movement arrows** (slave voyages, silver flows, indenture) are drawn to scale from data, with the database cited.
- A standing legend: “Maps show claims and evidence, not destiny.”

## Colonial archives

- The archive is shown as an institution: reading rooms, bundles, catalog numbers. Archives in formerly colonized countries (Mexico, Ghana, Senegal, Indonesia, Kenya, the Philippines, Namibia) are filmed with the same care as Seville, Kew, The Hague, and Aix.
- The India Office Records at the British Library are always described as the archive of an imperial company and state system. They are not the basis for an India series.
- Every colonial document on screen carries its author’s position: “Letter from a governor,” “Report by a district officer,” “Missionary account.”
- Gaps and destruction (the migrated archives, burned records) are shown as gaps, with the reason where known.
- Colonial photographs are captioned with photographer, date, commissioning body, and purpose where known.

## Living communities

- Indigenous nations, descendant communities of the enslaved, maroon peoples, Chagossians, and people under recent occupation are collaborators and sources, not exhibits.
- Filming on community land requires permission from the community’s own governing body (a First Nation, a Maroon council, a Saramaka *gaanman*, an Aboriginal land council).
- Cultural protocols are followed: for example, warnings for images of deceased Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, and restrictions on filming sacred spaces in terreiros and Vodou temples.
- Communities review how they are represented before picture lock where feasible.
- Interviewees are paid for their time and expertise on the same terms as academic contributors.

## Reconstructions

- Reconstructions are rare and always labeled **Reconstruction** on screen.
- Permitted: replica ships under sail (labeled), animated maps, 3D models of forts and ships from plans, and object handling.
- Not permitted: actors portraying enslaved people in bondage, conquest battles, massacres, or sexual violence. Nor are “face” reconstructions of historical individuals from remains without community consent.
- Later art — 19th-century “discovery” paintings, casta paintings, Eckhout’s Brazilian portraits, Victorian engravings of Livingstone — is labeled as later or commissioned imagery every time.
