## Before

Episode 25 ended with the VOC’s bankruptcy in 1799. After British occupation of Java (1811–16), the Dutch state took direct control. The Java War (1825–30), led by Prince Diponegoro, cost the Dutch heavily and the Javanese far more, with estimates of 200,000 Javanese deaths. The Netherlands, also losing Belgium after 1830, needed money.

## The turn

Governor-General Johannes van den Bosch introduced the *cultuurstelsel* (Cultivation System) in 1830. Villages were to set aside a portion of their land — in principle a fifth — or labor time to grow export crops for the government, which bought them at fixed low prices. In practice the demands often exceeded the rules. Javanese regents (*bupati*) and village heads, paid on commission, enforced it. The profits, the *batig slot* (colonial surplus), paid a large share of the Dutch state’s budget in the 1830s–60s and helped fund railways and debt reduction in the Netherlands.

Where export crops displaced rice and labor was diverted, famine followed. The famines in Cirebon (1843–44) and in Demak and Grobogan (1849–50) are documented, with death figures that are estimates. In 1860 Eduard Douwes Dekker, a former colonial official, published *Max Havelaar* under the pen name Multatuli. It denounced abuses in Lebak, Banten, and became famous. It was a Dutch official’s critique of colonial corruption, not a call to end colonial rule, and not the voice of Javanese farmers. The system was dismantled from the 1870s. The Agrarian Law of 1870 opened Java to private plantations. The “Ethical Policy” of 1901 promised welfare and education. Indonesian independence is later (Episode 64).

## Acts

### 1. The Java War

Diponegoro’s war as the context of Dutch financial crisis. Diponegoro as a Javanese prince with his own religious and political aims. His capture by deception in 1830 and his exile.

### 2. The system

Van den Bosch’s design, the fixed prices, and the role of regents and village heads. Javanese elites as enforcers and beneficiaries. The gap between rules and practice.

### 3. Profit and famine

The *batig slot* in Dutch budgets. Famine in Cirebon and Demak–Grobogan with estimated tolls and debated causes: crop demands, drought, and administration. No images of starving people; archival reports and landscapes.

### 4. Max Havelaar

The novel, its author, and its reception in the Netherlands. Pramoedya Ananta Toer later called it “the book that killed colonialism,” a strong claim to be labeled. The act distinguishes a Dutch critique from Javanese voices, and seeks the latter in petitions, *babad* chronicles, and village records.

### 5. After the system

The 1870 Agrarian Law and private plantations in Java and Sumatra (Deli tobacco worked by indentured Chinese and Javanese laborers under penal sanction). The Ethical Policy. The act hands Indonesian nationalism forward to Episodes 59 and 64.

## Comparative note

British India’s land revenue (Episode 21) taxed land in money. The Cultivation System demanded crops and labor through village structures. Both depended on local elites. The comparison is about institutions, not about which was worse.

## How we know

Colonial reports and the *Koloniaal Verslag*. Dutch parliamentary debates. Javanese chronicles, including Diponegoro’s own *babad*, written in exile (UNESCO Memory of the World). Scholarship by Robert Elson (*Village Java under the Cultivation System*, 1994), Peter Carey (*The Power of Prophecy*, 2007), and Cees Fasseur.

## Do not claim

- That *Max Havelaar* speaks for Javanese farmers, or that it called for independence.
- That famine deaths under the system are precisely known.
- That the Cultivation System was purely Dutch; Javanese elites enforced and profited from it.
- That the Ethical Policy ended colonial exploitation.
- That the system operated the same way in every residency.
