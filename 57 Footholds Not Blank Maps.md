## Before

Season 7 closed on Africa. Season 8 turns to Asia. Episode 2 showed an Indian Ocean full of merchants before 1498. Episode 14 introduced the Portuguese Estado da Índia in one act. This hour returns to it and to the other European footholds.

## The turn

Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498. The Zamorin, Calicut’s ruler, found his goods unimpressive. The Portuguese used naval violence to force their way in. Afonso de Albuquerque took Goa (1510), Malacca (1511), and Hormuz (1515). The Portuguese tried to control Indian Ocean trade through a system of passes (*cartazes*) for Asian ships. They never controlled most of it. Gujarati, Arab, Malay, and Chinese merchants continued to trade, often around them. Macao was settled around 1557 with the permission of Ming officials, and it paid ground rent. The Portuguese reached Japan in 1543. Nagasaki became the port of the Portuguese trade from 1571, as a Jesuit-influenced city, until the Tokugawa shogunate expelled the Portuguese in 1639.

The Dutch took Malacca (1641) and much of Portuguese Ceylon. In Japan the VOC was confined to the small artificial island of Dejima at Nagasaki from 1641 to 1854, as the only Europeans allowed to trade, under strict Japanese control. The English and French companies held factories on the Indian coast (Episode 17). Before the mid-18th century, European power in Asia was mostly coastal, dependent on Asian rulers’ permission, and limited by powerful states: the Mughals, the Safavids, the Ming and Qing, Tokugawa Japan, and Mataram in Java.

## Acts

### 1. Calicut, 1498

Da Gama’s arrival and the Zamorin’s court. The Portuguese bombardment of Calicut (1500–02), and their violence against Muslim pilgrims’ ships, documented by Portuguese sources themselves. Violence as a strategy, not a sign of dominance.

### 2. Goa and Malacca

Albuquerque’s conquests. Goa as the capital of the Estado da Índia. Malacca’s sultanate and its successors in Johor and Aceh. The *cartaz* system and its limits. South Asian history remains *Discovery of India*’s; Goa is filmed only as a Portuguese foothold.

### 3. Macao

Settlement under Ming permission, ground rent, and Macao as a hub between Japan, China, and Manila. Portuguese dependence on Chinese authority.

### 4. Nagasaki and Dejima

The Portuguese and Jesuits in Japan, Christian converts, and the Tokugawa ban and persecution (from 1614). The Shimabara rebellion (1637–38). The Dutch on Dejima: trade on Japanese terms, and annual reports to the shogunate (*fusetsugaki*).

### 5. Not blank maps

A map of Asia around 1700 showing Asian states and European footholds at scale. European positions appear as dots on coasts beside large empires. The act prepares the 19th-century shift, when European and Japanese power became territorial.

## Comparative note

In West Africa (Episode 9), European forts before the 19th century also depended on African rulers’ permission. The switch from footholds to territorial empire came late in both Asia and Africa, and for connected reasons: industrial power, steam, and imperial rivalry.

## How we know

Portuguese chronicles and Estado da Índia records (Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino; Goa archives). VOC Dejima records (Nationaal Archief). Japanese records of the Dutch trade. Chinese records on Macao. Scholarship by Sanjay Subrahmanyam (*The Portuguese Empire in Asia*, 1993), Charles Boxer, Adam Clulow (*The Company and the Shogun*, 2014), and Anthony Reid.

## Do not claim

- That Europeans dominated Asian trade from 1498.
- That Asia was a blank map for European claims.
- That Macao was a colony by conquest; it was settled under Ming permission.
- That Dejima was a Dutch colony; it was a Japanese-controlled trading post.
- That Goa’s place in South Asian history is covered here; it is filmed only as a foothold.
