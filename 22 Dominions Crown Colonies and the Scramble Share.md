## Before

Episode 21 filmed the Raj as a system and stopped. The empire had other systems. Settlers in Canada rebelled in 1837–38. London concluded that white settler colonies should govern themselves.

## The turn

The Durham Report (1839) recommended “responsible government” for Canada, and settler colonies gained it through the 1840s–50s. Canada became a confederation in 1867, Australia a federation in 1901, New Zealand a dominion in 1907, and the Union of South Africa was formed in 1910. The Statute of Westminster (1931) confirmed their legislative independence. Self-government was for settlers. The dominions passed laws against Indigenous peoples and Asian immigrants: Canada’s Indian Act (1876) and Chinese head tax (1885); Australia’s Immigration Restriction Act (1901), the basis of the White Australia policy; Natal’s literacy test (1897), copied elsewhere; and South Africa’s Natives Land Act (1913).

Crown colonies — Jamaica after 1865, Hong Kong, the Straits Settlements, Gold Coast, Ceylon — were ruled by governors with appointed councils, sometimes with limited elected members. Protectorates were ruled indirectly. In Africa, Britain took the largest share in the partition of 1880–1914, from Egypt (occupied 1882) and Sudan to Nigeria, Kenya, Uganda, and the Rhodesias. That scramble is Season 7’s subject; here the hour places Britain in it.

## After

Episode 23 follows the wars that strained the imperial center, from South Africa to two world wars.

## Acts

### 1. Responsible government

The 1837–38 rebellions, the Durham Report, and self-government for settlers. The act states plainly that Indigenous peoples were not the “responsible” public in question.

### 2. White dominions and race laws

The Indian Act, the Chinese head tax, the Immigration Restriction Act and its dictation test, Natal’s 1897 act, and the 1913 Natives Land Act. Laws are shown as documents with dates. Indigenous and Asian communities’ protests and petitions are included.

### 3. Crown colonies

Jamaica’s loss of its assembly after the Morant Bay rebellion (1865; Episode 65), Hong Kong’s governor, and the Gold Coast Legislative Council. Rule by governors, with local elites as partners and critics.

### 4. Britain’s scramble share

A map of British Africa by 1914, drawn as claimed and then as ruled, with the gaps between. The Royal Niger Company and British South Africa Company handed forward to Season 7. Egypt as a “veiled protectorate” after 1882.

### 5. One empire, many rules

The Imperial Conferences and the language of a “British Commonwealth” (1920s). The act closes on the contradiction: an empire that called itself a family of free nations and governed most of its subjects without their consent.

## Comparative note

France claimed to assimilate some colonial subjects into citizenship (Episode 27), while Britain kept dominions and colonies distinct. Neither was a morality ranking. They were different legal architectures, and each excluded most colonized people from full rights.

## How we know

Colonial Office and Dominions Office records (The National Archives, Kew). Dominion legislation and parliamentary debates. The Durham Report. Indigenous and Asian community petitions. Scholarship by John Darwin (*The Empire Project*, 2009), Marilyn Lake and Henry Reynolds (*Drawing the Global Colour Line*, 2008).

## Do not claim

- That self-government in the dominions meant self-government for Indigenous peoples.
- That the dominions were decolonized in 1931; they were settler states.
- That the British empire had one constitution or one system of rule.
- That the African partition is narrated in full here; it is Season 7.
- That the Commonwealth was an equal partnership in this period.
