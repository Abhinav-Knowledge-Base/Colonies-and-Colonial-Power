## Before

Episode 28 showed a settler colony claimed as national territory. The Congo Free State was something else: a colony owned by one man, recognized by other powers, and run for revenue.

## The turn

Leopold II founded the International African Association (1876) as a humanitarian and scientific enterprise. He hired Henry Morton Stanley to sign treaties with Congolese rulers, many under unequal or misunderstood terms. At the Berlin Conference (1884–85; Episode 52) the powers recognized his claim. The Congo Free State was his personal possession, not Belgium’s. After the 1890s rubber boom, the state and concession companies (such as ABIR and the Anversoise) imposed rubber quotas on villages. The Force Publique, an army of African soldiers under European officers, enforced them. It took hostages, burned villages, and killed. Soldiers were required to account for ammunition. The documentary record includes reports that severed hands were brought to officers as proof, and photographs of mutilated people taken by missionaries, notably Alice Seeley Harris.

The regime was exposed by African American historian George Washington Williams (open letter, 1890), missionaries such as William Sheppard, the shipping clerk E. D. Morel, and British consul Roger Casement, whose report was published in 1904. Morel’s Congo Reform Association campaigned internationally. Belgium annexed the territory in 1908. Annexation ended Leopold’s personal rule but did not make colonial rule just. Forced labor continued in the Belgian Congo in other forms.

How many people died is disputed. There was no census before 1924. Estimates rest on backward projection, missionary reports, and assumptions about births, famine, and sleeping sickness, which also spread in this period. Adam Hochschild’s popular figure of about 10 million deaths (1998) draws on earlier estimates of population halving. Some historians consider that too high or unknowable. The series shows the range and its methods (Episode 7). It does not lock one number as fact and does not dismiss the catastrophe.

## Acts

### 1. A king’s colony

Leopold’s association, Stanley’s treaties, and recognition at Berlin. The Congo Free State as a personal sovereignty with an international humanitarian mask.

### 2. Rubber and the quota

Concession companies, quotas, hostage-taking, and the Force Publique. Congolese soldiers, sentries, and intermediaries were agents of the violence, many of them conscripted and themselves coerced. The violence was the state’s and the companies’ system.

### 3. The documentary record

Casement’s report, missionary testimony, and Alice Seeley Harris’s photographs. The record is described, and its existence and provenance are shown. Photographs of mutilated people are not used as spectacle. If shown at all, one image is shown briefly with its provenance and with consultation (see `RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md`). No recreation of mutilation.

### 4. How many died

The demographic debate: sources, methods, the role of sleeping sickness and famine, and the range of estimates. The on-screen label reads “estimates; disputed.”

### 5. 1908 and after

Annexation, the Belgian Congo’s forced labor and concessions (Huileries du Congo Belge, 1911), and the 2020 statement of King Philippe expressing “deepest regrets.” Leopold’s statues removed or defaced in Belgium in 2020. Handoff to Episode 70 for independence and the Congo crisis.

## Comparative note

German Southwest Africa (Episode 30) produced an explicit extermination order. The Congo Free State produced mass death through a revenue system of terror, forced labor, famine, and disease. The series compares institutions and intent where evidenced. It does not rank catastrophes.

## How we know

Casement’s 1904 report (Parliamentary Papers). The Belgian Commission of Inquiry (1904–05). Missionary archives (Baptist Missionary Society; Congo Balolo Mission). Alice Seeley Harris’s photographs (Anti-Slavery International). The AfricaMuseum, Tervuren. Scholarship by Jan Vansina, Jean-Luc Vellut, Adam Hochschild, and Robert Harms.

## Do not claim

- That exactly 10 million people died, or any single figure, as established fact.
- That the absence of a census means the catastrophe is unproven.
- That the Congo Free State was a Belgian colony before 1908.
- That annexation in 1908 made colonial rule in the Congo just.
- That Congolese agents bear the system’s responsibility; they acted within it, often under coercion.
