## Before

Episode 19 followed settlers taking Indigenous land. After Britain’s victory in the Seven Years’ War (1756–63), the empire in North America was larger and more expensive, and its settlers wanted more land.

## The turn

Britain’s Royal Proclamation of 1763 drew a line along the Appalachians, reserving land to the west for Indigenous nations and forbidding settlement. Settlers and speculators resented it. Pontiac’s War (1763–66) showed Indigenous nations resisting British rule directly. New taxes (the Stamp Act 1765, the Townshend duties) added grievance. The revolution of 1775–83 created the United States. Its Declaration of Independence complained that the king had incited “merciless Indian Savages” against the settlers. The new republic kept slavery; it was written into the Constitution through the three-fifths clause and the fugitive slave clause. It expanded west onto Indigenous land (Episode 46).

Many Indigenous nations sided with Britain, as did many enslaved people who fled to British lines after Lord Dunmore’s proclamation (1775) and the Philipsburg Proclamation (1779). Some Black Loyalists went to Nova Scotia and, in 1792, to Sierra Leone. Loyalists settled in what became Upper Canada. Britain lost thirteen colonies and kept a world empire. It held Canada and the Caribbean. The East India Company expanded in South Asia (Episode 21). The First Fleet sailed for Australia in 1787, partly because convicts could no longer be sent to America.

## After

Episode 21 turns to South Asia: the East India Company’s revenue, army, law, and land system, filmed once as an imperial system and then handed to *Discovery of India*.

## Acts

### 1. The Proclamation line

The 1763 Proclamation map and Pontiac’s War. Indigenous nations as powers Britain tried to manage. Settler anger at a line that protected Indigenous land.

### 2. A settler rebellion

Taxes, representation, and land. The revolution presented as a rebellion by settlers who wanted self-government and land, and who were also shaped by real political ideas. The Declaration read, including its line about Indigenous people.

### 3. Who was free

Slavery in all thirteen colonies in 1776. Gradual abolition in northern states after the war. The Constitution’s protections for slavery. Enslaved people who fought on both sides and those who fled to British lines.

### 4. Loyalists and allies

Black Loyalists to Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone. Mohawk leader Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea) and the Haudenosaunee who allied with Britain and were left out of the peace treaty of 1783. Indigenous nations were not parties to the treaty that transferred their land.

### 5. Empire kept

A world map in 1790: British Canada, the Caribbean, the East India Company’s territories, Sierra Leone, and the First Fleet in Sydney. The act says plainly that 1776 did not end colonial power. It relocated some of it into a new settler republic.

## Comparative note

Haiti’s revolution (Episode 38) was led by enslaved people and abolished slavery. The United States’ revolution was led by settlers and kept it. Episode 44 compares both with the Spanish American revolutions, without making 1776 the model everyone copied.

## How we know

The Proclamation of 1763, the Declaration, the Constitution, and the Treaty of Paris (1783). Loyalist claims and the *Book of Negroes* (1783), recording Black Loyalists evacuated from New York. Indigenous diplomatic records. Scholarship by Colin Calloway (*The Indian World of George Washington*, 2018), Alan Taylor, and Cassandra Pybus.

## Do not claim

- That 1776 ended colonialism in North America.
- That the American Revolution was an anti-colonial revolution in the same sense as 20th-century decolonization.
- That the founders all opposed slavery, or that the Constitution was neutral on it.
- That Indigenous nations were bystanders.
- That Britain’s empire shrank overall after 1783.
