## Before

Season 2 ended with Iberian empires under attack. England, a smaller and poorer kingdom in the 16th century, entered overseas trade through privateering and chartered merchant companies.

## The turn

English overseas trade used the chartered company form introduced in Episode 4. The Levant Company (1592, from the Turkey Company of 1581) traded in the Ottoman empire under capitulations granted by the sultan. It was a guest, not a ruler. The East India Company (1600) set up factories at Surat (1613, with Mughal permission), Madras (1639–40), Bombay (1668, from the Portuguese via a royal dowry), and Calcutta (1690). For more than a century it was one trading power among many in the Indian Ocean. The Royal African Company (1672) held a monopoly on English trade with West Africa, including the slave trade, from forts such as Cape Coast Castle. The Hudson’s Bay Company (1670) claimed a vast drainage basin in North America and traded furs with Cree and other nations who knew the country and set many of the terms.

A factory was a warehouse with walls, a garrison, and a treaty. The flag came later and unevenly. This hour films the company form as English power’s early shape. It does not tell a biography of Clive, whose later career belongs to the Company’s territorial turn (Episode 21) and to *Discovery of India*.

## After

## Acts

### 1. Guests of the sultan

The Levant Company in Aleppo and Istanbul under Ottoman capitulations. English merchants operated at the sultan’s pleasure. The Ottoman empire was not a colony (Episode 31). The act uses it to show that a company could be a supplicant.

### 2. Surat and the Mughal court

Thomas Roe’s embassy to Jahangir (1615–19) as an English petitioner at a powerful court. The Surat factory’s daily business. One act only, as a comparison of trading-post forms; South Asian history is *Discovery of India*.

### 3. Cape Coast and the Royal African Company

The company’s forts and its role in the slave trade. The company branded enslaved people with its initials, “DY” for the Duke of York, the company’s governor and later James II. This is evidenced and stated once, without imagery. The monopoly was opened to private traders in 1698, and the volume of the trade rose. Handed forward to Season 5.

### 4. Hudson’s Bay

York Factory and the fur trade. Cree and Assiniboine traders, and Indigenous and Métis women whose marriages with traders made the trade work (“country marriages”). The company’s charter claimed Rupert’s Land for 200 years; Indigenous nations did not recognize the claim. Handed forward to Episode 47.

### 5. From factory to flag

How some factories became territories and others did not. The act compares the four companies’ fates and introduces the question for the season: when did trade become rule, and who paid for it?

## Comparative note

The Dutch VOC (Episode 25) moved faster than the English company toward territorial rule, in Java and the Spice Islands in the 17th century. The English company’s territorial turn came mainly after the 1750s. Timing depended on local politics and opportunity as much as company policy.

## How we know

Company records: East India Company papers within the India Office Records (British Library); Royal African Company papers (T 70, The National Archives, Kew); Hudson’s Bay Company Archives (Archives of Manitoba, Winnipeg; UNESCO Memory of the World). Levant Company papers (SP 105). Scholarship by K. N. Chaudhuri, William Pettigrew on the Royal African Company, and Philip Stern (*The Company-State*, 2011).

## Do not claim

- That the East India Company ruled territory from 1600.
- That the English were welcomed or dominant at Mughal or Ottoman courts.
- That the Hudson’s Bay Company controlled Rupert’s Land in practice.
- That the Royal African Company was only a trading company; it was a slaving company.
- That company history is the biography of a few men.
