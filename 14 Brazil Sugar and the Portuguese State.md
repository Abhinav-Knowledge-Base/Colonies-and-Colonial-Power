## Before

Episode 13 closed the Pacific loop of Spanish silver. Back in the Atlantic, Pedro Álvares Cabral’s fleet had touched the Brazilian coast in 1500 on its way to India. The Tordesillas line placed it on Portugal’s side.

## The turn

Early Portuguese Brazil traded brazilwood with Tupi-speaking peoples. From 1534 the crown granted hereditary captaincies to donees who were meant to settle and defend the coast. Most failed. In 1549 the crown sent a governor-general, Tomé de Sousa, and founded Salvador da Bahia. Jesuits arrived the same year. Sugar spread through Pernambuco and Bahia. Mills first used enslaved and coerced Indigenous labor. Epidemics, war, and Jesuit and crown restrictions (partly observed, often evaded) shifted the labor force toward enslaved Africans from the late 16th century. Brazil became the largest single destination of the Atlantic slave trade, receiving an estimated four to five million or more people over its history (Slave Voyages estimates).

Enslaved people resisted from the start. Palmares, in the hills of Alagoas, grew from about 1605 into a federation of settlements with thousands of inhabitants. It survived Dutch and Portuguese expeditions until 1694; its leader Zumbi was killed in 1695. The Dutch West India Company held northeastern Brazil from 1630 to 1654 (Episode 16).

The same crown held the Estado da Índia: Goa (taken 1510), Malacca (1511), Hormuz, Macao (settled c. 1557 with Ming permission), and forts along the Indian Ocean. These were footholds and a system of passes (*cartazes*), not a conquest of India or China. Their history within South Asia belongs to *Discovery of India*. Episode 57 returns to the Asian footholds.

## Acts

### 1. Brazilwood and the coast

Tupi and other Indigenous peoples as trading partners, allies, and enemies. Early accounts (Pero Vaz de Caminha’s letter, 1500; Hans Staden’s captivity narrative, 1557) are labeled as claims shaped by their authors’ purposes.

### 2. Captaincies and a governor

The donatary system, its failures, and royal government from 1549. Brazil was run by the crown, not by a company, until the Dutch intrusion.

### 3. Sugar and enslavement

*Engenhos* in Pernambuco and Bahia. The shift from Indigenous to African enslaved labor as a process with several causes, not a clean switch. Indigenous enslavement continued in the interior for centuries through slaving expeditions (*bandeiras*).

### 4. Palmares

Its structure, its economy, and its politics. The 1678 treaty offered to Ganga Zumba and rejected by Zumbi. Destruction in 1694. Palmares as fact and as later memory: 20 November, Black Consciousness Day in Brazil, marks Zumbi’s death. Agency without romance.

### 5. The other half of the crown

Goa, Malacca, and Macao in one act: the *cartaz* system, the Goa Inquisition (1560), and the limits of Portuguese power against Asian states. Then the handoff: the history of the subcontinent is *Discovery of India*, a separate project.

## Comparative note

Spanish America relied heavily on Indigenous labor drafts (Episodes 12, 42). Portuguese Brazil turned more heavily to African slavery. The difference came from the size of settled Indigenous populations, disease, and the structure of the sugar economy. It was not a difference in moral character.

## How we know

Portuguese crown records (Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino, Lisbon; Arquivo Nacional, Rio de Janeiro). Jesuit letters. Dutch WIC records for 1630–54. Archaeology at Serra da Barriga (Palmares). Slave Voyages estimates for Brazil. Scholarship by Stuart Schwartz (*Sugar Plantations in the Formation of Brazilian Society*, 1985) and on Palmares by Flávio Gomes.

## Do not claim

- That Brazil’s colonization followed the Spanish model.
- That Indigenous enslavement ended when African slavery grew.
- That Palmares was a utopia; it had its own hierarchy and its own captives.
- That Portugal conquered India or China; the Estado da Índia held footholds.
- That Brazil’s share of the slave trade is a precise number; it is an estimate.
