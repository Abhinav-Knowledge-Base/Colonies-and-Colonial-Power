## Before

Episode 62 stopped at the edge of South Asia. Japan had invaded China in 1937. In December 1941 it attacked Pearl Harbor, Hong Kong, Malaya, and the Philippines at once.

## The turn

Within months Japan took Hong Kong, Malaya, Singapore (February 1942), the Dutch East Indies, Burma, and the Philippines. French Indochina remained under Vichy French administration with Japanese forces present until Japan overthrew the French in March 1945. The speed of European collapse destroyed the myth of European invincibility. Japan proclaimed a Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere. In practice it extracted resources, imposed forced labor — the *rōmusha* in Java, and the Burma-Thailand railway, built by Asian laborers and Allied prisoners at enormous cost of life — and ruled violently. Nationalists took different paths. In Indonesia, Sukarno cooperated with Japan while preparing independence. In Burma, Aung San allied with Japan and then turned against it in 1945. In Vietnam, Hồ Chí Minh’s Viet Minh (founded 1941) fought both Japan and France; its war was longer than this war. In the Philippines, the Hukbalahap and other guerrillas resisted.

Two famines killed on a vast scale. In northern Vietnam in 1944–45, famine killed an estimated 1 to 2 million people (Vietnamese sources give 2 million). Causes included Japanese and French requisitioning of rice, forced cultivation of industrial crops, disrupted transport, and natural disasters. In Bengal in 1943, famine killed an estimated 2.1 to 3 million people. Its causes are debated: the fall of Burma and the loss of rice imports, war inflation, the British “denial policy” that removed boats and rice from coastal areas, a cyclone, crop disease, and government decisions on relief and exports. Amartya Sen emphasized failures of entitlement rather than food supply. Others emphasize imperial policy and wartime priorities, and some emphasize crop failure. The series presents the debate. It names the famine as occurring under imperial rule, with policy under debate, and hands its place in South Asian history to *Discovery of India*.

The Japanese military established a system of “comfort stations” in which women and girls — many from Korea, and also from China, the Philippines, Indonesia, Taiwan, and elsewhere, including Dutch women in the Indies — were held in sexual slavery. The number is unknown; estimates range from tens of thousands to much higher. It was a state system documented in military records and survivor testimony, not a rumor. It is named here without graphic detail.

## After

Episode 64 follows Asia’s many clocks of independence.

## Acts

### 1. The collapse of 1942

Singapore’s fall and the end of European invincibility. Surrender footage used with context, not as spectacle.

### 2. Occupation

The Co-Prosperity Sphere as propaganda and practice. *Rōmusha*, the Burma-Thailand railway, and the Sook Ching massacres of Chinese in Singapore (1942), with estimated tolls. Asian laborers as the majority of the railway’s dead.

### 3. Collaborate, resist, or both

Sukarno, Aung San, Hồ Chí Minh, and Filipino guerrillas. Each case shown on its own terms. “Collaboration” examined as a word that simplifies hard choices.

### 4. Famine

Vietnam 1944–45 and Bengal 1943, each with estimated tolls and debated causes. No images of starving people. The camera stays on documents, rice records, and places. Bengal is handed onward to *Discovery of India*.

### 5. A state system of sexual slavery

The comfort-station system as documented through Japanese military records (such as those found by historian Yoshimi Yoshiaki in 1992) and survivor testimony, beginning with Kim Hak-sun’s public testimony in 1991. The Kōno Statement (1993). One careful act. No reenactment. Survivors’ words are used only from public testimony and with their organizations’ consent.

## Comparative note

In Europe, German occupation also broke colonial powers at home: France and the Netherlands were occupied. The war weakened every European empire at once, in Asia and Africa (Episode 67).

## How we know

Japanese military records. Allied intelligence and war crimes trial records (Tokyo and regional tribunals). Colonial government records on famine. Survivor testimony. Scholarship by Christopher Bayly and Tim Harper (*Forgotten Armies*, 2004), Yoshimi Yoshiaki (*Comfort Women*, 1995; English 2000), Amartya Sen (*Poverty and Famines*, 1981), Janam Mukherjee (*Hungry Bengal*, 2015), and Geoffrey Gunn on the Vietnam famine.

## Do not claim

- That Japan liberated Asia from colonialism.
- That the Bengal famine’s causes are settled.
- That famine tolls in Vietnam or Bengal are known precisely.
- That the comfort-station system was voluntary prostitution or an unproven rumor.
- That nationalists who cooperated with Japan were simply traitors, or those who resisted simply heroes.
