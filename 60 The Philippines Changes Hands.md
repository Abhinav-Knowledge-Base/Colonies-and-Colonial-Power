## Before

Episode 13 showed Manila as the hub of the galleon trade. Episode 59 showed Southeast Asia divided. The Philippines had its own path: a Spanish colony since the 16th century, with Muslim sultanates in the south that Spain never fully conquered.

## The turn

Spanish rule in the Philippines depended heavily on the religious orders. Friars were parish priests, landlords (the friar estates, *haciendas*), and local officials, and they were often the only Spaniards in a town. Filipino elites (*ilustrados*) educated in Manila and Europe demanded reform in the Propaganda Movement of the 1880s. After the execution of three Filipino priests (Gomburza) in 1872, anti-friar feeling grew. José Rizal’s novels *Noli Me Tángere* (1887) and *El Filibusterismo* (1891) attacked friar power. Rizal was a reformist, not a revolutionary. He was executed by Spain in December 1896, after the Katipunan, a secret society founded by Andrés Bonifacio in 1892, began an armed revolution that August.

Emilio Aguinaldo, who took control of the revolution (and whose faction executed Bonifacio in 1897), declared independence on 12 June 1898, during the Spanish-American War. The Treaty of Paris (December 1898) transferred the Philippines from Spain to the United States for $20 million. The Philippine Republic (Malolos Republic, 1899) resisted. The Philippine-American War (1899–1902, with resistance continuing in places until 1913) killed about 4,200 US soldiers and an estimated 20,000 Filipino combatants. Estimates of civilian deaths from violence, disease, and famine range from about 200,000 to much higher (Episode 32). Under US rule, Filipinos organized parties, won an elected assembly (1907), and pressed for independence. The Tydings-McDuffie Act (1934) set a ten-year transition. Independence came on 4 July 1946, after Japanese occupation (Episode 63).

## Acts

### 1. Friar rule

Parishes, estates, and the friars’ power. The Muslim south (the Sulu and Maguindanao sultanates) and the Cordillera highlands, never fully under Spanish control.

### 2. Reform and revolution

Gomburza (1872), the Propaganda Movement, and Rizal’s novels and execution. Bonifacio and the Katipunan. The revolution of 1896 and its internal conflicts.

### 3. 1898

Aguinaldo’s declaration, Dewey at Manila Bay, and the mock battle of Manila (August 1898) arranged between Spanish and US forces to exclude Filipinos. The Treaty of Paris. Filipinos excluded from the decisions over their country.

### 4. War

The Philippine-American War with estimated tolls. Documented abuses, including the Samar campaign of 1901–02 after Balangiga and the water cure investigated by the Senate, named without methods. The Moro wars in the south, including the Bud Dajo massacre (1906). Filipino guerrilla strategies.

### 5. Nationalism under US rule

The Philippine Assembly (1907), Manuel Quezon and Sergio Osmeña, the Commonwealth (1935), and independence in 1946. The Bell Trade Act’s economic conditions. The act closes on a nation that changed colonial hands twice and kept its own nationalist movement throughout.

## Comparative note

Cuba (1898) and Puerto Rico also changed hands from Spain to US control. Cuba became nominally independent in 1902 under the Platt Amendment, and Puerto Rico remains a US territory (Episode 72).

## How we know

Spanish colonial records (Archivo General de Indias; National Archives of the Philippines). The Philippine Revolutionary Records captured by the US Army. US War Department reports and Senate hearings (1902). Rizal’s writings. Scholarship by Reynaldo Ileto (*Pasyon and Revolution*, 1979), Paul Kramer, Glenn May, and Vicente Rafael.

## Do not claim

- That Filipinos were passive under Spanish or US rule.
- That Rizal led the armed revolution; he was a reformist.
- That war death tolls are known precisely.
- That the United States liberated the Philippines in 1898; it bought and conquered it.
- That the Muslim south was fully under Spanish rule.
