## Before

Episode 53 followed conquest. In some colonies, conquest was followed by settlement. Algeria, told in Episode 28, is the largest North African case. This hour covers settler colonies south of the Sahara.

## The turn

The Cape Colony grew from the VOC station of 1652 (Episode 25) onto Khoekhoe and San land and then Xhosa land in a century of frontier wars. After Britain took the Cape (1795/1806), Boer trekkers moved inland from the 1830s and founded republics. The discovery of diamonds (1867) and gold (1886) drew capital and migrant labor, and mining compounds controlled African workers. Pass laws — requiring Africans to carry documents to move or work — had origins in the Cape’s 18th- and 19th-century regulations and expanded with mining. The Union of South Africa (1910) passed the Natives Land Act (1913), restricting African land ownership to reserves of about 7 percent of the country, later expanded to about 13 percent. Sol Plaatje wrote: “Awaking on Friday morning, June 20, 1913, the South African native found himself, not actually a slave, but a pariah in the land of his birth.” Apartheid, formalized after the National Party’s victory in 1948, intensified segregation. It lasted until the 1990s, long after the independence of most of Africa.

In Southern Rhodesia, the British South Africa Company took Ndebele and Shona land after wars in 1893 and 1896–97. The Land Apportionment Act (1930) divided land by race. Kenya’s “White Highlands” were reserved for European settlers, and Africans became squatters or laborers on land that had been theirs. Portugal’s Angola and Mozambique had forced labor (*chibalo*), a legal distinction between *indígenas* and *assimilados*, and after 1945 state-sponsored settler schemes (*colonatos*).

## After

Episode 55 follows resistance before the flags came down.

## Acts

### 1. The Cape frontier

Khoekhoe, San, and Xhosa resistance. The frontier wars (1779–1879), and the Xhosa cattle-killing of 1856–57 as a catastrophe with debated causes. The Great Trek as settler migration onto African land.

### 2. Minerals and passes

Kimberley’s diamonds and the Witwatersrand gold. Migrant labor, compounds, and the expansion of pass laws. Mining capital as an engine of settler power.

### 3. The 1913 Land Act

The act’s text, its reserves, and Sol Plaatje’s *Native Life in South Africa* (1916). The South African Native National Congress (1912), later the African National Congress, protesting to London.

### 4. Rhodesia, Kenya, Portugal

The First Chimurenga (1896–97) and the spirit mediums Nehanda and Kaguvi. Kenya’s White Highlands and the squatter system, setting up Episode 69. Portuguese forced labor and settler schemes.

### 5. 1948 is later

Apartheid’s formalization in 1948, its laws, and its end in the 1990s, noted as later than the scramble and not over at independence elsewhere. The act closes on settler colonies as the hardest to decolonize: settlers had land, votes, and guns, and did not intend to leave.

## Comparative note

Australia and North America (Episode 19) became settler-majority states. Southern African settler colonies remained settler-minority states ruling African majorities. That shaped how, and how violently, they ended.

## How we know

Colonial and Union legislation. Native Affairs Department records (National Archives of South Africa). Mining company records. Sol Plaatje’s writings. Oral histories. Scholarship by Colin Bundy, Shula Marks, Terence Ranger (*Revolt in Southern Rhodesia*, 1967), Jeff Peires (*The Dead Will Arise*, 1989), and Dane Kennedy (*Islands of White*, 1987) on settler society in Kenya and Southern Rhodesia.

## Do not claim

- That apartheid began with the scramble or ended with African independence in 1960.
- That settler land was empty or unused.
- That pass laws began in 1948; their origins are much earlier.
- That Portuguese colonialism was mild or “lusotropical”; that was an ideology.
- That settlers were a single group; Boers, British, and Portuguese settlers had different histories.
