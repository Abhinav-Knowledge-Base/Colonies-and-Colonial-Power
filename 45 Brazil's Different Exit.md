## Before

Episode 44 followed the Spanish American republics. Portugal’s American colony took another path, beginning with a royal flight.

## The turn

When Napoleon invaded Portugal in 1807, the royal family and court, perhaps 10,000–15,000 people, sailed to Brazil under British escort. They arrived in 1808. Rio de Janeiro became the capital of the Portuguese empire. Ports opened to foreign trade, and in 1815 Brazil was raised to a kingdom equal with Portugal. When João VI returned to Lisbon in 1821, the Portuguese Cortes tried to reduce Brazil’s status. His son Pedro, who had stayed as regent, declared independence on 7 September 1822 and became Emperor Pedro I. Fighting occurred, especially in Bahia, where Portuguese troops held out until July 1823. Brazilian forces there included Black and Indigenous soldiers, and a woman, Maria Quitéria, who fought disguised as a man. There was no continental war of liberation.

The Empire of Brazil kept slavery. Brazil continued importing enslaved Africans illegally after an 1831 law banning the trade, until British naval pressure and the Eusébio de Queirós Law (1850) ended imports. Slavery itself ended only with the Lei Áurea of 1888 (Episode 39), after the Free Womb Law (1871), the Sexagenarian Law (1885), a mass abolitionist movement, and the flight of enslaved people from plantations. The monarchy fell to a military coup the following year, in 1889. Brazil’s exit preserved a dynasty, a territorial unity that Spanish America lost, and slavery for 66 more years.

## After

Episode 46 follows the United States as it expanded across the continent and beyond.

## Acts

### 1. The court crosses the ocean

The 1807–08 transfer as a unique event: a European monarchy governing from its colony. Rio de Janeiro remade as a capital, with enslaved labor visible everywhere in the city.

### 2. 1822

The Cortes, Pedro’s declaration, and the Bahia war of 1822–23. Maria Quitéria and Joana Angélica as figures of later memory with documented cores. Independence as an elite-negotiated break with fighting in regions.

### 3. An empire with slaves

The illegal trade after 1831, estimated from Slave Voyages data at hundreds of thousands of people. British pressure and the 1850 law. Coffee expansion in the Paraíba valley and São Paulo on enslaved labor.

### 4. Abolition’s long road

Free Womb Law, abolitionists (André Rebouças, Joaquim Nabuco, and Luís Gama, a formerly enslaved lawyer who freed hundreds through the courts), and mass flight. The Lei Áurea signed by Princess Isabel, and the later memory that credited her alone.

### 5. What the exit kept

The monarchy until 1889, territorial unity, and a planter class that received no compensation but kept land. No land reform for the freed. State-subsidized European immigration. The act hands off to Episode 48.

## Comparative note

Spanish America fragmented into many republics; Brazil held together under a monarchy. Historians link this to the court’s presence, elite fear of slave revolt (the Haitian example), and the centralizing role of the crown. The comparison is institutional.

## How we know

Imperial Brazilian government records (Arquivo Nacional, Rio de Janeiro). Parliamentary debates on the slave trade and abolition. Slave Voyages data for the illegal trade. Abolitionist writings. Scholarship by Emília Viotti da Costa, Leslie Bethell, Sidney Chalhoub, and Kirsten Schultz (*Tropical Versailles*, 2001).

## Do not claim

- That Brazilian independence was bloodless everywhere.
- That Princess Isabel alone freed the enslaved.
- That the illegal trade after 1831 was small.
- That Brazil’s monarchy was a Portuguese colony after 1822; it was an independent empire.
- That abolition in 1888 brought equality (Episode 48).
