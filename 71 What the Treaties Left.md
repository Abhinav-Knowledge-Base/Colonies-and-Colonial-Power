## Before

Episode 70 showed decolonization tangled in the Cold War. By the mid-1960s most of Africa and Asia was independent. The question became what independence had not changed.

## The turn

Colonial borders remained. In 1964 the Organisation of African Unity resolved at Cairo that member states would respect the borders existing at independence, choosing stability over redrawing. Colonial languages remained the languages of law, government, and higher education in many states: French, English, and Portuguese. Economies built around one or two exports — cocoa, copper, groundnuts, coffee — remained dependent on world prices set elsewhere. Former colonial powers kept economic ties. The CFA franc, pegged first to the French franc and then the euro and guaranteed by the French Treasury, continued in fourteen African states; reforms announced in 2019 changed the West African version.

In 1965 Nkrumah published *Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism*. He argued that formally independent states remained economically controlled from outside. The series presents neocolonialism as a critique, named with its author, and as an argument about structures, not a secret cabal.

Objects taken in conquest and collecting stayed in European museums. In 1897 a British punitive expedition sacked Benin City and took thousands of objects, including the brass and ivory works known as the Benin Bronzes. They were sold and dispersed to museums worldwide. Restitution has begun. Germany transferred ownership of its Benin collections to Nigeria in 2022. The Smithsonian and the Horniman Museum returned objects the same year. The British Museum, which holds the largest collection, is restricted by the British Museum Act 1963. The series treats this as a restitution case, not a heist story. Every object shown carries a provenance sentence.

Debt came later. The oil shocks of the 1970s and the interest-rate rise of 1979–82 produced debt crises in many former colonies. In the 1980s and 1990s the IMF and World Bank lent on conditions — structural adjustment programs — that required cuts in public spending, privatization, and trade liberalization. Their effects are debated. This is one act, not a World Bank history.

Episode 72 closes the series: the ending stays unfinished.

## Acts

### 1. Borders

The OAU’s Cairo resolution (1964), secession attempts (Katanga, Biafra 1967–70), and border wars. Borders kept as drawn, and why.

### 2. Languages and economies

Colonial languages in government and schools. Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o’s *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) as an argument for African languages. Monocrop economies and price dependence.

### 3. Neocolonialism, as a critique

Nkrumah’s book, its argument, and its critics. The CFA franc as a case. The act distinguishes structural critique from conspiracy.

### 4. The Benin Bronzes

The 1897 expedition, the dispersal, and the restitution debate. The Oba of Benin and the Nigerian National Commission for Museums and Monuments. The camera shows objects only with provenance. No heist framing.

### 5. Debt and adjustment, in one act

Debt crises and structural adjustment in the 1980s–90s, with debated effects. The HIPC debt relief initiative (1996). One act only.

## Comparative note

Latin American states, independent since the 19th century (Episode 44), also experienced export dependence and debt crises, and Latin American dependency theory (Raúl Prebisch, André Gunder Frank) paralleled Nkrumah’s critique. Dependence did not need a recent colonial flag.

## How we know

OAU resolutions. Central bank and treasury records on the CFA franc. Nkrumah’s writings. Museum provenance records and restitution agreements. IMF and World Bank archives. Scholarship by Dan Hicks (*The Brutish Museums*, 2020), Bénédicte Savoy and Felwine Sarr (*Report on the Restitution of African Cultural Heritage*, 2018), Fanny Pigeaud and Ndongo Samba Sylla on the CFA franc, and Thandika Mkandawire on adjustment.

## Do not claim

- That neocolonialism is a secret cabal; it is a critique of structures.
- That the Benin Bronzes are a heist story; they are a case of military looting and restitution.
- That structural adjustment’s effects are settled.
- That colonial borders were kept only because of European pressure; African states chose to keep them.
- That restitution of objects is complete.
