## Before

Episode 58 showed China forced open but not colonized. To its south, Southeast Asia’s kingdoms and sultanates fell under European rule in the 19th century. Dutch Java’s Cultivation System was told in Episode 26.

## The turn

The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 split the Malay world into British and Dutch spheres along the Straits of Malacca. The Dutch extended rule across the archipelago, fighting a long war in Aceh (1873–c. 1904) and conquering Bali (1906–08, where Balinese nobles’ ritual mass deaths, *puputan*, are documented). Britain founded Singapore (1819) and formed the Straits Settlements (1826). It extended control over Malay states through Residents from the Pangkor Treaty (1874). It conquered Burma in three wars (1824–26, 1852, 1885) and ruled it as a province of British India until 1937. France took Cochinchina (1862–67), made Cambodia (1863) and Annam and Tonkin (1883–84) protectorates, and formed the Indochinese Union (1887), adding Laos in 1893.

Siam kept its independence. Kings Mongkut (Rama IV) and Chulalongkorn (Rama V) signed unequal treaties (the Bowring Treaty, 1855), reformed the state, and ceded territory to France (1893, 1904, 1907) and Britain (1909). They used Anglo-French rivalry to remain a buffer. Colonial economies turned to exports: rubber plantations in Malaya and Sumatra, rice in the Irrawaddy, Chao Phraya, and Mekong deltas, tin in Malaya. Labor migration brought Chinese workers to Malaya’s tin mines and Indian (largely Tamil) workers to rubber estates. J. S. Furnivall called the result a “plural society,” in which communities met in the marketplace but lived apart. Migrants built families, associations, and politics; they were not merely a problem for colonial or later nationalist states.

## After

Episode 60 turns to the Philippines, which changed colonial hands in 1898.

## Acts

### 1. Lines on water

The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 on a map, shown as drawn. The Aceh war as a long colonial war, and Bali’s *puputan*, stated without imagery.

### 2. British Malaya and Burma

Singapore, Pangkor, and the Residents. Burma’s wars and the fall of Mandalay (1885). Burma governed as part of British India, as an administrative fact, not an India episode.

### 3. French Indochina

Conquest of Cochinchina and the protectorates. The Indochinese Union. Rice and rubber (Michelin plantations and their labor conditions). Early Vietnamese resistance, such as the Cần Vương movement (1885–96).

### 4. Siam’s negotiated survival

Mongkut and Chulalongkorn, the Bowring Treaty, reforms, and territorial cessions. Siam as an independent state that also colonized its own peripheries. Survival as negotiation, not luck.

### 5. Plantations, migrants, and plural societies

Rubber estates, tin mines, Chinese and Tamil workers, and the *kangany* recruiting system. Furnivall’s “plural society” as a model. Migrants’ own associations, temples, schools, and unions. The act refuses to reduce migrants to a problem.

## Comparative note

Caribbean and Mauritian plantation colonies also drew indentured Indian labor after slavery (Episode 40). Southeast Asia’s plantations used migrant labor under different contracts. Colonial plural societies left legacies of communal politics in both regions.

## How we know

Colonial archives (Nationaal Archief; The National Archives, UK; ANOM for Indochina). Thai royal archives. Plantation and labor records. Chinese and Tamil community records. Scholarship by J. S. Furnivall (*Colonial Policy and Practice*, 1948), Thongchai Winichakul (*Siam Mapped*, 1994), Anthony Reid, and Sunil Amrith (*Crossing the Bay of Bengal*, 2013).

## Do not claim

- That Siam survived because it was never pressured; it negotiated and ceded territory.
- That Chinese and Indian migrants were colonial agents or a problem by nature.
- That the plural society model describes every colony.
- That Burma’s place in British India makes this an India episode.
- That colonial borders in Southeast Asia followed natural lines.
