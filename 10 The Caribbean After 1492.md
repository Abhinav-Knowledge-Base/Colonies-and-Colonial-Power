## Before

Episode 9 left the plantation model on islands off Africa. Castile had just finished conquering Granada and the Canaries. In 1492 a Genoese navigator under Castilian contract sailed west.

## The turn

Columbus made four voyages (1492–1504). They were real feats of navigation, exploiting the Atlantic trade winds. They were also violent from the start. Columbus seized Taíno people on his first voyage and imposed a gold tribute on Hispaniola that could not be met. The encomienda granted Spaniards the labor and tribute of Indigenous people in return for nominal protection and Christian instruction. In practice it was forced labor. Epidemic disease arrived: smallpox is documented on Hispaniola from 1518. Warfare, forced labor, famine, and flight also played their part. Hispaniola’s Indigenous population fell catastrophically within a generation. The starting figure is modeled and contested (Episode 7), so the series shows the collapse without a single percentage.

Inside the empire, protest came early. In 1511 the Dominican friar Antonio de Montesinos preached in Santo Domingo, asking the settlers by what right they held the Indians in such servitude. The Laws of Burgos (1512) regulated but did not end the encomienda. Bartolomé de las Casas, once an encomendero himself, gave up his grant in 1514 and spent the rest of his life denouncing the abuse. In his early proposals (1516–18) he also suggested importing enslaved Africans to spare the Indigenous. He later regretted this in his *History of the Indies*. The debate at Valladolid (1550–51) set Las Casas against Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda on whether the conquest was just. It was a debate inside empire, not the end of it.

## Acts

### 1. The voyage as navigation

Trade winds, ship types, and Columbus’s logbook, which survives only in Las Casas’s later abstract. The act credits the navigation and labels the log as a secondhand source.

### 2. The voyage as violence

Seizure of Taíno captives in 1492, the tribute system of 1495, and the revolt of the cacique Caonabo and later Enriquillo (1519–1533), who negotiated a peace with the Crown. The Taíno are shown as political actors with chiefdoms (*cacicazgos*).

### 3. Encomienda and collapse

How the encomienda worked, with the Laws of Burgos text. Epidemic, labor, war, and famine together. A graphic shows competing estimates of Hispaniola’s 1492 population. It is labeled “modeled” and has no single percentage.

### 4. Las Casas, whole

Las Casas as witness, polemicist, and bishop of Chiapas. The *Brevísima relación* (1552) was translated across Europe and used by Spain’s rivals as propaganda (the “Black Legend”). His early proposal about African slaves is stated plainly. He is not canonized.

### 5. Valladolid

Sepúlveda’s Aristotelian argument for natural slavery and Las Casas’s rebuttal. No formal verdict was issued. The New Laws of 1542 tried to phase out encomiendas and were partly revoked after settler revolt in Peru. The act ends on the point that a debate about conquest’s justice did not stop conquest.

## Comparative note

Taíno descendants and Taíno identity movements exist today in Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic, Cuba, and the diaspora. Genetic studies show Indigenous ancestry persisted. The series does not end Taíno history in the 16th century.

## How we know

Columbus’s writings, largely through Las Casas’s transcriptions. The Laws of Burgos. Las Casas’s works. Records in the Archivo General de Indias. Archaeology of La Isabela and Taíno sites. Demographic scholarship (Cook and Borah; Massimo Livi Bacci). Ancient DNA studies on Caribbean populations, labeled as recent and developing.

## Do not claim

- That Columbus “discovered” America or that his voyages were only navigation.
- That smallpox alone destroyed the Taíno; labor, war, famine, and flight also mattered.
- That Hispaniola’s 1492 population is known.
- That Las Casas was a saint without contradictions.
- That the Taíno are extinct.
