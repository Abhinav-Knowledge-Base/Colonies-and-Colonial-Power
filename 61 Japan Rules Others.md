## Before

Episode 32 introduced Japan as an imperial peer, taking Hokkaido, Ryukyu, Taiwan, and Korea. Episode 60 showed the Philippines under the US. This hour looks inside Japan’s two main colonies.

## The turn

Japan took Taiwan from the Qing in 1895, after crushing the short-lived Republic of Formosa and years of armed resistance. The Government-General built railways, ports, schools, and a sugar industry. It also enforced police rule and campaigned against Indigenous peoples in the mountains. In the Wushe (Musha) uprising of 1930, Seediq fighters led by Mona Rudao killed about 130 Japanese. The Japanese response, including aerial attacks and the use of poison gas, killed hundreds of Seediq. The accounts come from Japanese records and Seediq memory.

Korea became a protectorate in 1905 and was annexed in 1910. The Government-General under military governors-general ruled harshly in the 1910s. The land survey (1910–18) registered land ownership. Critics argue it dispossessed many Korean farmers, while some historians argue its effects were more mixed; the debate is presented. On 1 March 1919, Koreans declared independence in a nonviolent mass movement across the peninsula. Japanese police and troops suppressed it. Korean sources record thousands of dead, and Japanese figures are lower. Japan shifted to a “cultural rule” policy in the 1920s, then in the 1930s to forced assimilation (*kōminka*). Korean-language education was restricted. From 1940 Koreans were pressed to adopt Japanese-style names (*sōshi-kaimei*). Shinto shrine worship was required. During the war, Koreans were mobilized for labor and military service. The system of military sexual slavery (“comfort women”), which drew heavily on Korean women, is treated in Episode 63.

Some historians describe Japanese rule as “colonial modernity”: railways, industry, and schools that were real, built for Japan’s purposes and imposed by force. The term is presented as a debated concept, not a justification.

## Acts

### 1. Taiwan, 1895

The Republic of Formosa and armed resistance. The Government-General, Gotō Shinpei’s administration, sugar and railways, and police rule. Han Taiwanese and Indigenous peoples under Japanese rule.

### 2. Wushe, 1930

Mona Rudao, the Seediq uprising, and the Japanese response. The camera stays on documents, landscapes, and Seediq testimony, with consultation.

### 3. Korea annexed

The protectorate, the annexation treaty, and the Righteous Armies’ resistance (*uibyeong*). An Jung-geun’s assassination of Itō Hirobumi (1909). The 1910s military rule.

### 4. 1 March 1919

The Declaration of Independence, the nationwide demonstrations, and their suppression, with Korean and Japanese casualty figures side by side. Yu Gwan-sun, a student who died in prison, as a figure of memory with a documented core. The Provisional Government in Shanghai.

### 5. Names and language

The land survey debate. *Kōminka*, *sōshi-kaimei*, and language policy. “Colonial modernity” as a debated concept. The act closes by handing wartime mobilization and sexual slavery to Episode 63.

## Comparative note

European empires also justified rule by civilization. Japan claimed kinship with those it ruled — “same script, same race” — while subordinating them. The claim of shared identity made assimilation more intrusive, not less.

## How we know

Japanese Government-General records. Korean independence movement documents. Taiwanese and Seediq testimony. The 1919 Declaration. Scholarship by Andre Schmid, Gi-Wook Shin and Michael Robinson (eds., *Colonial Modernity in Korea*, 1999), Leo Ching (*Becoming “Japanese”*, 2001), Paul Barclay on Taiwan’s Indigenous peoples, and Mark Caprio.

## Do not claim

- That Japanese colonial rule was beneficial because it built infrastructure.
- That “colonial modernity” is a justification; it is a debated analytical term.
- That 1 March casualties are settled; Korean and Japanese figures differ.
- That the land survey’s effects are uncontested.
- That Japan liberated Asians from Western colonialism in Taiwan or Korea.
