## Before

Season 5 covered the plantation and the slave trade. Episode 41 showed Indigenous survival. Most colonial Spanish America’s labor came from Indigenous and mixed populations through other systems of obligation and coercion.

## The turn

The New Laws of 1542 restricted the encomienda (Episode 10). In its place, colonial officials organized *repartimiento* drafts. Indigenous towns had to send a share of their men in rotation to work for Spanish employers at set wages on farms, construction, and mines. The Andean *mita* (Episode 12) was the largest version. Indigenous towns also paid tribute in money or goods. *Corregidores* forced Indigenous people to buy goods at inflated prices (the *reparto de mercancías*), a major grievance behind the Túpac Amaru II rebellion (Episode 65).

Over time many Indigenous and mixed-ancestry workers moved to Spanish haciendas, where they received advances and could become bound by debt. How coercive debt peonage was varied greatly by region and period. In some places it was a way for workers to secure advances; in others it bound families across generations. In the Andes, *yanaconas* were dependent laborers detached from their communities. *Obrajes* (textile workshops) often held workers under near-prison conditions. Enslaved Africans worked in cities as domestic servants, artisans, and hired-out laborers, and Lima and Mexico City had large Black populations. Free Indigenous villages kept communal land and self-government within limits.

## After

Episode 43 turns to people who escaped colonial control: maroons and borderland nations.

## Acts

### 1. From encomienda to repartimiento

The New Laws and the settler revolt in Peru (1544–48). The shift to crown-organized labor drafts. Records of a repartimiento draft from a Mexican town.

### 2. Tribute and the forced market

Tribute rolls, the *reparto de mercancías*, and Indigenous complaints and lawsuits against *corregidores*.

### 3. The hacienda and debt

Hacienda account books showing advances and debts. The historiographical debate over how coercive debt peonage was. Regional variation stated plainly.

### 4. Obrajes and yanaconas

Textile workshops in Quito and Puebla. Inspectors’ reports on locked workshops. *Yanaconas* in the Andes, and how they differed from mitayos.

### 5. Urban slavery and free villages

Enslaved and free Black people in Lima and Mexico City, including religious brotherhoods (*cofradías*). Free Indigenous villages holding land. The act closes by showing that colonial labor was a map of many systems, not a single regime.

## Comparative note

Brazil relied more heavily on African slavery (Episode 14), and British North America on slavery and indentured servitude. The Spanish American mix of drafts, debt, and tribute reflected large, settled Indigenous populations and a crown that claimed them as tributary vassals.

## How we know

Repartimiento and tribute records. Hacienda account books. *Obraje* inspection reports. Indigenous petitions and lawsuits. Notarial records of slave sales in Lima and Mexico City. Scholarship by Charles Gibson (*The Aztecs Under Spanish Rule*, 1964), Arnold Bauer on debt peonage, Herman Bennett on Africans in colonial Mexico, and Steve Stern on the Andes.

## Do not claim

- That debt peonage was the same everywhere or always slavery by another name.
- That Spanish America relied mainly on African slavery.
- That free Indigenous villages were free of colonial obligations.
- That repartimiento was voluntary wage labor.
- That these systems ended with independence; many continued (Episode 48).
