## Before

Season 5 followed the Atlantic machine. Season 6 returns to the Americas as a whole. It begins with the ecological and demographic transformation that followed contact, and with the people who lived through it.

## The turn

Alfred Crosby called it the Columbian Exchange (1972). From the Americas went maize, potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, chili peppers, cacao, and tobacco. Potatoes and maize became staples from Ireland to China. From Europe, Africa, and Asia came wheat, sugar cane, rice, bananas, horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, and diseases to which Indigenous Americans had no prior exposure: smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus, and later malaria and yellow fever, which arrived with the slave trade. Livestock trampled Indigenous fields. Horses transformed Plains and Pampas societies, which adopted them and built new economies and military power (Episode 43).

Indigenous populations fell catastrophically across the hemisphere in the 16th and 17th centuries. The scale is modeled, not known (Episode 7). Disease worked together with war, enslavement, forced labor, famine, and displacement, which made epidemics deadlier. Survivors adapted. Indigenous communities kept languages, rebuilt towns, and used colonial courts. The *Juzgado General de Indios* in New Spain (from 1592) heard thousands of Indigenous suits. Nahua, Maya, Mixtec, and Andean communities litigated over land and tribute for centuries. Indigenous nations remain today, with millions of speakers of Quechua, Nahuatl, Maya languages, Guaraní, and others.

## Acts

### 1. Out of the Americas

Maize, potatoes, cassava, and chili peppers, and their global spread. Indigenous agricultural science — Andean potato varieties, Mesoamerican milpa — as knowledge Indigenous farmers built. It is not presented as a gift that was “discovered.”

### 2. Into the Americas

Livestock, European crops, and weeds. Elinor Melville’s study of sheep in the Valle del Mezquital (*A Plague of Sheep*, 1994) as a case of ecological transformation.

### 3. Epidemics without a single percentage

Smallpox, measles, and cocoliztli epidemics in 16th-century Mexico (the 1545 and 1576 epidemics, whose cause is debated; recent ancient-DNA work suggests *Salmonella enterica* Paratyphi C for one site). Demographic models shown with their ranges. Disease presented as one factor among several.

### 4. Survival and adaptation

Indigenous towns rebuilt under colonial rule. Nahua *cabildos*, Andean *ayllus*, Maya communities keeping records in their own languages (the *Books of Chilam Balam*). The horse on the Plains and the Pampas.

### 5. The courts

The *Juzgado General de Indios*, Andean litigation, and Indigenous petitions to the Crown. Colonial law as a site of struggle. The act ends with living Indigenous nations and speakers, not with a requiem.

## Comparative note

Hawaii, Aotearoa, and Australia also experienced epidemic collapse after contact (Episodes 19, 32), with different timing and scale. The demographic effects of contact were not unique to the Americas, but their scale there was exceptional.

## How we know

Colonial records of tribute and population. Parish registers. The records of the *Juzgado General de Indios* in the Archivo General de la Nación (Mexico). Indigenous-language documents. Ancient DNA and paleo-environmental studies, labeled as developing science. Scholarship by Alfred Crosby, Elinor Melville, Woodrow Borah (*Justice by Insurance*, 1983), and Noble David Cook (*Born to Die*, 1998).

## Do not claim

- That disease alone caused the collapse.
- That the percentage decline is known.
- That Indigenous history ended with conquest.
- That the Columbian Exchange was a balanced trade; its human costs fell heavily on the Americas and Africa.
- That Indigenous crops were “discovered”; they were developed by Indigenous farmers.
