# Colonies and Colonial Power

*Also called:* **The Colonial Age**

Complete series documentation. One file per episode. Seventy-two episodes.

This folder is only this series. *Discovery of India*, *Cradles of Civilization*, *600–500 BCE*, and *The Last Five Hundred Years* are separate projects, with their own episode counts, honesty rules, and archives. None of them is a season of this series, and this series is not a season, preface, or sequel of any of them. *Discovery of India* owns the long political and social history of the subcontinent, including Company rule, 1857, mass nationalism, Partition, and the republic. This series films the Raj once, as one system inside a world empire (Episode 21), and stops, and repeats the handoff in Episode 62. It does not narrate Gandhi’s life, the Constituent Assembly, or Partition’s regional history. *Cradles of Civilization* owns the origins of cities, states, and writing. The Mexica, the Inca, Kongo, and every other state here appear as societies already in history when Europeans arrived, never as a “cradle.” *600–500 BCE* is a different series with no shared plot. *The Last Five Hundred Years* is the history of instruments and of nature answering back from the 16th century. Botanical gardens, surveys, quinine, and censuses appear here only as tools of rule, not as a science series.

## Thesis

Colonial power was a world system — ships, companies, plantations, missions, law, and guns — not a series of heroic voyages. It remade maps, labor, and ecologies. It moved an estimated 12.5 million Africans across the Atlantic, turned mountains of silver into Chinese tax payments, drew straight lines through other people’s lands, and gave merchants armies.

It was met by survival, adaptation, revolt, and decolonization. Those did not arrive on one day and did not finish. Haiti was free in 1804; Algeria in 1962; Mozambique in 1975. Settler states never “left.” Seventeen territories are still on the United Nations’ list.

The series moves by system, by empire, by the Atlantic machine, and by region, then closes on freedom struggles and what remained. It is honest about violence and extraction. It centers the agency of colonized people: rulers who negotiated, soldiers who mutinied, enslaved people who revolted, farmers who sued, women who marched, writers who wrote back. It carries no imperial nostalgia. It never claims colonialism as pure progress. It never claims colonized peoples were only victims.

## How the episodes connect

Episodes 1–8 build the tools before any empire is allowed to star. The word “colony” is opened like an object. The world the ships entered is shown full of states. The ships, the bulls, and the line on the ocean are filmed as claims. The chartered company is defined as a form. Settler, extractive, plantation, trading-post, and protectorate are defined as models. The archive is exposed as a colonial machine, and the method for every later death toll is taught. The season ends with a list of refusals, including the boundaries with the sibling projects.

Episodes 9–16 follow the Iberian empires: Atlantic islands as the plantation’s rehearsal, Hispaniola and Las Casas whole, Mexico through alliance and Malintzin, the Andes and Potosí, the Manila galleon and the massacres of Manila’s Chinese, Brazil as a Portuguese story with Palmares, the colonial city and its casta paintings, and Iberian rule changing hands.

Episodes 17–24 follow Britain: factories before flags, the Caribbean interest, settlers and the peoples already there (each in its own century), a settler rebellion that kept slavery, the Raj as one system and then a handoff, dominions and race laws, wars that strained the center, and a ledger instead of nostalgia.

Episodes 25–32 follow other empires: the VOC and Banda, the Cultivation System, France overseas, Algeria as a settler republic, the Congo Free State with its disputed toll, German colonies and the Herero and Nama genocide, Italy and Russia and land empire, and Japan and the United States as imperial peers.

Episodes 33–40 film the Atlantic machine: capture and the coast, the Middle Passage without spectacle, the plantation, the household and the law of the mother, cultures made under slavery, Haiti whole, abolition as politics, and the trade’s afterlives.

Episodes 41–48 return to the Americas: collapse and survival, labor beside slavery, maroons and borderlands, creole independence, Brazil’s different exit, the United States as postcolonial and colonial at once, Canada and the North, and equality unfinished.

Episodes 49–56 turn to Africa: states before the scramble, the coast after 1807, missionaries and explorers as claim-makers, Berlin’s record against its myth, conquest on the ground with Adwa, settler Africa, resistance before the flags came down, and a refusal of one “African experience.”

Episodes 57–64 turn to Asia: footholds on crowded seas, unequal treaties in China, Southeast Asia divided, the Philippines changing hands, Japan ruling other Asians, an explicit refusal to retell the Raj, war and occupation and famine and sexual slavery, and Asia leaving on many clocks.

Episodes 65–72 follow resistance and after: revolts older than nationalism, mass politics, the war as hinge, flags and partitions and failed federations, the wars of exit in Algeria and Kenya, Bandung and the Cold War, what the treaties left, and an ending that stays unfinished.

## Honesty rules

1. Say “unknown” when a death toll, date, or motive cannot be settled. Ranges and database estimates stay labeled as estimates.
2. On screen, label **evidenced**, **inferred**, and **later memory**. A conqueror’s letter is a claim.
3. Do not draw modern national borders on pre-colonial maps as if they were natural. When colonial borders are the subject, show them as drawn.
4. Do not rank empires in a morality contest (“the worst,” “the milder”). Compare institutions.
5. Disease, guns, and alliances all matter in early conquest. Do not pick one cause as the whole story. Indigenous and African polities make decisions.
6. Demographic collapse in the Americas is real. The numbers are modeled, not a single revealed percentage.
7. The Atlantic slave trade’s scale comes from voyage evidence — the Slave Voyages / Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database tradition: on the order of 12.5 million people embarked and about 10.7 million disembarked, always as “estimates.” Do not invent a more precise number. Name the Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan slave trades as different systems, so they are neither erased nor used as a diversion from the Atlantic machine.
8. Abolition is politics: interests, revolts, evangelical campaigns, and compensation paid to owners. It is not a morality play in which Britain saves the world.
9. Do not aestheticize the Middle Passage, massacre, famine, torture, sexual violence, or genocide. Establish the analytical need. No lingering on bodies. No reconstructed rape. No music that sells horror.
10. Name perpetrators at the scale the evidence allows: a named order, a company, a government, a unit, a crowd, or unknown. Do not assign collective guilt to present-day nations as if a living people were a colonial office.
11. Settler and extractive colonialism are a useful distinction and a model, not a law every colony fits.
12. Missionary records are not a census of belief. Schools produced both clerks and later critics.
13. The colonial archive over-represents the state. Silence has causes.
14. Decolonization runs on many clocks: Haiti 1804; mainland Latin America in the early 19th century; the dominions; 1945–1975 in much of Asia and Africa; the Portuguese empire into the 1970s; settler states that did not “leave.” Apartheid South Africa does not end in 1960.
15. “Neocolonialism” is a critique, named with its author (Kwame Nkrumah, 1965), not a secret-cabal episode.
16. Living communities — Indigenous nations, descendants of the enslaved, people under recent occupation — are not exhibits. Consult. Show no sensational human remains.
17. This series does not absorb *Discovery of India*, *Cradles of Civilization*, *600–500 BCE*, or *The Last Five Hundred Years*. Cross-reference them as other projects only.

## Season connections

**S1 — The System (1–8).** “Colony” has a Roman past and a modern overseas career. 1492 did not invent domination; what was new was oceanic legal claim, chartered companies, the Atlantic plantation, and global ecological transfer. The ships enter a world full of states. The papal bulls and Tordesillas are claims, not justice. The company fuses commerce and sovereignty. Settler, extractive, and in-between are models. The archive leans toward the state. Numbers come as ranges. The season ends with refusals and sibling boundaries.

**S2 — Iberian Worlds (9–16).** Madeira and São Tomé rehearse the plantation; Elmina trades for gold; Kongo negotiates as a state. Hispaniola collapses under encomienda and epidemic; Las Casas is witness and, for a time, advocate of African slavery. Tenochtitlan falls to an alliance; Malintzin is an actor. Cajamarca is a claim; Potosí is a labor fact. Silver crosses the Pacific; Manila’s Chinese are massacred. Brazil is Portuguese; Palmares lasts a century; the Estado da Índia holds footholds. Casta paintings are ideology. Iberian rule changes hands.

**S3 — Britain (17–24).** English power begins as factories. Barbados and Jamaica build a planter lobby. Settlement in North America, Australia, and Aotearoa is dated in its own centuries, and none of the land is empty. 1776 keeps slavery and empire. The Raj is filmed once as a system and handed to *Discovery of India*. Dominions pass race laws. The South African War’s camps, colonial soldiers, and Amritsar named once. A ledger replaces nostalgia.

**S4 — Other Empires (25–32).** The VOC nearly destroys the Bandanese. The Cultivation System feeds the Dutch budget and famine. France governs with a Code Noir and a civilizing mission. Algeria is declared France and most Algerians are subjects. The Congo Free State’s toll is disputed; the catastrophe is not. Von Trotha’s order and the camps are named as genocide. Italy gasses Ethiopia; Russia conquers over land. Japan and the United States are imperial peers.

**S5 — The Atlantic Machine (33–40).** Capture has named African and European actors. The ship is a system with a barricade, a mortality estimate, and constant revolt. Sugar and cotton regimes differ in law and are not ranked. The law of the mother makes coercion a demographic fact. Cultures are made, not given. Haiti wins, kills, and pays. Abolition is revolt, campaign, and compensation to owners. The afterlives are argued, not equated.

**S6 — The Americas (41–48).** The Columbian Exchange runs both ways; Indigenous peoples go to court for centuries. Labor drafts and debt sit beside slavery. Maroons win treaties; Comanche and Mapuche hold borders. Creole republics keep slavery; Haiti is isolated. Brazil keeps an emperor and slavery to 1888. The United States is postcolonial and colonial. Canada signs treaties read two ways and runs residential schools. Equality stays unfinished.

**S7 — Africa (49–56).** Asante, Dahomey, Sokoto, Ethiopia, Buganda, and the Zulu kingdom are states. Palm oil does not end coercion. Explorers’ maps and treaties are claims. Berlin sets rules, not most borders, and seats no African. Conquest takes decades and African allies; Adwa is an African victory. Settler colonies build land acts and passes; apartheid begins later and ends later. Resistance runs from Bambatha to Chilembwe to newspapers. Africa is not one colony.

**S8 — Asia (57–64).** Europeans hold footholds in crowded seas. China is forced open, not colonized, and not “backward.” Southeast Asia is divided; Siam negotiates survival; migrants are people. The Philippines changes hands and keeps its nationalism. Japan turns civilization against other Asians. The Raj is refused a second time. War breaks European prestige; famine and sexual slavery are named with care. Asia leaves on many clocks.

**S9 — Resistance and After (65–72).** Revolt is older than nationalism. Mass parties, unions, and women’s movements build pressure. The war is a hinge. Independence takes the form of constitution, dominion, partition, or failed federation. Algeria and Kenya are wars of exit, filmed analytically. Bandung and the Congo show decolonization inside the Cold War. Borders, languages, looted objects, and debt remain. The ending stays unfinished.

## What the last episode still owes

A count of the dead of the Congo Free State that rests on more than backward projection. A pre-contact population for the Americas that is observed rather than modeled. The number of Africans who died between capture and the coast, before any ship’s record began. The names of most of the roughly 12.5 million people embarked. A settled toll for Banda in 1621, for the Algerian conquest, for the Philippine-American War, for the Herero and Nama, for the Bengal and Vietnamese famines, and for the Algerian war of independence. The voices of enslaved and colonized women, most of whom appear only as entries in ledgers kept by others. The inland societies of Africa, the Amazon, New Guinea, and Central Asia whose European sources are thin and whose own histories live in languages and oral traditions the series could only glimpse. What Malintzin thought. What the Bandanese in the Kei Islands remember that the VOC did not write down. How much the empire enriched the metropole, measured in a way that is not secretly a verdict. The end of the many clocks of decolonization, several of which are still running. The method can count, compare, and label. It cannot close these accounts without new evidence and without the communities whose history this is.
