## Before

Episode 38 showed Haiti ending slavery by revolution. Elsewhere slavery ended by law, war, and pressure, and slowly.

## The turn

British abolitionists — Quakers, evangelicals such as William Wilberforce, and Black writers such as Equiano and Ottobah Cugoano — campaigned from 1787 against the slave trade. Parliament abolished the British slave trade in 1807, and the United States banned the import of enslaved people in 1808. Slavery itself continued. Enslaved people revolted: in Barbados (Bussa’s rebellion, 1816), Demerara (1823), and Jamaica, where the Baptist War of 1831–32, led by Samuel Sharpe, involved tens of thousands. These revolts, and the violent repression that followed, pushed Parliament toward emancipation. The Slavery Abolition Act (1833) freed enslaved people in most British colonies from 1834, but imposed an “apprenticeship” of unpaid labor that ended in 1838. Owners received £20 million in compensation, about 40 percent of the government’s annual budget. The enslaved received nothing.

France abolished slavery in 1794, restored it in 1802, and abolished it again in 1848, with compensation to owners. The Danish West Indies ended slavery in 1848 after an uprising on St. Croix. The Dutch abolished it in 1863, again compensating owners. In the United States slavery ended through a civil war (1861–65) and the Thirteenth Amendment (1865). The war was not only about slavery, and it was also about slavery. Secession declarations named slavery’s protection, and the Union’s war aims changed as enslaved people fled to Union lines and as Black soldiers enlisted. Spain abolished slavery in Puerto Rico in 1873 and in Cuba in 1886. Brazil was last, with the Lei Áurea of 1888, after a Free Womb Law (1871) and mass flight from plantations. The Malê revolt of Muslim Africans in Bahia (1835) had shaken Brazilian slaveholders. Haiti’s example was a fear in every slaveholding empire.

## After

Episode 40 follows the afterlives of the trade: apprenticeship, indenture, convict leasing, and the argument for reparations.

## Acts

### 1. Campaigners

The abolition movement as a mass campaign: petitions, the sugar boycott, the *Brookes* print, and the kneeling-slave medallion. Black abolitionists as authors and speakers, not only as images.

### 2. Revolt as cause

Bussa, Demerara, the Baptist War, and the Malê revolt, each from trial records and testimony. Enslaved revolt presented as a cause of abolition, not a backdrop.

### 3. Compensation

The 1833 Act, the £20 million, and apprenticeship. The Legacies of British Slavery database shows who was paid, including clergy, widows, and banks. The UK government finished repaying the loan raised for compensation only in 2015. The act presents that as evidenced fact, with its funding structure explained briefly.

### 4. France, the Netherlands, and the United States

1848 in France and Victor Schœlcher’s role. The Dutch 1863 law and its ten-year state supervision period. The US Civil War in one careful formulation. The Thirteenth Amendment’s exception clause for punishment of crime (handoff to Episode 40).

### 5. Cuba and Brazil last

Spain’s Moret Law (1870) and gradual abolition, and Brazil’s long process to 1888. The Royal Navy’s West Africa Squadron (from 1808) as a real anti-trade force and a tool of British power. The act refuses the “Britain saved the world” frame.

## Comparative note

Slavery persisted or expanded in parts of Africa and the Indian Ocean after Atlantic abolition. European colonial powers later used anti-slavery as a justification for conquest in Africa (Episodes 50–52) while imposing forced labor themselves.

## How we know

Parliamentary Papers and statutes. The Legacies of British Slavery database (UCL). Trial records of revolts. French, Dutch, Spanish, and Brazilian abolition laws. Scholarship by Seymour Drescher (*Econocide*, 1977), Eric Williams, Christopher Brown (*Moral Capital*, 2006), João José Reis (*Slave Rebellion in Brazil*, 1993), and Gelien Matthews on Caribbean revolts.

## Do not claim

- That Britain abolished slavery out of pure morality, or that it “saved the world.”
- That enslaved people were passive recipients of freedom.
- That abolition ended coerced labor; apprenticeship and indenture followed.
- That the US Civil War was only about slavery, or not about slavery at all.
- That enslaved people or their descendants received compensation from these governments; owners did.
