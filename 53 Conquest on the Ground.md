## Before

Episode 52 showed partition on paper. To make claims real, European powers had to conquer. That took longer, cost more, and met more resistance than the maps suggest.

## The turn

European armies in Africa had breech-loading rifles, artillery, and from the 1880s the Maxim gun. Hilaire Belloc’s line — “Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not” — is often quoted as if it explained everything. It does not. Conquest depended on African soldiers (the *tirailleurs sénégalais*, the West African Frontier Force, the Force Publique, the *askari*), on African carriers who moved supplies on their heads over long distances, on African allies who used Europeans against their own rivals, and on disease, famine, and political division.

Some African states fought long wars. Samori Touré built the Wassoulou empire in the upper Niger region, with a professional army and workshops that repaired and copied firearms. He fought the French from 1882 to 1898, moving his entire state eastward, before he was captured and exiled. Ethiopia under Menelik II bought modern weapons, mobilized a very large army, and on 1 March 1896 defeated an Italian army at Adwa. It was an African victory that preserved Ethiopian independence and resonated across the colonized world. The British conquest of the Sokoto Caliphate (1900–1903) and of Asante (1896, and the War of the Golden Stool in 1900 led by Yaa Asantewaa) took years. At Omdurman (1898), British-Egyptian forces with Maxim guns killed about 10,000 Sudanese soldiers in a morning, the scene most often used to make machine guns the whole story. It is one scene among many.

Episode 54 turns to Africa’s settler colonies.

## Acts

### 1. The Maxim gun, in proportion

What the weapon did and did not do. Omdurman as the most-used example, placed among longer campaigns. Belloc’s couplet quoted and then taken apart.

### 2. Samori

The Wassoulou state, its army and gun workshops, and its scorched-earth retreat, which also devastated populations in its path. Samori is filmed as a state-builder and warlord, not a simple hero. His capture in 1898.

### 3. A campaign with logistics

The French campaigns in the Western Sudan, or the British Sokoto campaign: carriers, food, water, disease, African soldiers, and allies. A map of supply lines, not a battle reenactment.

### 4. Adwa

Menelik’s diplomacy and arms purchases, Empress Taytu Betul’s role, the mobilization, and the battle. Adwa as an African victory and its meaning for Pan-Africanism. Ethiopia’s own expansion southward in the same years is named, to avoid a simple story.

### 5. Asante and Yaa Asantewaa

The British demand for the Golden Stool in 1900 and the war led by Yaa Asantewaa, queen mother of Ejisu. Her exile. The Golden Stool was never surrendered. The act closes by showing conquest as a long process with African choices on every side.

## Comparative note

In the Americas, Spanish conquest also depended on Indigenous allies and took decades (Episodes 11–12). The pattern of European conquest through local alliances, disease, and persistence, not technology alone, recurs across four centuries.

## How we know

Military records (French colonial archives at ANOM and the Service historique de la Défense; British War Office). African accounts and oral traditions. Ethiopian chronicles. Scholarship by Martin Klein, Yves Person (*Samori*, 1968–75), Raymond Jonas (*The Battle of Adwa*, 2011), Bruce Vandervort (*Wars of Imperial Conquest in Africa*, 1998), and Daniel Headrick on technology and empire.

## Do not claim

- That machine guns alone conquered Africa.
- That Adwa was a minor skirmish; it was a decisive African victory.
- That African soldiers in colonial armies were only victims or traitors; they had varied motives.
- That conquest was quick; it took decades.
- That Samori or Menelik were simple heroes; both built states through war and conquest.
