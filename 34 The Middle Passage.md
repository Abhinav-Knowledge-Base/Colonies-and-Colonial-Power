## Before

Episode 33 followed captives to the coast. Now they were loaded onto ships for a crossing that typically took one to three months.

## The turn

The slave ship was built and run for one purpose. A wooden barricade (*barricado*) across the deck separated the enslaved from the crew and let the crew fire on them in case of revolt. Men were usually shackled in pairs below deck. Women and children were often held separately, closer to the crew, which exposed them to sexual violence. Mortality during the crossing averaged about 12 percent over the trade’s history, higher in early periods and on some routes. That is an estimate from the Slave Voyages database: the difference between an estimated 12.5 million embarked and about 10.7 million disembarked. Sailors also died at high rates.

The enslaved resisted. They refused food, jumped overboard, and rose against crews. The database records hundreds of shipboard revolts. Scholars have estimated that about one voyage in ten experienced some form of revolt, and that resistance raised costs and may have reduced the number of people carried. That is an estimate, and the argument is debated. Revolts such as those on the *Amistad* (1839) are documented in court records.

The ship is known through plans, models, logs, investigations, and testimony. The *Brookes* diagram (1788), produced by abolitionists in Plymouth, showed how people were packed. Olaudah Equiano’s *Interesting Narrative* (1789) contains a famous account of the crossing. Baptismal and naval records suggest he may have been born in South Carolina, which would make his account of Africa and the Middle Passage drawn from others. Vincent Carretta raised this; other scholars dispute it. The series says so. The account remains a powerful and significant text.

## Acts

### 1. The ship as a machine

Ship plans, a model, and the *barricado*. Tonnage, crew, and ship’s surgeon. Numbers are shown as design parameters, not as spectacle. The camera stays on documents, plans, and models.

### 2. Numbers and mortality

The Slave Voyages database: embarked, disembarked, and mortality, all labeled as estimates. Mortality causes are listed plainly — dysentery, smallpox, dehydration, violence, suicide — without imagery.

### 3. Resistance aboard

Shipboard revolts documented in logs, trials, and insurance claims. The *Amistad* case and Sengbe Pieh (Joseph Cinqué). Hunger strikes, refusals, and flight overboard as refusal. Resistance as a constant, not an exception.

### 4. Testimony

Equiano’s narrative with the debate over his birthplace. The 1788–91 parliamentary testimony of sailors and surgeons (Alexander Falconbridge). The *Zong* case (1781–83), in which the crew threw more than 130 captives overboard and the owners sued for insurance. It is stated as a legal record, with no reconstruction.

### 5. Why no reenactment

A short segment explaining the series’ choice not to stage the Middle Passage: no staged drowning, no actors in chains, no music that sells horror. Descendant voices speak about memory, and memorials are shown: the Middle Passage Ceremonies and Port Markers Project in the United States and the Memorial ACTe in Guadeloupe. The act closes on the scale as an estimate and each person as unknown by name.

## Comparative note

Indian Ocean slave voyages and later indentured-labor voyages (Episode 40) also had high mortality and resistance. They were different systems with different legal regimes. They are not the same as the Middle Passage and are not used to dilute it.

## How we know

The Slave Voyages database. Ship logs, insurance records, and court records. Parliamentary Papers (1788–92). The *Brookes* diagram. Equiano’s narrative and Carretta’s biography (*Equiano, the African*, 2005) and its critics. Scholarship by Marcus Rediker (*The Slave Ship*, 2007), Stephanie Smallwood (*Saltwater Slavery*, 2007), and Eltis and Richardson.

## Do not claim

- That mortality figures or numbers carried are exact counts; they are estimates.
- That Equiano’s firsthand account of the Middle Passage is proven or disproven; it is debated.
- That the enslaved did not resist.
- That any staged image in this series depicts a real person’s experience; the series does not stage the crossing.
- That the Middle Passage was comparable to other ocean voyages of its time; it was a system of forced transport of people as property.
