## Before

Episode 2 showed a world full of states. Now two small kingdoms on the Atlantic edge of Europe, Portugal and Castile, sent ships south and west. They also sent lawyers to Rome.

## The turn

The caravel was a small, handy ship with lateen or mixed rig, suited to coastal exploration and sailing against the wind. The carrack was larger, a cargo and war vessel. Both borrowed from Mediterranean, Atlantic, and ultimately Arab and Asian sailing traditions. Ship-mounted cannon gave European vessels an advantage at sea in some encounters, though not everywhere and not always. Portolan charts, the magnetic compass, and later latitude sailing by quadrant and astrolabe made return voyages repeatable.

The legal turn mattered as much. Papal bulls — *Dum Diversas* (1452), *Romanus Pontifex* (1455), and *Inter caetera* (1493) — authorized Portuguese and Castilian monarchs to conquer and enslave non-Christians and to claim lands. The Treaty of Alcáçovas (1479) divided Atlantic spheres between Portugal and Castile. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) drew a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands. The Treaty of Zaragoza (1529) tried to draw the matching line on the other side of the world, in the Moluccas. None of the people living on either side of these lines was asked. The line was a European agreement about other people’s lands. Other European powers ignored it when they could.

## After

Episode 4 turns from the monarch’s claim to a different vehicle: the chartered company.

## Acts

### 1. Two ships and a gun

Replica caravel and carrack under sail, labeled as replicas. A bronze breech-loading swivel gun (*berço*) from a museum. The act explains sailing performance plainly and avoids “superior technology” as an explanation of everything. Ottoman, Gujarati, and Chinese ships were also armed and capable.

### 2. The chart and the sky

Portolan charts, the magnetic compass, the mariner’s astrolabe and quadrant, and Portuguese pilots’ rules for the sun’s declination. Navigation is framed as accumulated craft, not a Eurocentric miracle. Episode 57 returns to Asian and Arab navigational knowledge. Instruments as a science story belong to *The Last Five Hundred Years*, a separate series; here they appear only as tools of claim.

### 3. The bulls

*Dum Diversas*, *Romanus Pontifex*, and *Inter caetera* read in translation. Each is presented as a claim of authority by a pope who had no presence in Africa or the Americas. The act notes that these texts were later cited in the “doctrine of discovery” in settler-state law (for example, *Johnson v. M’Intosh*, US Supreme Court, 1823) and that the Vatican issued a statement in 2023 repudiating the doctrine. It does not treat the bulls as the sole cause of later law.

### 4. Alcáçovas, Tordesillas, Zaragoza

Three treaties on one animated globe. The Tordesillas line’s longitude could not be measured accurately at the time. Brazil’s eastern bulge fell on the Portuguese side. The Zaragoza line’s placement was bargained with money. The act shows the lines as drawn and labels them “claimed, not ruled.”

### 5. Who ignored the line

French privateers, English merchants, and the Dutch Republic rejected Iberian claims. Hugo Grotius argued for *Mare Liberum* (1609), commissioned in the context of the VOC’s seizure of the Portuguese carrack *Santa Catarina* (1603). Indigenous and African rulers were not parties to any of it. The act ends by distinguishing claim from control, a distinction the series uses in every later map.

## Comparative note

The Ming state also claimed a world order through tribute diplomacy, and Ottoman sultans claimed universal authority. Legal claims to rule the world were not unique. Dividing the planet’s oceans between two kingdoms in a bilateral treaty was specific to these Iberian treaties.

## How we know

Treaty texts in the Archivo General de Indias and the Torre do Tombo (Lisbon). Papal bulls in Vatican registers. Surviving portolan charts and navigational manuals (for example, the *Regimento do astrolábio*). Nautical archaeology of 15th- and 16th-century wrecks.

## Do not claim

- That the papal bulls conferred justice, legitimacy, or real control over the lands they named.
- That the Tordesillas line could be measured precisely in 1494.
- That European ships were superior in every sea and every encounter.
- That the “doctrine of discovery” is simply the text of one bull; it is a later legal construction that cited these texts.
- That navigation was invented in Portugal.
