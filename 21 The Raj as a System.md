## Before

Episode 17 showed the East India Company as a trader at Surat and Madras. Episode 20 showed Britain keeping a world empire after losing thirteen colonies. In South Asia, the Company became something no charter had anticipated: a territorial power collecting land revenue from millions.

## The turn

After the battle of Plassey (1757) and the grant of the *diwani* — the right to collect revenue — of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa from the Mughal emperor in 1765, the Company became a revenue state. Land revenue paid for an army. The army, recruited overwhelmingly from South Asian soldiers (sepoys) under British officers, extended Company territory through wars and treaties. Law was codified and administered through Company courts. Land settlements such as the Permanent Settlement of Bengal (1793) and the *ryotwari* system in Madras fixed who owed revenue and on what terms. Parliament regulated the Company from 1773 and ended its trading monopoly in stages (1813, 1833).

In 1857 a rebellion that began among the Company’s own soldiers spread across much of northern India. It was suppressed with great violence on both sides. In 1858 Parliament transferred rule from the Company to the Crown. This hour names 1857 in one careful act, as a rupture that changed the form of imperial rule. It does not re-enact the war.

For the world empire, India mattered as a system: a source of revenue, an army deployable across Asia and Africa, and a market. The political and social history of the subcontinent — its regions, religions, caste, nationalism, Partition, and the republic — is *Discovery of India*, a separate project. It is not continued here. There is no Gandhi biography in this series.

## After

Episode 22 turns to the white dominions and crown colonies, which Britain governed very differently from India.

## Acts

### 1. The revenue state

The *diwani* grant of 1765 as a document. How land revenue became the Company’s base. The Bengal famine of 1769–70 is named as a catastrophe under Company rule whose toll is estimated, not known. It is stated once.

### 2. The army

The Company’s presidency armies. Sepoys as professional soldiers with their own interests, terms, and grievances. Their deployment beyond South Asia, to Java (1811), Burma, China, and later Africa, made them an imperial instrument (Episode 62 returns to this).

### 3. Law and land

Company courts, the codification that later produced the Indian Penal Code (1860), and land settlements that fixed revenue obligations. The act films these as instruments of a world empire, comparable with Dutch Java and French Algeria, not as a history of Indian society.

### 4. 1857, one act

The rebellion named as a rupture: its military origin, its spread, and its suppression. Violence on both sides is stated at the scale historians document, without staging. Its names — mutiny, rebellion, first war of independence — are themselves arguments. The consequence for this series is institutional: the Government of India Act 1858 ended Company rule.

### 5. Here the series stops

Crown rule, the India Office, and the Indian Army sketched as parts of a global system. Then a title card and a plain statement: the history of the subcontinent under British rule and after — regions, religions, caste, the national movement, Partition, and the republic — is *Discovery of India*, a separate project with its own episodes, rules, and archive. This series does not continue it.

## Comparative note

Dutch Java (Episode 26) and French Indochina (Episode 59) also built revenue systems on land and labor. The difference in South Asia was scale and the size of the army it paid for, which Britain used across the world.

## How we know

The India Office Records at the British Library, treated here as the archive of an imperial system and not as the basis of an India series. Parliamentary papers. Company regulating acts. Scholarship by C. A. Bayly, P. J. Marshall, and Douglas Peers on the Company army. Regional and social history is left to *Discovery of India*.

## Do not claim

- That this episode is a history of India under British rule; it is one system within a world empire.
- That the Company ruled from 1600, or that its rule was uncontested.
- That 1857 is narrated or re-enacted here; it is named as a rupture and left.
- That Gandhi, the Congress, the Constituent Assembly, or Partition are covered in this series; they belong to *Discovery of India*.
- That Company or Crown rule was either pure progress or the whole of South Asian history in this period.
