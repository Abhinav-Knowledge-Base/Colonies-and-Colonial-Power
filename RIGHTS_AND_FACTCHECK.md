# Rights and fact-check

Clearance and claim rules for *Colonies and Colonial Power* (*The Colonial Age*). This document belongs to this series only.

Series bible: [`README.md`](README.md). Production: [`PRODUCTION_GUIDE.md`](PRODUCTION_GUIDE.md). Packaging: [`SEASONS_AND_PATHS.md`](SEASONS_AND_PATHS.md). Evidence: [`SOURCE_CATALOG.md`](SOURCE_CATALOG.md). Comparison: [`COMPARATIVE_MATRIX.md`](COMPARATIVE_MATRIX.md).

## 1. Claim labels

Every factual claim in narration or caption is checked against a source and assigned a label from the production guide: **EV** (evidenced), **CL** (claim by an interested party), **INF** (inferred), **MEM** (later memory), **EST** (estimate), **MOD** (modeled), or **CON** (contested).

Fact-check procedure:

1. Each script line with a date, number, name, quotation, or causal claim is logged with its source.
2. Numbers are checked against the most recent edition of their source (for example, the current Slave Voyages estimates) and shown with the label and source.
3. Quotations are checked against a published edition and credited. Online quotations are never trusted without a primary or scholarly source.
4. Contested claims are reviewed by at least two specialists with different positions where possible.
5. Each episode’s “Do not claim” list is checked against the final script and captions before picture lock.

### Standing numerical rules

| Topic | Rule |
|---|---|
| Atlantic slave trade | On the order of 12.5 million embarked, about 10.7 million disembarked — “estimates,” Slave Voyages. No more precise figure. |
| Americas’ demographic collapse | Modeled; ranges only; no single percentage. |
| Congo Free State | Range with methods; label **CON**; no single figure as fact. |
| Herero and Nama | Proportions as estimates; label **EST**. |
| Amritsar 1919 | Hunter Committee figure (379) and Indian estimates (about 1,000) shown together. |

## 2. Human remains

- **No human remains are shown on screen.** This includes skulls taken from Namibia, Guanche mummies, remains in museum stores, epidemic cemeteries, and the remains of enslaved people in burial grounds.
- Where remains are part of the story — repatriation of Herero and Nama skulls from Germany, Lumumba’s tooth returned in 2022, Aboriginal ancestral remains returned from European museums — the story is told through documents, ceremonies filmed with the community’s permission, and interviews.
- Burial grounds (for example, Newton Slave Burial Ground in Barbados, the African Burial Ground in New York) are filmed only as landscapes and memorials, with the custodians’ permission.
- Medical and “racial science” photographs of colonized people are not used.

## 3. Indigenous and descendant consultation

- Every episode that films an Indigenous nation, a maroon people, a descendant community of the enslaved or indentured, or a people under recent occupation logs its consultation: who was consulted, when, what was agreed, and what was changed.
- Consultation is with the community’s own governing or representative body, not only with individual interviewees.
- Communities may restrict filming of sacred sites, ceremonies, and knowledge. Restrictions are respected without argument.
- Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander viewers are warned that the program may contain images and voices of deceased persons.
- Communities are offered review of their representation before picture lock where feasible, and are credited.
- Descendant communities are not asked to perform suffering for the camera.

## 4. Sexual violence

- **No graphic depiction of sexual violence. No reconstruction.** This applies to slavery (Episode 36), conquest, and the Japanese military sexual slavery system (Episode 63).
- Survivor testimony is used only from public record (for example, testimony given before courts, commissions, or public hearings) and with consent from survivors or their organizations.
- Perpetrators’ own records (Thistlewood’s diaries) are shown as documents without extended reading.
- Language is precise: “sexual violence,” “sexual slavery,” “rape.” Not euphemism, and not sensational detail.

## 5. Genocide episodes

Episodes treating events that historians or states have called genocide (Episode 30, Herero and Nama; related material in Episodes 19, 29, 46, 47) follow additional rules:

- The term is attributed: which historians, courts, commissions, or governments use it, and on what basis. The German government’s 2021 acknowledgment is presented with the objections of Herero and Nama representatives. The Canadian TRC’s term “cultural genocide” is attributed to the TRC.
- Where the term is debated (the Congo Free State; the California case; the Circassian expulsion), the debate is presented rather than resolved by narration.
- Orders and laws are read once as documents. No repeated use for dramatic effect.
- Descendant communities are consulted on how their history is framed.
- No comparison ranking of genocides.

## 6. Restitution and colonial collections

- Every object shown on screen carries a **provenance sentence**: how it left its place of origin, when, by whom (if known), and where it is now. Example: “Taken by British forces from Benin City in 1897; now in the Ethnologisches Museum, Berlin; ownership transferred to Nigeria in 2022.”
- **No unprovenanced “tribal” objects** are presented as known loot, and no objects are presented as “ethnographic” without a provenance sentence. Where provenance is unknown, the caption says so.
- Restitution cases (Benin Bronzes, Maqdala objects, Yuanmingyuan objects, Herero and Nama objects, Congolese collections at Tervuren) are presented as legal and political processes, not heist stories.
- Museums are asked for permission and their own current labels are shown.
- Requests from source communities not to film certain objects are respected.

## 7. Colonial photographs of prisoners and the dead

- Colonial photographs of prisoners (chained captives, detainees, executed men, camp inmates) and of the dead or mutilated raise questions of consent and dignity. Their subjects could not refuse.
- Use requires: (1) analytical need — the photograph’s existence is itself evidence the episode needs; (2) provenance — photographer, date, purpose; (3) consultation with descendant communities where identifiable; (4) brevity — no slow zoom, no music.
- Where possible, the series shows the photograph’s context (the album, the report, the campaign pamphlet) rather than the image full-frame.
- Alice Seeley Harris’s Congo photographs (Episode 29), Boer War camp photographs (Episode 23), Herero prisoners in chains (Episode 30), and Mau Mau detention images (Episode 69) are reviewed individually under these rules. Default: not shown full-frame.
- Named individuals in such photographs are identified where the record allows, so that they are not anonymous objects.

## 8. Archival rights

- All archive material is **permission required** unless explicitly cleared as public domain in all target territories.
- US federal records (NARA, Library of Congress) may be public domain in the US; status is verified for other territories.
- Newsreels (British Pathé, INA, Istituto Luce, ECPAD, NHK) are licensed and labeled with the producer and original purpose.
- Archives in formerly colonized states are credited on screen and paid on the same terms as metropolitan archives.

## 9. Living people and recent events

- Survivors, veterans, and descendants interviewed on camera give informed consent, including consent for use in educational cuts.
- Claims about living people or current institutions (universities, companies, governments) are checked against their public statements and offered for right of reply where appropriate.
- Ongoing disputes (Chagos, New Caledonia, Western Sahara, Palestine, reparations) are presented with dates and attributed positions. Captions are updated before each distribution.

## 10. Sibling-project boundaries

Fact-check includes a boundary check. If a script line narrates the history of the subcontinent beyond Episode 21’s system view, the origins of pre-colonial states, the axial age, or the history of science, it is cut or handed to the owning project: *Discovery of India*, *Cradles of Civilization*, *600–500 BCE*, or *The Last Five Hundred Years*.
