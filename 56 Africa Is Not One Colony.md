## Before

Episodes 49–55 followed states, trade, claims, conquest, settlement, and resistance. The season closes by breaking up the idea of a single colonial Africa.

## The turn

British indirect rule, articulated by Frederick Lugard in northern Nigeria and in *The Dual Mandate* (1922), governed through existing rulers or through “chiefs” appointed where none had existed. In southeastern Nigeria, colonial officials created “warrant chiefs,” which helped provoke the Women’s War of 1929 (Episode 55). French policy spoke of assimilation and later “association.” In practice most Africans were *sujets* under the *indigénat*, while a small number of *évolués* and the citizens of the Four Communes had more rights. Belgian Congo combined state, Church, and company in a paternalist system. Settler colonies (Episode 54) had their own logic.

Economies varied too. On the Northern Rhodesian Copperbelt, large mines drew migrant workers and produced strikes in 1935 and 1940. In the Gold Coast, African farmers built the world’s largest cocoa industry on their own initiative, and Polly Hill’s study showed them as capitalist migrant farmers. In the Belgian Congo, Lever’s Huileries du Congo Belge concession (1911) used forced labor to collect palm fruit. In Lagos, African lawyers such as Christopher Sapara Williams (called to the bar in 1879) practiced in colonial courts and led early political associations. These lives do not add up to a single experience.

## Acts

### 1. Indirect rule

Lugard, the Sokoto emirates under British rule, and warrant chiefs in the southeast. How indirect rule strengthened some authorities and invented others. Mahmood Mamdani’s argument in *Citizen and Subject* (1996) about “decentralized despotism,” presented as an argument.

### 2. Assimilation and its limits

French West Africa, *sujets* and *citoyens*, the *indigénat*, and forced labor. The small number of *évolués*. Senghor’s generation, handed forward to Episode 66.

### 3. A miner and a farmer

The Copperbelt strikes of 1935 and 1940, and migrant labor. Gold Coast cocoa farmers, Polly Hill’s *The Migrant Cocoa-Farmers of Southern Ghana* (1963), and the cocoa hold-ups of 1937–38 against European buying firms.

### 4. A concession and a lawyer

Huileries du Congo Belge and forced labor. Sapara Williams and the Lagos bar. Colonial courts as places of both domination and argument.

### 5. Many Africas

A matrix on screen: rule type, economy, and labor regime, colony by colony, with no moral ranking. The act closes Season 7 by refusing one “African experience.”

## Comparative note

In Asia (Season 8), the variety was as great: Dutch Java, British Malaya, French Indochina, Siam’s negotiated survival, and Japanese Korea. Comparative method across colonies is the point of Season 9.

## How we know

Colonial administrative reports. Mining company and union records. Cocoa marketing records. Company archives (Unilever Archives). Court records. Scholarship by Mahmood Mamdani, Frederick Cooper (*Decolonization and African Society*, 1996), Polly Hill, Jules Marchal on Lever in the Congo, and Kristin Mann on Lagos.

## Do not claim

- That there was a single African colonial experience.
- That indirect rule preserved “traditional” authority unchanged.
- That French assimilation made most Africans French citizens.
- That African farmers were only coerced laborers; some built large cash-crop economies on their own initiative.
- That Mamdani’s thesis is uncontested; it is an influential argument.
