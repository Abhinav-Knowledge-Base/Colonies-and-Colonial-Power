## Before

Episode 67 showed the war as a hinge. After 1945, colonial powers and nationalist movements negotiated — or fought over — how independence would happen. The form mattered as much as the date.

## The turn

The most common path was a negotiated constitution. Colonial powers held conferences, drafted constitutions (often in London or Paris), held elections, and transferred power. Ghana (1957) and Nigeria (1960) became independent this way. Some colonies became dominions first: Ceylon (1948), Ghana at independence, and others retained the British monarch as head of state for a time. France offered its African colonies a referendum in 1958 on joining the French Community. Guinea alone voted no, under Sékou Touré, and became independent immediately, and France withdrew abruptly. The rest became independent in 1960.

Partition was an exit technique. British India was partitioned in 1947. This series states that as a clause and hands it to *Discovery of India*, which tells it. Palestine came under British military occupation in 1917–18 and was then governed by Britain under a League of Nations mandate approved in 1922. In November 1947 the UN General Assembly adopted Resolution 181, a plan to partition the mandate into Jewish and Arab states with an international regime for Jerusalem. The Jewish Agency accepted it, and Arab leaders and states rejected it. Civil war followed. Britain withdrew on 14 May 1948, and Israel declared independence the same day. Arab states invaded. By 1949 armistice lines divided the territory. An estimated 700,000 to 750,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled, and most were not allowed to return — the Nakba, as Palestinians call it. Historians debate the balance of flight and expulsion in particular places, and document both. Many Jewish survivors of the Holocaust were among the new state’s population, and in the following years large numbers of Jews left or were driven from Arab countries. The series presents this as a partition under a mandate, in one act, and does not take sides by map graphics that pretend neutrality. Any map carries a note on its sources and on what its lines mean (see `PRODUCTION_GUIDE.md`).

Federations were meant to create viable states from small colonies. Three failed. The Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland (Central African Federation, 1953–63) was imposed over African opposition to entrench settler power and collapsed when Nyasaland and Northern Rhodesia moved to independence as Malawi and Zambia (1964). The West Indies Federation (1958–62) ended when Jamaica voted to leave in a 1961 referendum and Trinidad followed. The Mali Federation of Senegal and French Soudan (1959–60) broke apart two months after independence in August 1960.

## After

Episode 69 follows the wars of exit in Algeria and Kenya.

## Acts

### 1. Independence as a document

Constitutional conferences at Lancaster House and in Paris. Ghana 1957, Nigeria 1960, and the French Community referendum of 1958. Guinea’s “no.”

### 2. The dominion path

Ceylon and others: independence with the Crown as head of state, the Commonwealth, and later republics. Continuity of institutions and law.

### 3. Partition as exit

India, 1947, in one clause with a handoff card to *Discovery of India*. Palestine, 1948, in one careful act: the mandate, Resolution 181, the war, the flight and expulsion of Palestinian Arabs with estimated numbers, Israel’s founding, and Jewish departures from Arab countries in the following years. Maps with notes, historians from different positions, and no staged scenes. Partition as a colonial exit technique that left wars.

### 4. Federations that failed

The Central African Federation (1953–63), the West Indies Federation (1958–62), and the Mali Federation (1959–60), each with its dates and the reasons for failure: settler interests, island rivalries, and political differences between leaders (Senghor and Modibo Keïta).

### 5. What a flag settled

What the forms decided: borders, constitutions, and whether minorities and settlers had guarantees. What they did not: economic dependence and internal conflicts. Handoff to Episode 71.

## Comparative note

The Spanish American republics (Episode 44) fragmented after independence. Mid-20th-century federations attempted the opposite: to build larger states from colonial units. Both show that colonial administrative lines were powerful, whether kept or merged.

## How we know

Constitutional conference records (The National Archives, UK; ANOM). UN General Assembly records. The 1958 referendum results. Federation records. Scholarship by Frederick Cooper (*Citizenship between Empire and Nation*, 2014), Eric Duke on the West Indies Federation, Benny Morris (*The Birth of the Palestinian Refugee Problem Revisited*, 2004), Rashid Khalidi (*The Iron Cage*, 2006), and Avi Shlaim, presented as scholars with differing interpretations.

## Do not claim

- That this episode tells the Partition of India; it names it and hands it on.
- That independence took one standard form.
- That the Palestine partition and war can be summarized by a single map without sources and notes.
- That Palestinian displacement in 1948 was entirely flight or entirely expulsion; historians document both.
- That federations failed only because of African or Caribbean leaders; colonial design and settler interests played major roles.
