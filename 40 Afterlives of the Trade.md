## Before

Episode 39 showed abolition as politics. Emancipation left formerly enslaved people with freedom and little else. Planters kept the land and sought new labor.

## The turn

In the British Caribbean, apprenticeship (1834–38) kept the formerly enslaved working without wages. When it ended, planters in Trinidad, British Guiana, Jamaica, and Mauritius imported indentured laborers from India. More than a million Indians went under indenture to British, French, and Dutch colonies between 1834 and 1917. Chinese laborers went to Cuba and Peru. Javanese went to Suriname. Indenture was contract labor under penal sanction, with high mortality and abuse. It was not slavery, and it was a coerced labor system that replaced it.

In the United States, Southern states passed Black Codes (1865–66) restricting freedpeople’s movement and labor. The Thirteenth Amendment allowed involuntary servitude as punishment for crime, and convict leasing hired out prisoners, overwhelmingly Black, to private companies. Death rates were high. Sharecropping bound families to land through debt. Reconstruction’s gains were reversed by violence and by Jim Crow laws. Historians debate how far these systems continued slavery and how far they were new forms of racial control. The series argues each case rather than asserting identity.

Institutions built on the trade still exist. Insurance markets, banks, and universities profited from it. Georgetown University’s Jesuit owners sold 272 enslaved people in 1838. The University of Glasgow published a report in 2018 on the slavery-derived donations it received. Reparations are a living political argument: CARICOM’s Ten-Point Plan (2013), US proposals such as H.R. 40, and city-level programs. They are presented as argument, with their supporters and critics.

## Acts

### 1. Apprenticeship and the land

The end of apprenticeship in 1838, and freedpeople’s efforts to acquire land and form free villages in Jamaica and Guyana. Planters’ efforts to prevent it. The Morant Bay rebellion of 1865 is handed forward to Episode 65.

### 2. Indenture

Indian indenture from Calcutta and Madras, Chinese contract labor, and Javanese migration. Contracts, depots, and ships. Living descendant communities in Trinidad, Guyana, Suriname, Fiji, and Mauritius. The act notes that the history of indenture’s origins in South Asia belongs partly to *Discovery of India*, and films here only the plantation colonies.

### 3. Black Codes and convict leasing

Mississippi’s and South Carolina’s 1865 codes read as text. The Thirteenth Amendment’s exception clause. Convict leasing records and mortality from state reports. The debate over continuity versus new forms of control.

### 4. Profits and institutions

Insurance, banking, and university endowments. Georgetown’s 1838 sale and the descendants’ organizing. Glasgow’s reparative-justice program. Lloyd’s of London’s 2020 acknowledgment. Each is evidenced by the institution’s own records and reports.

### 5. Reparations as argument

CARICOM’s Ten-Point Plan, H.R. 40, and the Evanston program (2021). Arguments for, arguments against, and questions of form: apology, education, health, and land. The act presents the argument and does not decide it.

## Comparative note

In the Dutch empire, after the 1863 abolition in Suriname came ten years of state supervision (1863–73). Brazil’s abolition in 1888 came with no land reform and with state-subsidized European immigration. Afterlives differed by empire and state.

## How we know

Colonial Office records on apprenticeship and indenture. Emigration registers (the Indian indenture records in the National Archives of Trinidad and Tobago and Guyana; Suriname’s immigration registers, online). State penitentiary reports on convict leasing. Institutional reports (Georgetown Working Group, 2016; Glasgow, 2018). Scholarship by Hugh Tinker (*A New System of Slavery*, 1974, whose title is itself an argument), Douglas Blackmon, Eric Foner, and Hilary Beckles (*Britain’s Black Debt*, 2013).

## Do not claim

- That indenture was the same as slavery; it was a different coerced labor system.
- That convict leasing was simply slavery under another name without argument.
- That reparations are either settled policy or unreasonable; they are a living political argument.
- That abolition ended racial coercion in labor.
- That descendant communities of indentured laborers are a footnote.
