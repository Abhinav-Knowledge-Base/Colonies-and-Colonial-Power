## Before

Episode 51 ended with treaties and maps as paper claims. By the early 1880s European rivalry in Africa was intensifying: Britain’s occupation of Egypt (1882), French and Belgian claims on the Congo, and Germany’s sudden acquisitions in 1884.

## The turn

Otto von Bismarck hosted a conference in Berlin from November 1884 to February 1885. Fourteen states attended, including the United States and the Ottoman empire. No African state or ruler was invited. The resulting General Act did not draw most of Africa’s borders. It declared free trade and navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers. It recognized the Congo Free State under Leopold II (Episode 29). It committed signatories to suppress the slave trade. It required that new claims on the African coast be notified to other powers and backed by “effective occupation,” a principle later stretched inland.

The actual partition happened over the next three decades, through bilateral treaties between European powers (the Anglo-German agreements of 1886 and 1890, the Anglo-French convention of 1898), military conquest (Episode 53), and deals that drew straight lines along latitudes and rivers. Many borders cut across political and cultural units; others followed existing ones. The popular myth of Africa carved up on one table in Berlin compresses a longer and more violent process into a single scene. The myth is filmed as a myth, and the diplomatic record is filmed as the record.

## After

Episode 53 moves from paper to the ground: conquest over decades.

## Acts

### 1. The conference room

Who attended, who did not, and what they discussed. A reading of the General Act’s key articles. No African delegate.

### 2. The myth

The image of Europeans with crayons and a map: where it comes from, and what it gets right (Europe decided without Africans) and wrong (borders drawn in one afternoon). Popular maps are labeled as simplifications.

### 3. Effective occupation

The principle, how it was stretched from coasts to interiors, and how it encouraged conquest to prove claims.

### 4. Lines drawn later

Bilateral treaties, 1886–1914. The Anglo-German treaty of 1890 (the Heligoland–Zanzibar Treaty). Straight lines as drawn: colonial borders shown as drawn, not as natural. The Fashoda crisis (1898). Borders that split communities, and borders that followed existing ones.

### 5. What Berlin did

The conference’s real effects: rules for claims, recognition of Leopold, and a diplomatic framework for partition. The act closes by setting up the ground war of Episode 53. It notes that the Organisation of African Unity later chose to keep colonial borders (Cairo resolution, 1964; Episode 71).

## Comparative note

The Congress of Vienna (1815) and the post-1919 settlements also drew borders without the consent of those living within them. Berlin was one of several European diplomatic settlements that decided others’ territories.

## How we know

The General Act of the Berlin Conference and conference protocols. Bilateral treaty texts. Foreign ministry records (British FO, French Quai d’Orsay, German Auswärtiges Amt). Scholarship by Stig Förster, Wolfgang Mommsen, and Ronald Robinson (eds.), *Bismarck, Europe and Africa* (1988); Thomas Pakenham (*The Scramble for Africa*, 1991), used critically; and A. I. Asiwaju on partitioned Africans.

## Do not claim

- That the Berlin Conference drew Africa’s borders.
- That any African ruler was represented.
- That the partition was complete in 1885; it took decades.
- That every colonial border ignored existing polities; some followed them.
- That the crayons-on-a-map image is the historical record.
