## Before

Season 4 ended with the imperial peers of the 19th century. Season 5 returns to the system that joined three continents from the 16th to the 19th centuries: the Atlantic slave trade. It carried on the order of 12.5 million people from Africa, of whom about 10.7 million arrived in the Americas. These are estimates from the Slave Voyages database (Episode 7).

## The turn

Most captives were enslaved within Africa before they reached a European ship. They were taken in wars between states, in raids, through judicial sentences for crimes or witchcraft accusations, through debt, or by kidnapping. African rulers and merchants — in Dahomey, Oyo, Asante, the Aro network of the Bight of Biafra, the Kongo and Ndongo regions, and elsewhere — sold captives to Europeans in exchange for textiles, metals, cowries, alcohol, and guns. Some states restricted the trade: Benin limited the sale of male captives for long periods. Kongo’s Afonso I protested it (Episode 9). Others built power on it.

European companies and private traders built forts and trading lodges along the coast: Elmina, Cape Coast, Ouidah, Bonny, Luanda, and Gorée. The Portuguese governed Luanda directly. Elsewhere Europeans usually depended on the permission of African rulers. Whether guns bought with captives fueled wars that produced more captives — the “gun-slave cycle” — is a debated model. Some historians find strong evidence in particular regions, and others find the link weaker. The series presents it as a model. It neither blames “Africans” as a bloc nor erases African participation. Responsibility is named at the scale evidence allows: a state, a merchant house, a company.

## Acts

### 1. Ways of becoming enslaved

War, raid, judicial enslavement, debt, and kidnapping, each with a documented example. Slavery existed in many African societies in forms that differed from Atlantic chattel slavery. The Atlantic demand transformed some of them.

### 2. States and merchants

Dahomey’s royal trade through Ouidah, the Aro network, Asante’s northern wars, and Portuguese Angola’s wars with Ndongo and Queen Njinga (Nzinga Mbande, 1620s–1660s), who fought, negotiated, and also traded in captives. Named actors, not a bloc.

### 3. Forts and factors

Elmina, Cape Coast Castle, and the dungeons. Gorée’s “House of Slaves” and the debate over how many captives actually passed through Gorée; the site’s memorial meaning stands whatever the number. Company officers, African brokers, and Afro-European intermediaries.

### 4. The gun-slave cycle, as a debate

The evidence for and against the model, region by region. The act uses it to show how historians argue causation.

### 5. Looking for the captive’s view

Rare narratives: Olaudah Equiano’s account of capture (its firsthand status debated; Episode 34), Mahommah Gardo Baquaqua (1854), and Omar ibn Said’s Arabic autobiography (1831). Archaeology of coastal and inland sites, and oral traditions of raiding and loss. The act closes on how much remains unknown.

## Comparative note

The trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean slave trades were different systems, older and running in parallel, carrying captives north and east across the Sahara and the ocean. They are named so they are not erased. They are not used to divert attention from the Atlantic machine, which this season films.

## How we know

Company records (Royal African Company, WIC, Portuguese crown). The Slave Voyages database, including the African Origins project. African oral traditions. Archaeology of coastal and inland sites. Scholarship by John Thornton, Paul Lovejoy (*Transformations in Slavery*), Joseph Inikori, Robin Law on Ouidah, and Linda Heywood on Njinga.

## Do not claim

- That Africans as a whole sold Africans; name states, merchants, and networks.
- That African participation was absent or marginal.
- That the gun-slave cycle is proven for all regions.
- That the Gorée House of Slaves was the main embarkation point for millions; its memorial role is separate from its disputed volume.
- That the Indian Ocean or trans-Saharan trades make the Atlantic trade less significant.
