## Before

Seven episodes built the tools: a word, a world, a line, a charter, a vocabulary, an archive, and a method for numbers. This hour closes the season by saying what the series will not do with them.

## The turn

Documentaries about empire often fail in predictable ways. Some are nostalgic: pith helmets, trains, golden light over a governor’s lawn, and a voiceover about roads and railways. Some are morality plays in which one empire is the worst and another the mildest. Some stage atrocities for emotional effect and use the colonized dead as backdrop. Some make colonized peoples pure victims, or pure noble resisters. Some claim one cause — germs, guns, or steel — explains conquest.

This series refuses each of these, and says so on screen. It also refuses to become other series. *Discovery of India* owns the long political and social history of the subcontinent, including Company rule, 1857, mass nationalism, Partition, and the republic. This series films the Raj once as a system inside a world empire (Episode 21) and stops, and says so again in Episode 62. *Cradles of Civilization* owns the origins of cities, states, and writing. The Aztec, Inca, and African states appear here as societies already in history when Europeans arrived. *600–500 BCE* is a different series with no connection to this one’s plot. *The Last Five Hundred Years* tells the history of instruments and nature answering back from the 16th century. Botanical gardens, surveys, and censuses appear here only as tools of rule.

## Acts

### 1. No discovery

The word “discovery” appears on screen only inside quotation marks and only as a historical term. Lands already inhabited were reached, invaded, claimed, or entered. The act shows a 19th-century painting of a “discovery” scene and labels it as later imperial art.

### 2. No nostalgia

The series will not use sepia filters, swelling orchestral music, or governor’s-lawn golden hour to make empire beautiful. Railways and hospitals are discussed as infrastructure, with the questions who paid, who built, who benefited, and who died building them.

### 3. No morality contest

The series will not call any empire “the worst” or “the mildest.” It compares institutions: labor law, land policy, violence, and exit. The comparative matrix (see `COMPARATIVE_MATRIX.md`) has no moral ranking column.

### 4. No spectacle, no single cause

No staged massacres, no reconstructed torture or rape, no lingering on bodies, no music that sells horror. Conquest is explained through several causes at once: disease, weapons, alliances, local politics, and the decisions of Indigenous and African rulers. Colonized people are shown surviving, adapting, collaborating, bargaining, and revolting — not only suffering.

### 5. The neighbors

The four sibling projects are named, with their subjects, as separate works. The act states the handoffs the viewer will meet: Episode 21 on the Raj, Episode 62 on India in Asia, Episode 64 on 1947 as a date only, and Episode 68 on Partition as a clause. It ends with the series title card.

## Comparative note

Every refusal here has a counterpart in the honesty rules in the README. This hour turns those rules into screen practice, so the viewer can hold the series to them.

## How we know

This is a method episode. Its sources are the series’ own documents: `README.md`, `PRODUCTION_GUIDE.md`, and `RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md`. It also draws on critiques of imperial nostalgia in media studies and of “guns, germs, and steel” monocausal explanations in the historiography of conquest.

## Do not claim

- That this series is a history of India under British rule; that is *Discovery of India*.
- That this series tells how the Aztec, Inca, or Kongo states began; that belongs elsewhere.
- That colonial infrastructure proves colonialism was beneficial.
- That any single factor — smallpox, steel, horses, or guns — explains conquest.
- That the series is neutral between empire and its victims because it refuses a morality contest; it is honest about violence and compares institutions.
