## Before

Episodes 10–14 covered conquest, labor, and trade. Colonial society also needed cities, law, and belief. By 1600 Mexico City, Lima, and Potosí were among the largest cities in the Americas.

## The turn

Spanish law divided society into a *república de españoles* and a *república de indios*, each with its own courts, towns, and obligations. Reality was mixed. Mestizos, mulatos, free and enslaved Africans, and Asians lived in the cities. Eighteenth-century casta paintings, produced mostly in New Spain, showed sets of 16 scenes, each with a father, a mother, and a child labeled by mixture — “Spaniard and Indian produce mestizo,” and so on. They were ideology, not census. They presented an idealized hierarchy with Spaniards at the top, often for European buyers.

The Church was the largest landowner in many regions, a lender, a censor through the Inquisition, and the keeper of parish registers. It destroyed Indigenous religious objects and books. It also produced some of the most important Indigenous-language records. Nahua and Quechua writers used the Latin alphabet for wills, petitions, annals, and town histories. Churches and cathedrals were built by Indigenous and African laborers, many coerced through the repartimiento and other drafts.

## After

Episode 16 closes the season by showing Iberian power sharing, losing, and anticipating the loss of its American empire.

## Acts

### 1. The grid

Colonial city planning under the Laws of the Indies (1573 ordinances). The plaza, cathedral, and cabildo. Mexico City built over Tenochtitlan. Grids are shown as imposed designs, not as neutral order.

### 2. Two republics and many people

The *república de indios*, Indigenous town councils (*cabildos*), and Indigenous nobles who litigated to defend land and status. Free Black and mulato militias and guilds. The act shows how the legal model broke down in practice.

### 3. The casta paintings

A full set, such as those attributed to Miguel Cabrera (1763), with the labels read aloud. The act explains that these paintings are ideology for elite and European viewers. Real classification in parish registers was inconsistent and could be negotiated.

### 4. The Church as landlord and record

Jesuit haciendas, Church mortgages (*censos*), and the Inquisition’s jurisdiction (Indigenous people were formally exempt after 1571; Africans and others were not). Parish registers as demographic sources. Nahuatl annals and Quechua writing. Guaman Poma’s drawings of priests abusing Indigenous parishioners.

### 5. Who built the Baroque

A cathedral façade in Mexico City, Puebla, or Cusco, and the labor that built it: repartimiento drafts, wage labor, enslaved craftsmen. Indigenous and African artists whose styles shaped the buildings. The act closes on the principle that beauty and coercion can share a wall.

## Comparative note

Portuguese Brazil had no legal “two republics” system but had its own color hierarchies and Black lay brotherhoods (*irmandades*). Episode 48 returns to how these hierarchies outlived independence and turned into national myths of mixture.

## How we know

Colonial legislation (*Recopilación de Leyes de los Reynos de las Indias*, 1680). Casta paintings in museum collections. Parish registers. Inquisition records in the Archivo General de la Nación (Mexico) and the Archivo Histórico Nacional (Madrid). Nahuatl and Quechua documents. Scholarship by Ilona Katzew (*Casta Painting*, 2004), James Lockhart (*The Nahuas After the Conquest*, 1992), and Kenneth Mills on Andean religion.

## Do not claim

- That casta paintings reflect how people actually identified or were counted.
- That the Church was only a tool of oppression, or only a protector.
- That Indigenous people did not write in the colonial period.
- That the two-republic legal model described colonial society accurately.
- That colonial Baroque art was purely European; Indigenous and African artists shaped it.
