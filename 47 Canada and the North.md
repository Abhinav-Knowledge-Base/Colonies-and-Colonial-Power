## Before

Episode 46 followed the United States west. North of it, British North America became a dominion in 1867 (Episode 22) and expanded across the continent through purchase and treaty.

## The turn

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 recognized Indigenous land rights, requiring land to be acquired only by the Crown through treaty. It remains in Canada’s constitution. In 1870 Canada acquired Rupert’s Land from the Hudson’s Bay Company (Episode 17). The Métis of Red River — a people of mixed Indigenous and European descent with their own culture — formed a provisional government under Louis Riel in 1869–70. They negotiated Manitoba’s entry into Canada. The numbered treaties (1871–1921) followed across the prairies and north. The Crown understood them as land surrenders. Many First Nations understood them as agreements to share land in exchange for ongoing support. Oral records of the negotiations and the written texts differ. In 1885 the North-West Resistance, led by Riel and Gabriel Dumont with some Cree allies, was defeated. Riel was hanged for treason, and eight Cree and Assiniboine men were hanged at Battleford. Riel remains contested: a traitor to some then, a founder of Manitoba now, recognized by Parliament in 1992.

The Indian Act (1876) governed First Nations’ lives, status, and land. Residential schools, funded by the federal government and run mostly by churches, operated from the 1880s until the last closed in 1996. More than 150,000 First Nations, Inuit, and Métis children attended. The schools aimed to assimilate them, separated them from families, and suppressed their languages. Abuse and deaths are documented. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015) documented thousands of deaths and said the true number is incomplete. It called the system cultural genocide. The series does not exploit children’s suffering.

## Acts

### 1. The Proclamation and New France’s end

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 and its place in section 25 of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms. French Canadians and Indigenous nations under British rule.

### 2. Red River, 1869–70

The Métis provisional government, Riel, and the Manitoba Act (1870). The execution of Thomas Scott and its political consequences. Métis nationhood.

### 3. The numbered treaties

Treaty texts versus oral histories of the negotiations, for example Treaty 6 (1876) and its “medicine chest” clause. Present-day treaty rights and disputes. Treaty First Nations leaders speaking for their own understanding.

### 4. 1885

The North-West Resistance, Batoche, and Riel’s trial and execution. Big Bear and Poundmaker, Cree chiefs convicted though their roles were contested; the Canadian government exonerated Poundmaker in 2019. Riel as contested memory handled through the documentary record.

### 5. Residential schools

The system’s design, the Indian Act, and the TRC’s findings. Survivors’ testimony used only with consent and from the TRC’s public record. No reconstructions of children’s suffering. Present-day searches of former school grounds are named without speculation. The act closes on living First Nations, Inuit, and Métis governments and languages.

## Comparative note

Australia’s removal of Aboriginal children (the Stolen Generations, documented in *Bringing Them Home*, 1997) and US Indian boarding schools (a federal investigative report was published in 2022) show that assimilation through schooling was a settler-state policy across several countries.

## How we know

Treaty texts and Crown records (Library and Archives Canada). Indigenous oral histories of treaty negotiations. Trial transcripts of 1885. The TRC’s final report (2015) and the National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation archive. Scholarship by J. R. Miller, Sarah Carter, Jean Teillet (*The North-West Is Our Mother*, 2019), and John Milloy.

## Do not claim

- That the numbered treaties were understood the same way by both parties.
- That Riel is simply a traitor or simply a hero.
- That the TRC’s death count is complete; the Commission said it was not.
- That residential schools were a well-intentioned educational policy that went wrong; assimilation was the stated aim.
- That Indigenous nations in Canada are a closed chapter.
