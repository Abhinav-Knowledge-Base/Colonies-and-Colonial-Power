## The turn

On 1 November 1954 the Front de Libération Nationale (FLN) launched coordinated attacks across Algeria. France treated Algeria as national territory and sent hundreds of thousands of conscripts. The war included FLN attacks on civilians and violence against rival nationalists (the Messalists) and suspected collaborators. It also included French mass internment, forced resettlement of some two million rural Algerians into camps (*camps de regroupement*), summary executions, and torture as documented state practice. Torture was exposed at the time by Henri Alleg’s *La Question* (1958), later admitted by General Paul Aussaresses (2001), and acknowledged by President Macron in the case of Maurice Audin (2018) and Ali Boumendjel (2021). The Battle of Algiers (1957) and the 17 October 1961 killing of Algerian demonstrators in Paris are part of the record. De Gaulle, returned to power in 1958, moved toward self-determination. The settler Organisation armée secrète (OAS) waged terror against it. The Évian Accords (March 1962) led to independence on 5 July 1962. About 800,000 to one million settlers left, as did many Algerian Jews. Many harkis, Algerians who had served with French forces, were killed after independence, and those who reached France were interned in camps. Algerian deaths are disputed: the Algerian state’s figure is 1.5 million, and many historians estimate around 300,000–400,000 or more. The figure is labeled as contested.

In Kenya, the rising the British called “Mau Mau” — a name of uncertain origin; fighters often called themselves the Kenya Land and Freedom Army — grew from Kikuyu, Embu, and Meru grievances over land, labor, and settler rule. Britain declared a State of Emergency in October 1952. Fighters killed 32 European settlers and far larger numbers of Africans, including loyalists. British forces and the African Home Guard killed more than 11,000 fighters by official count. Tens of thousands of Kikuyu were detained in camps (official figures are around 80,000 detainees at peak; Caroline Elkins has argued the true number passing through camps was much higher, and this is debated). Around a million people were forced into guarded “villages.” Torture and collective punishment in detention are documented. At Hola camp in March 1959, guards beat 11 detainees to death. Dedan Kimathi was captured and hanged in 1957. In 2011 a High Court case brought by Kenyan survivors (Mutua and others v. Foreign and Commonwealth Office) exposed the migrated archives (Episode 6). In 2013 the British government expressed regret and agreed to pay £19.9 million to 5,228 claimants. Kenya became independent in 1963 under Jomo Kenyatta, whom Britain had imprisoned. Land redistribution after independence favored the well-connected, and the land question in Kenya remains unfinished.

## Acts

### 1. Why settlers made war

A comparison of Algeria and Kenya: settler land, settler political power, and blocked reform. Why these exits were wars.

### 2. Algeria, 1954–62

The FLN, the French army, regroupment, and the documented use of torture — named as state practice, with sources, and never described in method. FLN violence against civilians and rivals. The OAS. 17 October 1961 in Paris.

### 3. Exit and exodus

Évian, independence, settler and Jewish departures, and the fate of the harkis. Contested death tolls with their sources.

### 4. Kenya’s Emergency

Land grievances, the name “Mau Mau” and the fighters’ own names, loyalists and the Home Guard, detention and villagization, and Hola. Figures shown with the debate between official counts and Elkins’s estimates. No battle scenes, no reenacted abuse.

### 5. Reckonings

The 2013 settlement, the migrated archives, and French acknowledgments. Kenyan land after independence. The act closes on unfinished business in both countries.

## Comparative note

Portugal fought colonial wars in Angola, Mozambique, and Guinea-Bissau from 1961 to 1974. Those wars helped bring down the Portuguese dictatorship in the Carnation Revolution (1974), after which the colonies became independent (1974–75). Southern Rhodesia’s settlers declared independence unilaterally in 1965, and a liberation war followed until Zimbabwe’s independence in 1980. The settler exits were the longest and most violent.

## How we know

French military and government archives, partly opened in 2021. The National Archives, Kew, including the FCO 141 migrated archives. Court records from the Mau Mau case. Survivor testimony. Scholarship by Benjamin Stora, Raphaëlle Branche (*La torture et l’armée*, 2001), Todd Shepard (*The Invention of Decolonization*, 2006), David Anderson (*Histories of the Hanged*, 2005), and Caroline Elkins (*Imperial Reckoning*, 2005), with critics of her figures.

## Do not claim

- That Algerian death tolls are settled.
- That torture in Algeria or abuse in Kenya was the work of a few rogue individuals; it was documented state practice.
- That the FLN or Mau Mau fighters committed no violence against civilians.
- That Kenyan detention figures are settled; official and scholarly estimates differ.
- That independence resolved the land question in Kenya.
