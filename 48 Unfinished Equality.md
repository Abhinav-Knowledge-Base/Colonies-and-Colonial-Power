## Before

Episodes 41–47 followed collapse, labor, escape, independence, and expansion. Every state in the Americas was independent by the early 20th century except for remaining European and US territories. Independence did not settle who belonged, who owned land, or who could vote.

## The turn

After independence, liberal reformers in many Latin American states attacked Indigenous communal land as an obstacle to progress. Mexico’s Lerdo Law (1856) forced the sale of corporate lands, including those of Indigenous communities, and fed hacienda expansion. Literacy and property requirements excluded most Indigenous and poor voters for generations. Peru enfranchised non-literate citizens only in 1979. In the United States, Reconstruction amendments promised equal citizenship to Black Americans. Jim Crow, disenfranchisement, and violence denied it until the civil rights movement and the Voting Rights Act (1965). Native Americans became US citizens by statute only in 1924, and some states barred them from voting into the 1940s–60s.

National myths offered unity through mixture. In Mexico, José Vasconcelos’s *La raza cósmica* (1925) celebrated the mestizo as the nation’s future. Post-revolutionary *indigenismo* honored the Indigenous past while seeking to assimilate living Indigenous people. In Brazil, Gilberto Freyre’s *Casa-grande e senzala* (1933) was later popularized as a myth of “racial democracy.” Such myths could erase Black and Indigenous people as distinct groups with specific claims. Afro-Mexicans were recognized in Mexico’s constitution only in 2019 and counted in the 2020 census. Brazil’s Black movement, and affirmative action from the 2000s, challenged the racial democracy myth.

## Acts

### 1. Land after independence

The Lerdo Law, liberal land reforms, and Indigenous resistance. The Mexican Revolution (1910–20) and the *ejido* land reform as a partial answer. Zapata’s Plan de Ayala (1911).

### 2. Who could vote

Literacy and property rules across the hemisphere. The Fifteenth Amendment and its evasion. The Indian Citizenship Act (1924). Peru 1979. The Voting Rights Act (1965). A timeline, not a victory lap.

### 3. The myth of mixture

Vasconcelos and *indigenismo*. Freyre and racial democracy. How celebration of mixture could deny racism and erase Black and Indigenous identities.

### 4. Counting and recognition

Censuses that dropped or added racial categories. Afro-Mexican recognition (2019). Brazil’s affirmative action laws (2012). Indigenous constitutional recognition: Bolivia’s plurinational constitution (2009) and Ecuador’s (2008).

### 5. Not solved

Present-day land defenders, the Zapatista uprising (1994), and Black and Indigenous movements across the Americas. The act closes the season by refusing “solved”: equality after colonialism in the Americas is unfinished.

## Comparative note

Post-independence African and Asian states (Season 9) also inherited colonial hierarchies and faced debates over language, land, and minority rights. The Americas had a head start of a century and did not finish either.

## How we know

Constitutions, land laws, and electoral laws. Censuses. National ideology texts (Vasconcelos, Freyre). Social movement records. Scholarship by Florencia Mallon, Peter Wade (*Race and Ethnicity in Latin America*), George Reid Andrews (*Afro-Latin America*, 2004), and Alexander Keyssar (*The Right to Vote*, 2000).

## Do not claim

- That independence brought equality in the Americas.
- That *mestizaje* or racial democracy describes an absence of racism.
- That Indigenous and Black movements are recent inventions.
- That the United States had full voting rights for all adults before 1965.
- That the problem is solved anywhere in the hemisphere.
