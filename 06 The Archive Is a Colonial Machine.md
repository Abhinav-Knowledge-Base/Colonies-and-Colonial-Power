## Before

The series now has a vocabulary. Before any empire is filmed in detail, the viewer needs to know where the evidence comes from and why it leans.

## The turn

Colonial states wrote constantly. They produced tax rolls, censuses, court records, district officers’ reports, correspondence with the metropole, and maps. These archives are enormous: the Archivo General de Indias in Seville, the India Office Records in the British Library, the VOC archives in The Hague and Jakarta, the Archives nationales d’outre-mer in Aix-en-Provence. They are indispensable. They are also a machine that produced knowledge for ruling. A census category decided who counted as what. A district report described “disturbances” from one side. A missionary dictionary fixed a language’s spelling and sometimes its categories.

Silence in the archive has causes. People who could not write in the ruler’s language, who were enslaved, who were women outside the administration’s interest, or who lived beyond its reach appear rarely, or only as objects. Records were also destroyed on purpose. In the 1950s–60s British officials destroyed or removed files at decolonization (the “migrated archives,” held at Hanslope Park and revealed in 2011).

## After

Episode 7 takes the archive’s numbers — the counts of the dead and the embarked — and shows how historians make and test them.

## Acts

### 1. A district report

A colonial district officer’s annual report (for example, from a British African colony in the 1920s). Tax collected, roads built, “chiefs” appointed, and one line about “unrest.” The act reads it closely and asks who is missing: the people who refused the tax, the women who walked to market, the man called a chief who may not have been one before the report said so.

### 2. A census category

Colonial censuses classified people by caste, tribe, race, or religion in ways that could harden fluid identities. The act uses the Belgian administration’s ethnic identity cards in Rwanda and Burundi (1930s) as a case, stating carefully that colonial classification did not create the categories from nothing but did fix and politicize them. It is not a history of the 1994 genocide. The act names the British Indian census as another example and leaves its regional history to *Discovery of India*.

### 3. A missionary dictionary

Alonso de Molina’s Nahuatl–Spanish dictionary (1555; expanded 1571), or a 19th-century Bantu-language dictionary. Missionaries preserved languages and also reshaped them. Their dictionaries became tools for converts and later for scholars and nationalists who used the written language against colonial rule.

### 4. Writing back

Indigenous and colonized writing inside the archive: Nahuatl notarial records and wills; Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala’s *Nueva corónica y buen gobierno* (c. 1615), an Andean author’s 1,200-page letter to the king of Spain; petitions from enslaved people; letters from Kongo’s King Afonso I (1526). Each is labeled as evidenced, and as written within colonial constraints.

### 5. Other evidence

Oral history and oral tradition, recorded with consultation and labeled as later memory. Archaeology of plantations, maroon settlements, and slave quarters. Material culture, landscapes, and the “migrated archives.” The act closes with the series’ on-screen labels: **evidenced**, **inferred**, **later memory**, **claim by an interested party**.

## Comparative note

Pre-colonial states also kept archives with their own biases: Inca *khipu*, Ethiopian royal chronicles, Qing memorials. The problem is not that colonial archives are uniquely biased. It is that they became the default source for colonized societies that had fewer ways to preserve their own voices under colonial conditions.

## How we know

The archives named above. Ann Laura Stoler, *Along the Archival Grain* (2009). Scholarship on Nahuatl and Quechua writing (the “New Philology” of James Lockhart and others). Oral-history methodology (Jan Vansina, *Oral Tradition as History*, 1985). The migrated archives, FCO 141 at The National Archives, Kew.

## Do not claim

- That colonial archives are useless; they are indispensable and must be read against their grain.
- That colonial classification invented Hutu and Tutsi, or caste; it fixed and politicized existing categories.
- That silence in the archive means absence in the past.
- That oral tradition is either infallible or worthless; it is evidence with its own methods.
- That the “migrated archives” are complete; destruction means some gaps are permanent.
