## Before

Season 6 closed the Americas. Season 7 turns to Africa. Episodes 9 and 33 already showed African states on the Atlantic coast. Before the late-19th-century conquest, most of the continent was governed by Africans. African political history did not begin with Europe, and its deep past is told elsewhere.

## The turn

Asante, centered on Kumasi, rose around 1700 under Osei Tutu. The Golden Stool symbolized its unity. It became a powerful state with a bureaucracy, roads, and control of gold and trade. It fought Britain in a series of wars in the 19th century. Dahomey, with its capital at Abomey, was a centralized kingdom with a standing army that included a women’s regiment (called “Amazons” by Europeans). It was deeply involved in the slave trade and later in palm oil. The Sokoto Caliphate, founded after Usman dan Fodio’s jihad (from 1804), became one of the largest states in 19th-century Africa, a federation of emirates across what is now northern Nigeria and beyond. Ethiopia under Tewodros II (r. 1855–68), Yohannes IV, and Menelik II reunified and expanded. Buganda, on Lake Victoria, had a king (*kabaka*), a council, and chiefs, and received Muslim and Christian missionaries in the 1840s–70s. The Zulu kingdom under Shaka (c. 1816–28) and his successors became a military power and defeated a British army at Isandlwana in 1879. Kongo continued as a kingdom with reduced central power through the 18th and 19th centuries.

These states made decisions: to trade, fight, convert, ally, and modernize armies. Their later defeat was not inevitable, and their earlier power was not a prelude.

## Acts

### 1. Asante

Osei Tutu, Okomfo Anokye, and the Golden Stool. Kumasi’s bureaucracy and roads. The Anglo-Asante wars (1824–1900) as wars between states.

### 2. Dahomey

Abomey’s royal palaces (UNESCO), the army and its women soldiers, the annual customs, and the slave trade and its shift to palm oil. Dahomey is filmed as a state with its own politics, including its role in slaving.

### 3. Sokoto

Usman dan Fodio, the jihad, the caliphate’s structure, and its scholarly tradition. Nana Asma’u, dan Fodio’s daughter, as a poet and educator whose works survive.

### 4. Ethiopia and Buganda

Tewodros’s modernizing state and the British expedition to Maqdala (1868), with its looted manuscripts and objects (a restitution case). Buganda’s court and its politics of religious factions.

### 5. The Zulu kingdom and Kongo

Shaka’s state, its expansion, and the *mfecane* debate (whether Zulu expansion caused regional upheaval, or whether that story served settler narratives). Kongo’s later centuries. The act closes with a map of African states in 1870, drawn without European claims.

## Comparative note

In the same decades, Japan modernized its state and army and avoided colonization. Ethiopia also modernized its army and defeated Italy at Adwa (Episode 53). Asante, Dahomey, and the Zulu kingdom fought well and lost. Comparing outcomes requires attention to timing, geography, and European priorities, not to cultural ranking.

## How we know

African chronicles and Arabic-script writings (Sokoto’s manuscripts). Oral traditions of Asante, Dahomey, and Buganda, labeled as later memory where applicable. European traders’ and travelers’ accounts (claims). Archaeology of capitals. Scholarship by Ivor Wilks (*Asante in the Nineteenth Century*, 1975), Robin Law, Murray Last (*The Sokoto Caliphate*, 1967), Richard Reid, and Carolyn Hamilton on the *mfecane* debate.

## Do not claim

- That Africa was stateless or waiting before European conquest.
- That African states were peaceful; they fought and some enslaved.
- That the fall of these states was inevitable.
- That the Dahomey women soldiers were a legend; they are documented, though European descriptions exaggerated.
- That ancient Egypt or Great Zimbabwe is this hour’s subject; they are other centuries and other projects.
