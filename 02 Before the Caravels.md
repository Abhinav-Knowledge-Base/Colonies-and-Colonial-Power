## Before

Maps of “the Age of Discovery” often show empty coasts waiting for sails. Voiceovers speak of “unknown lands.” They were unknown to Lisbon and Seville. They were not unknown to the people who lived there, traded there, and fought over them.

## The turn

In 1400 the largest cities in the world were not in western Europe. The Triple Alliance of Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan took tribute across central Mexico. Tawantinsuyu, the Inca state, had expanded along the Andes only in the previous century. Kongo was a kingdom with a capital at Mbanza Kongo and provinces paying tribute. Kilwa, Mombasa, and Malindi were Muslim, Swahili-speaking port towns trading gold and ivory to Arabia, Persia, and India. Ming fleets under Zheng He sailed to the Swahili coast between 1405 and 1433 and then stopped by imperial decision. Gujarati, Tamil, Malay, Arab, and Chinese merchants ran an Indian Ocean system based on monsoon winds. When the Portuguese arrived, they had to pay, fight, or bargain their way into it.

The hour is deliberately short on origins. How these societies formed belongs to *Cradles of Civilization* and other projects. Here they are societies already in history, with their own politics, when European ships arrived.

## Acts

### 1. A map without Europe at the center

A graphic of 15th-century trade routes: monsoon lanes across the Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan caravan routes, Andean roads (*qhapaq ñan*), Mesoamerican tribute and market networks, and the Silk Road overland routes. No modern national borders are drawn. Polities are marked by centers and zones of influence, and edges are shaded where they are uncertain.

### 2. Tenochtitlan and Cusco

Two imperial capitals in the Americas at the end of the 15th century. The Triple Alliance ruled through tribute and had enemies such as Tlaxcala, which never submitted. The Inca state ruled through labor obligations recorded on *khipu*. Both were recent and contested empires, and that matters for Episodes 11 and 12. No retelling of their origins.

### 3. Kongo and the West African coast

Kongo as a centralized kingdom with a shell currency (*nzimbu*). The Akan goldfields supplying trans-Saharan trade. Benin City’s walls and court. Each is shown as a partner, rival, or power that European traders had to deal with.

### 4. The Swahili coast and the Indian Ocean

Kilwa’s Great Mosque and Husuni Kubwa palace ruins, Chinese porcelain set into coral-stone tombs, and Gujarati textiles in East African trade. The Indian Ocean already had merchants, credit, and shipping laws. Vasco da Gama’s 1498 voyage relied on local knowledge of the route from Malindi to Calicut; the identity of his pilot is uncertain and the popular attribution to Ahmad ibn Majid is disputed. That route led into an Indian Ocean system already there. It did not open one.

### 5. Ming and the choice not to sail

Zheng He’s voyages (1405–1433) as proof that oceanic capacity was not uniquely European. They also show that states chose different strategies. The Ming court ended the voyages for reasons historians debate. The act refuses the “what if China had colonized” game.

## Comparative note

Every one of these states had conquered others. Tawantinsuyu resettled whole populations (*mitmaqkuna*). Kilwa’s sultans taxed trade. Showing them is not a claim that the world before 1492 was peaceful. It is a claim that it was full of history.

## How we know

Archaeology of Kilwa, Mbanza Kongo, Tenochtitlan (Templo Mayor project), and Cusco. Chinese records of Zheng He’s voyages and Ma Huan’s *Yingya Shenglan*. Arabic geographical and navigational texts. Early Portuguese accounts, which are claims by newcomers. Indigenous-language and colonial-era records of the Mexica and Inca, most written after conquest (see Episode 6).

## Do not claim

- That these societies were “discovered,” or were waiting for contact.
- That their origins are the subject here; they are told elsewhere.
- That a single Arab pilot “showed da Gama the way to India”; the pilot’s identity is uncertain.
- That Zheng He reached the Americas.
- That Kongo, Kilwa, or Tenochtitlan had modern national borders.
