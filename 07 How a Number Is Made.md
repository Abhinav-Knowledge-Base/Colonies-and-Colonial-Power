## Before

Numbers of the dead circulate as slogans: “95 percent,” “10 million,” “100 million.” Some are careful estimates stripped of their ranges. Some are guesses repeated until they look like facts. Some are minimized to deny that anything happened.

## The turn

Historians build numbers in three main ways. They count surviving records, such as ship manifests, tax rolls, and parish registers. They estimate what is missing, such as voyages without records or villages outside the census. They model change over time, for example by projecting population from later counts and assumed rates of decline. Every step carries assumptions. Honest numbers therefore come with ranges and with an account of how they were made.

Three cases run through the series. The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (Slave Voyages) documents more than 36,000 voyages. From these and estimates for missing voyages, scholars estimate that on the order of 12.5 million Africans were embarked and about 10.7 million disembarked in the Americas — estimates, revised as research continues. Pre-contact population of the Americas has been estimated from under 10 million to over 100 million. Many recent syntheses cluster around several tens of millions, but that is a modeled figure. Deaths in the Congo Free State are not recorded in any census. Estimates rest on later population figures and assumed baselines, and they range widely (Episode 29).

## After

Episode 8 lists what the series will not do, including the numbers it will not pretend to know.

## Acts

### 1. A ship’s manifest

A single voyage record in the Slave Voyages database: ship name, flag, captain, ports, number embarked, number disembarked. The act shows how the record was found (British, Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch, and other archives) and then how many voyages have no surviving record. The database’s own estimates page is shown with its methods.

### 2. From voyages to a total

How the database team estimates missing voyages by region and period. The difference between embarked and disembarked is shipboard mortality, and itself an estimate. The act states the series’ rule: the Atlantic trade’s scale is always shown as an estimate with its source, and never as a more precise theater number.

### 3. The Americas before and after

Henry Dobyns’s high estimates (1966), David Henige’s critique (*Numbers from Nowhere*, 1998), and later syntheses. The act shows why the baseline matters: a percentage of collapse depends on the starting number. It states plainly that demographic collapse was real and catastrophic, and that the numbers are modeled.

### 4. The Congo problem

No census before 1924. Estimates of population loss under Leopold’s regime rest on backward projection, missionary reports, and assumptions about birth rates and famine. The figure of about 10 million, popularized by Adam Hochschild (1998) from earlier estimates, is shown with its sources and critics. Some demographers argue for lower or unquantifiable figures. The act refuses both locking one number as fact and dismissing the catastrophe.

### 5. The rule for the rest of the series

On-screen conventions: every toll carries a range, a source, and a label (**recorded**, **estimated**, **modeled**, **contested**). Where no defensible number exists, the caption reads “unknown.” The act closes by listing tolls that will appear later — Banda 1621, Herero and Nama, the Bengal famine, the Philippine-American War, the Algerian war — each with its own debate.

## Comparative note

The same method applies to famines (Bengal 1943, Vietnam 1944–45), wars (Algeria 1954–62), and camps (South Africa 1899–1902, Kenya 1950s). Different evidence produces different kinds of uncertainty. A war with military records is not the same problem as a regime that kept no count of deaths.

## How we know

The Slave Voyages database and its methodology essays (David Eltis, David Richardson, and collaborators). Historical demography of the Americas (Cook and Borah; Dobyns; Henige; later syntheses). Scholarship on the Congo (Jan Vansina; Jean-Luc Vellut; Hochschild; critics). Famine demography (for the method, not the politics).

## Do not claim

- That the Atlantic slave trade moved exactly 12,521,337 people, or any precise figure, as a known count.
- That pre-contact American population is known; it is modeled.
- That the Congo Free State killed exactly 10 million people, or that the absence of a census means the catastrophe did not happen.
- That a percentage (“90 percent died”) is meaningful without a baseline.
- That low estimates are denial or high estimates are propaganda by default; each is judged on method.
