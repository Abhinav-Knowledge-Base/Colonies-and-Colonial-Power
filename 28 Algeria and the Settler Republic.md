## Before

Episode 27 introduced France’s second empire. It began with an invasion of the Regency of Algiers, an autonomous province of the Ottoman empire, in 1830.

## The turn

The French invasion of 1830 was followed by a long war of conquest. The emir Abd el-Kader built a state in western Algeria and fought until his surrender in 1847. Conquest in Kabylia continued into the 1850s, and a great revolt in 1871 was crushed. French armies used scorched-earth tactics, destroyed crops and villages, and massacred populations. The *enfumades* of 1844–45, in which troops asphyxiated people sheltering in caves, are documented by the officers themselves. Deaths from war, famine, and epidemic in the conquest decades were very large. Estimates vary widely and stay labeled as estimates.

Land was seized through confiscation, sequestration after revolts, and laws that broke up collective tribal land (the *sénatus-consulte* of 1863 and the Warnier Law of 1873). European settlers from France, Spain, Italy, and Malta received it. In 1848 the Second Republic declared Algeria an integral part of France, divided into departments. Yet Muslim Algerians were French subjects, not citizens. The *sénatus-consulte* of 1865 allowed them to apply for citizenship only by renouncing Muslim personal status, and few did. The Crémieux Decree (1870) made Algerian Jews citizens. The *code de l’indigénat* (1881) imposed summary penalties on Muslim Algerians. “Algeria is France” was a legal claim that excluded the majority.

## Acts

### 1. 1830

The invasion, its pretexts (the “fly-whisk incident” of 1827 and debts), and domestic French politics. The Regency of Algiers as an Ottoman province with its own rulers, not an empty coast.

### 2. Abd el-Kader

The emir’s state, army, and diplomacy, his 15-year war, his surrender, and his later life. He is filmed as a political and religious leader, not a romantic desert figure.

### 3. Conquest as war on society

Scorched earth and razzias, documented in officers’ own letters. The *enfumades* named once, without methods or imagery. Estimated demographic losses labeled as estimates with their sources.

### 4. Land

Confiscation, the 1863 and 1873 laws, and colonization villages. Settlers of many European origins. The act maps land transfer as drawn by colonial surveyors and labels it as their map.

### 5. The legal lie

Departments, citizenship rules, the Crémieux Decree, and the *indigénat*. Algerian political voices before 1945: the Young Algerians and Émir Khaled (1920s), and Messali Hadj’s Étoile Nord-Africaine (1926; Episode 66). The act ends with a title card: the war of independence, 1954–62, is Episode 69. Torture in that war was documented state practice and is treated there.

## Comparative note

Settler colonies in southern Africa (Episode 54) and Kenya also combined land seizure with legal exclusion. Algeria was distinctive because France claimed it as national territory, which shaped the violence of its end.

## How we know

French military correspondence and parliamentary debates. ANOM holdings for Algeria. Abd el-Kader’s writings and correspondence. Land and census records. Scholarship by Benjamin Stora, Charles-Robert Ageron, Benjamin Brower (*A Desert Named Peace*, 2009), and James McDougall (*A History of Algeria*, 2017).

## Do not claim

- That Algeria was legally or actually “France” for most Algerians.
- That conquest deaths are known precisely.
- That settlers were all French.
- That Abd el-Kader was only a warrior; he was a statesman and scholar.
- That the war of independence is narrated here; it is Episode 69.
