## Before

Episode 22 showed an empire of different rules. At the turn of the century that empire went to war in South Africa and then twice in Europe and beyond. Each war depended on colonial manpower and money.

## The turn

The South African War (1899–1902) pitted Britain against the Boer republics of the Transvaal and the Orange Free State. To defeat Boer guerrillas, British forces burned farms and moved Boer women and children and Black Africans into concentration camps. An estimated 26,000–28,000 Boer internees, most of them children, died of disease and neglect. Separate camps for Black Africans recorded at least 14,000 deaths, and scholars think the true figure was higher. These are estimates from camp records and later research. Emily Hobhouse’s reports exposed conditions in the Boer camps. The Black camps received far less attention at the time and afterward.

In the First World War, the empire mobilized soldiers and laborers from India, Africa, the Caribbean, and the dominions. More than a million Indian soldiers served overseas. Hundreds of thousands of African carriers served, and many died, in the East African campaign. In April 1919, at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer fired on an unarmed crowd. The official Hunter Committee recorded 379 dead. Indian inquiries put the figure much higher, around 1,000. The series names the massacre once, with both figures, as an event in the history of imperial violence. It does not tell the Indian freedom movement, which belongs to *Discovery of India*. In the Second World War, the Indian Army grew to about 2.5 million men, the largest volunteer army in history. African, Caribbean, and other colonial forces fought across the world. The metropole ended the war in debt to its colonies.

## After

Episode 24 asks what the empire extracted and what decolonization did not return, and refuses nostalgia as a genre.

## Acts

### 1. The South African War and the camps

Farm-burning and the camp system. Boer and Black African camps, the death figures as estimates with their sources, and Emily Hobhouse. No imagery of dying children. The camps are filmed through records and empty sites.

### 2. Colonial soldiers, 1914–18

Indian troops in France and Mesopotamia, the King’s African Rifles, the British West Indies Regiment and its mutiny at Taranto (1918), and the carrier corps in East Africa. Soldiers and laborers as actors with their own letters, grievances, and demands.

### 3. Amritsar, 1919, once

Jallianwala Bagh named with the Hunter Committee figure and higher Indian estimates. Dyer’s order, the Hunter Committee, and the controversy in Britain. No reconstruction of the shooting. The act ends with a handoff: its place in Indian political history belongs to *Discovery of India*.

### 4. The Second World War’s empire

The Indian Army, African and Caribbean troops, colonial labor, and requisitioning. Dependence on colonial manpower and money, and the sterling balances accumulated by colonies (Episode 24). Episode 67 treats the war as a hinge of decolonization.

### 5. The center strains

Imperial debt, US pressure, and colonial demands for self-government. The act closes by showing how war exposed the empire’s reliance on the people it ruled.

## Comparative note

France relied on *tirailleurs sénégalais* and North African troops in both wars. In December 1944 French forces killed West African soldiers who protested unpaid wages at Thiaroye, Senegal (Episode 67). Colonial soldiering across empires produced service, claims, and anger.

## How we know

War Office and Colonial Office records. Camp registers from the South African War. The Hunter Committee report (1920) and the Indian National Congress inquiry. Soldiers’ letters (censored Indian soldiers’ letters from France, preserved in the British Library). Scholarship by Elizabeth van Heyningen on the camps, Santanu Das on colonial soldiers, and David Killingray on African troops.

## Do not claim

- That the South African War camps were extermination camps; they were concentration camps with catastrophic mortality from disease and neglect.
- That camp death tolls are exact.
- That the Amritsar death toll is settled; official and Indian estimates differ.
- That this episode tells the Indian national movement; it belongs to *Discovery of India*.
- That colonial soldiers were only victims or only loyal subjects.
