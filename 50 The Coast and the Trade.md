## Before

Episode 49 showed African states before the scramble. Episode 33 covered capture and the coast. Overlap with Season 5 is acknowledged. This hour covers what came after Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807.

## The turn

After 1807, Britain and later other powers campaigned against the Atlantic slave trade, which continued illegally for decades to Brazil and Cuba. British and European merchants promoted “legitimate commerce”: palm oil for soap and machine lubricants, groundnuts for oil, ivory, and gum. The Niger Delta’s city-states — Bonny, New Calabar, and Opobo — became centers of the palm-oil trade, run by African merchant houses such as that of Jaja of Opobo, a formerly enslaved man who built his own trading state. In Senegambia, groundnut farming expanded.

Legitimate commerce did not end coercion. Palm oil and groundnuts required labor, and in many regions African producers used enslaved people to meet export demand. Historians such as Paul Lovejoy argue that slavery within West Africa expanded in the 19th century, partly because of this trade. At the same time, the Royal Navy’s West Africa Squadron intercepted slave ships and landed “liberated Africans” at Freetown, Sierra Leone. Many were settled there or compelled into apprenticeship. European powers began to claim that suppressing slavery required colonial rule, a justification later used in the scramble (Episodes 51–52). When African merchants like Jaja resisted European control of trade, they were removed. Jaja was tricked aboard a British ship and exiled in 1887.

## Acts

### 1. After 1807

The illegal trade, the West Africa Squadron, and the courts of mixed commission at Freetown. Liberated Africans and their fate. Freetown’s Krio society.

### 2. Palm oil

Niger Delta city-states, canoe houses, and merchant princes. Jaja of Opobo’s rise from slavery to merchant king. Palm oil’s uses in industrial Britain.

### 3. Groundnuts and other goods

Senegambian groundnuts, and migrant farmers (*navetanes*). Ivory from East and Central Africa. Commodity trades as African enterprises.

### 4. Coercion continues

Slavery within Africa expanding to produce exports: Lovejoy’s argument and its critics. Enslaved producers in Dahomey’s palm plantations and Sokoto’s agricultural estates. The Indian Ocean and East African slave trades (Zanzibar’s clove plantations) named as a different system, and not used as a diversion.

### 5. From anti-slavery to empire

Anti-slavery as a reason for intervention. Lagos annexed in 1861 partly on these grounds. Jaja’s exile in 1887. The act hands off to Episode 51.

## Comparative note

In the Americas, abolition was followed by indenture and other coercion (Episode 40). In Africa, abolition of the Atlantic trade was followed by expanded internal slavery and, later, colonial forced labor. In both cases, the end of one labor regime created new coercion.

## How we know

Royal Navy and mixed commission court records. Consular reports from the Bights of Benin and Biafra. African merchants’ correspondence. Freetown’s liberated African registers. Scholarship by Paul Lovejoy, Robin Law (ed., *From Slave Trade to “Legitimate” Commerce*, 1995), Martin Lynn, and Richard Anderson on liberated Africans.

## Do not claim

- That the end of the British slave trade ended slavery in Africa.
- That “legitimate commerce” was free of coercion.
- That African merchants were passive intermediaries; they built trading states.
- That anti-slavery motives made colonial conquest humanitarian.
- That the East African slave trade diminishes the Atlantic one; they were different systems.
