## Before

Episode 42 mapped labor inside colonial society. Some people lived outside it: escaped slaves who built their own communities, and Indigenous nations that colonial states could not conquer.

## The turn

Maroon communities formed wherever slavery existed. In Jamaica, Maroons who had escaped Spanish and then British slavery fought the British for decades. They signed treaties in 1739 (Cudjoe, for the Leeward Maroons) and 1740 (for the Windward Maroons), which recognized their freedom and land. In exchange, the Maroons agreed to return future runaways and help suppress revolts. Nanny of the Maroons led the Windward Maroons and is a National Hero of Jamaica, though much about her is known through later memory. In Suriname the Ndyuka (1760) and Saramaka (1762) won treaties from the Dutch and remain distinct peoples today. Palmares in Brazil (Episode 14) lasted until 1694. San Basilio de Palenque in Colombia won royal recognition in the early 18th century and survives with its own creole language.

On the borderlands, colonial states were weak. The Comanche built a powerful horse-based domain on the southern Plains in the 18th and early 19th centuries, raiding, trading, and dominating Spanish and Mexican settlements. Pekka Hämäläinen has called it the “Comanche Empire,” an argument that has been debated. The Apache resisted Spanish, Mexican, and US power for centuries. In the south, the Mapuche held the Araucanía against Spain. At parliaments (*parlamentos*) such as Quilín (1641) the Spanish Crown recognized a frontier on the Biobío River. Chile occupied the Araucanía in 1861–1883, and Argentina conducted the “Conquest of the Desert” (1878–1885).

## After

Episode 44 turns to creole independence in Spanish America.

## Acts

### 1. Jamaica’s Maroons

The First Maroon War, the 1739 and 1740 treaties, and their terms, including the return of runaways. The Second Maroon War (1795–96) and the deportation of Trelawny Town Maroons to Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone. Nanny as history and memory.

### 2. Suriname and Palenque

Ndyuka and Saramaka treaties, and their communities today, including the Saramaka case at the Inter-American Court of Human Rights (2007). San Basilio de Palenque and its language (UNESCO intangible heritage).

### 3. Agency without romance

Maroon treaties required returning runaways. Maroon communities had internal hierarchies and sometimes held captives. The act refuses both romance and dismissal.

### 4. The Comanche and Apache

Comanche power, trade, and raiding. The Hämäläinen debate. Apache resistance across three states. Borderlands where colonial power was limited.

### 5. Araucanía

Mapuche resistance to Spain, the Quilín parliament, and the Biobío frontier. Chile’s and Argentina’s later wars of conquest. Mapuche land claims today. The act closes by showing that colonial maps claimed spaces colonial states did not control.

## Comparative note

In Africa, colonial states also had limits until the late 19th century (Episode 49). In both cases maps drew claims over spaces run by others.

## How we know

Treaty texts (Jamaica Archives; Nationaal Archief for Suriname). Colonial military records. Maroon oral traditions, with consent. Spanish and Mexican frontier records. Mapuche parliamentary records. Scholarship by Richard Price (*Maroon Societies*, 1973; *First-Time*, 1983), Kenneth Bilby, Pekka Hämäläinen (*The Comanche Empire*, 2008), Brian DeLay, and José Bengoa on the Mapuche.

## Do not claim

- That maroon communities were utopias.
- That Maroon treaties were purely victories; they had costly terms.
- That the Comanche “empire” is an uncontested label.
- That the Mapuche were conquered by Spain.
- That maroon and Indigenous resistance ended in the colonial period.
