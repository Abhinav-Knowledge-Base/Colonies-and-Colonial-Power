## Before

Episode 34 followed the crossing. Survivors were sold at ports in Brazil, the Caribbean, and North America, and most were taken to plantations.

## The turn

Sugar was the plantation’s first great crop. Cane had to be cut and ground quickly, so mills ran day and night during harvest. Planters organized enslaved workers into gangs by strength and age and drove them with overseers and drivers, some of them enslaved. Mortality on Caribbean sugar plantations was so high that enslaved populations declined without continual imports. Coffee, indigo, rice, and tobacco each had their own labor regimes; Carolina rice planters used a task system.

After the 1790s, cotton transformed slavery in the United States. The cotton gin (1793) and demand from British and New England mills drove slavery westward into the Deep South. About a million enslaved people were forcibly moved from the upper South to the lower South in the domestic slave trade between 1790 and 1860. That figure is an estimate. The US enslaved population grew by natural increase, unlike the Caribbean’s.

The legal regimes differed. Spanish law, drawing on the medieval *Siete Partidas*, allowed enslaved people to buy their freedom (*coartación*) and to seek a new owner. Portuguese Brazil had frequent manumission and a large free Black population, alongside extreme violence. France had the Code Noir (Episode 27). British colonies had no single code and relied on colonial statutes such as Barbados’s 1661 act. The Dutch in Suriname were notorious for harsh treatment and large maroon communities. US states had their own slave codes. These differences mattered to people’s lives. They did not make any regime benign, and the series does not rank them.

## Acts

### 1. Sugar

Cane, mill, and boiling house on a Caribbean estate, from estate plans and archaeology. The gang system and the harvest’s night shifts. The planter’s account book as a document of lives reduced to labor units.

### 2. The clock and the mill

Time discipline on plantations — bells, shifts, and quotas — as a precursor of industrial labor organization. The debate over whether plantations were “factories in the field.”

### 3. Cotton

The US cotton kingdom, the domestic slave trade, and the pushing system of rising quotas documented in slave narratives and plantation records. Edward Baptist’s argument that violence increased productivity, and the economists’ critique of his figures, presented as a debate.

### 4. Laws compared

One act on legal differences: *coartación* in Cuba, manumission patterns in Brazil, the Code Noir, British colonial statutes, and US state codes. A comparison table on screen, with no ranking column.

### 5. Owners, markets, and resistance

Merchants, banks, insurers, and textile mills tied to plantations. Resistance on the plantation: slowdown, sabotage, flight, and revolt. The act hands revolt forward to Episodes 38 and 39.

## Comparative note

After emancipation, plantation economies turned to indentured labor from India, China, and Java (Episode 40), and to sharecropping. The plantation outlived slavery as an institution.

## How we know

Plantation records and inventories. Colonial and state legal codes. Slave narratives, including WPA interviews (1930s), labeled as later memory. Archaeology of plantation sites. Scholarship by Richard Dunn, Sidney Mintz (*Sweetness and Power*, 1985), Frank Tannenbaum’s comparative thesis (1946) and its critics, Edward Baptist, and Alan Olmstead and Paul Rhode.

## Do not claim

- That Spanish or Portuguese slavery was milder because of legal provisions; law and practice differed.
- That the domestic slave trade figure is exact.
- That Baptist’s productivity figures are uncontested.
- That plantations were only rural; they were tied to banks, insurers, and mills.
- That enslaved people simply obeyed; resistance was constant.
