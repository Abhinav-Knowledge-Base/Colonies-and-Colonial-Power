## Before

Episode 20 showed the United States born from a settler rebellion that kept slavery. Episode 45 showed Brazil’s exit. The United States spent its first century expanding across the continent.

## The turn

The Louisiana Purchase (1803) bought France’s claim — not the land of the Indigenous nations who lived there — to about 2.1 million square kilometers. France sold it after its defeat in Haiti (Episode 38). The Indian Removal Act (1830), signed by Andrew Jackson, authorized the president to negotiate the removal of Indigenous nations east of the Mississippi. In *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) the Supreme Court recognized Cherokee sovereignty against Georgia; the federal government did not enforce the decision. The Cherokee were forcibly removed in 1838–39. Estimates of Cherokee deaths during removal and in the camps before it are around 4,000, with estimates ranging from about 2,000 to over 6,000. The Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee (Creek), and Seminole were also removed, and the Seminole fought long wars against removal. The series names the law and the deaths as forced migration, and does not stage a death march.

The Mexican-American War (1846–48) ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. Mexico ceded about half its territory, including California and the Southwest. Mexicans and Indigenous peoples in the ceded lands were promised rights that were often ignored. Western expansion brought the California genocide (the state’s own governor called for extermination in 1851, and Governor Gavin Newsom apologized in 2019), wars on the Plains, reservation confinement, and the Dawes Act (1887), which broke up communal reservation land. The Wounded Knee massacre (1890) is named as a documented killing by the US Army. In 1898 the United States took overseas colonies (Episode 32).

## After

Episode 47 turns north to Canada.

## Acts

### 1. Louisiana

The purchase, what was sold (a European claim), and the Indigenous nations whose land it was. Haiti’s role in France’s decision.

### 2. Removal

The Indian Removal Act read as text. The Cherokee Nation’s legal fight, *Worcester v. Georgia*, and the Treaty of New Echota (1835), signed by a minority faction. The forced migration with estimated deaths. Removal of the other nations and the Seminole wars. Enslaved Black people held by some removed nations are named, as part of the record.

### 3. Taking half of Mexico

The war’s causes and the dissent at the time (Lincoln’s “spot resolutions,” Thoreau). The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo and its broken promises to Mexican citizens.

### 4. The West

California, the Plains wars, reservations, and the Dawes Act. Wounded Knee named from Army and Lakota testimony. Indigenous nations’ strategies of war, diplomacy, and survival.

### 5. Postcolonial and colonial

1898 as the overseas turn (Episode 32). The act closes on the formulation: the United States was a postcolonial state and a colonial power, both. Indigenous nations continue as sovereign nations within the United States.

## Comparative note

Russia’s land expansion (Episode 31) and Canada’s westward treaties (Episode 47) are the nearest comparisons. Each combined settlers, state power, and treaties with Indigenous nations whose terms were broken or contested.

## How we know

Federal statutes, treaties, and court decisions. Cherokee Nation records and the *Cherokee Phoenix* newspaper. Army records and Lakota testimony. Scholarship by Claudio Saunt (*Unworthy Republic*, 2020), Benjamin Madley (*An American Genocide*, 2016), Brian DeLay (*War of a Thousand Deserts*, 2008), and Ned Blackhawk (*The Rediscovery of America*, 2023).

## Do not claim

- That the Louisiana Purchase bought land from its Indigenous owners.
- That the Trail of Tears death toll is precisely known.
- That removal was voluntary; the Treaty of New Echota was signed by a minority faction and removal was enforced by troops.
- That the United States was not a colonial power because it had been a colony.
- That Indigenous nations are part of the past; they are sovereign nations today.
