## Before

Season 1 built the tools. Iberian monarchs now had bulls, treaties, ships, and royal trading houses. The first places those tools were used were not in the Americas. They were islands in the eastern Atlantic and the coast of West and Central Africa.

## The turn

The Canary Islands were inhabited by Indigenous peoples, often grouped under the name Guanches (strictly, the people of Tenerife). Castilian conquest took almost the whole 15th century, from 1402 to the fall of Tenerife in 1496, with enslavement, disease, and resistance. Madeira was uninhabited when Portuguese settlers arrived in the 1420s. By the late 1400s it was a sugar island, worked partly by enslaved people from the Canaries and Africa. São Tomé, settled from the 1480s–90s, became a model plantation colony with enslaved African labor on a large scale. It was also a transit point for the early slave trade.

On the mainland, the Portuguese built São Jorge da Mina (Elmina) in 1482 on the Gold Coast, with permission bargained from local Akan and Fante authorities. It was a trading fort for gold, not a conquest. Further south, Kongo’s ruler Nzinga a Nkuwu accepted baptism in 1491 as João I. His son Afonso I (r. c. 1509–1542) built a Christian Kongo state, sent envoys to Lisbon and Rome, and wrote letters in Portuguese. In 1526 he complained to João III that Portuguese traders were enslaving free Kongolese, including nobles. Kongo was a state that converted and negotiated, not an empty coast.

## Acts

### 1. The Canaries

Castilian conquest, Indigenous resistance on Gran Canaria and Tenerife, enslavement of islanders sold in Seville and Valencia, and the survival of Guanche heritage in later Canarian population and memory. Mummified remains held in museums are named as an ethics issue in one sentence. They are not shown.

### 2. Madeira and the sugar model

Sugar mills (*engenhos*), irrigation channels (*levadas*), and the export of sugar to Flanders and Genoa. Labor was mixed: free, enslaved, and indentured. The act states that Madeira was a rehearsal of plantation sugar, not yet the full Atlantic system.

### 3. São Tomé

A plantation island with thousands of enslaved Africans by the mid-1500s. It had frequent flight and revolt, including the revolt led by Amador in 1595. The act points forward to Season 5 and does not start the Middle Passage here.

### 4. Elmina

The castle built in 1482, the gold trade, and the permission of local rulers. Later, the Dutch captured it in 1637 and it became a slave-trading fort. That later history is handed forward to Episode 33.

### 5. Kongo as a state

Afonso I’s letters, the Christian Kongo court, and the balance of alliance and complaint with Portugal. Kongo is shown as a power making decisions under pressure. The act closes on the Atlantic triangle already visible: islands, forts, and a kingdom whose people were being drawn into a trade it could not fully control.

## Comparative note

The Mediterranean already had sugar grown with coerced labor, in Cyprus, Crete, and Sicily. The Atlantic islands moved that model westward and changed its labor source toward sub-Saharan Africa. The model then crossed to the Americas.

## How we know

Castilian and Portuguese chronicles (Zurara on the Portuguese in Africa, a claim-heavy royal chronicler). Notarial records of slave sales in Seville and Valencia. Archaeology of Madeiran and Canarian sites. Afonso I’s letters in Portuguese archives (Torre do Tombo). Studies by John Thornton on Kongo and Linda Heywood on Central Africa.

## Do not claim

- That the Canaries were empty or easily taken; conquest took most of the 15th century.
- That Kongo’s conversion was a Portuguese trick with no Kongolese agency.
- That Elmina was a slave fort from 1482; its early trade was gold.
- That São Tomé’s plantation was identical to later Caribbean or Brazilian ones.
- That the Atlantic slave trade began in full here; this is its rehearsal and handoff.
