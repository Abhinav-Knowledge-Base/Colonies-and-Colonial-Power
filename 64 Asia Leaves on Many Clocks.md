## The turn

On 17 August 1945 Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta proclaimed Indonesia’s independence. The Dutch returned and fought to reimpose control. Two large military campaigns, called “police actions” (*politionele acties*), came in 1947 and 1948, and there was a revolution with violence on many sides, including the *bersiap* period of violence against Dutch, Eurasian, Chinese, and others in 1945–46. Dutch troops committed mass killings, including at Rawagede (1947). The Netherlands transferred sovereignty in December 1949 under US pressure, keeping West New Guinea until 1962. A 2022 Dutch-funded research program concluded that Dutch forces had used extreme violence systematically, and the Dutch prime minister apologized.

In Vietnam, Hồ Chí Minh declared the Democratic Republic of Vietnam on 2 September 1945. France returned, and war began in 1946. It ended with the French defeat at Điện Biên Phủ in 1954 and the Geneva division of Vietnam, and led into a longer war. The Philippines became independent on 4 July 1946. India and Pakistan became independent in August 1947. This series notes the date and hands on: the history of independence and Partition in South Asia belongs to *Discovery of India*. Burma became independent on 4 January 1948, outside the Commonwealth. Ceylon (Sri Lanka) became a dominion on 4 February 1948. In Malaya, Britain fought a communist insurgency (the Malayan Emergency, 1948–60), using mass resettlement of Chinese villagers into “New Villages,” and granted independence in 1957. Singapore followed in stages (1959, 1963, 1965). Portugal held Goa until India took it in 1961 and Macao until 1999. Britain held Hong Kong until 1997.

## Acts

### 1. Indonesia, 1945–49

Proclamation, revolution, *bersiap*, the police actions, Rawagede, and the transfer of sovereignty. The 2022 research findings and apology. Violence on many sides, stated without imagery.

### 2. Vietnam, 1945–54

The Declaration of Independence (quoting the US Declaration), the war with France, Điện Biên Phủ, and Geneva. The act ends before the American war, which is a Cold War history this series does not narrate.

### 3. 1946–48: many dates

The Philippines, Burma, and Ceylon. A single card for India and Pakistan in 1947: the date and the handoff to *Discovery of India*. No Partition narrative.

### 4. Malaya

The Emergency, the Briggs Plan and New Villages, and independence in 1957. The Batang Kali killings (1948), still disputed in British courts.

### 5. Many clocks

A timeline graphic of Asian independence dates from 1946 to 1999, with the wars and partitions marked. The act refuses “Asia awoke” narration. Each clock had its own politics.

## Comparative note

African decolonization (Episodes 68–70) also ran on many clocks, from Libya (1951) and Ghana (1957) to Portuguese Africa (1974–75) and Zimbabwe (1980). The Asian and African cases shaped one another, through Bandung and the United Nations (Episode 70).

## How we know

Colonial and national archives (Nationaal Archief; ANRI; ANOM; The National Archives, UK). The Dutch research program “Independence, Decolonization, Violence and War in Indonesia, 1945–1950” (KITLV, NIMH, NIOD; 2022). Scholarship by Benedict Anderson (*Java in a Time of Revolution*, 1972), Christopher Goscha (*Vietnam: A New History*, 2016), and Tim Harper (*The End of Empire and the Making of Malaya*, 1999).

## Do not claim

- That Asian independence came in a single wave or on one date.
- That the Dutch or French withdrew willingly.
- That this episode narrates Indian independence or Partition; it notes the date and hands on.
- That the *bersiap* violence cancels Dutch colonial violence, or the reverse.
- That independence ended all colonial rule in Asia; Goa, Macao, Hong Kong, and West New Guinea came later.
