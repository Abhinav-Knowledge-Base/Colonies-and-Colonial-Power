## Before

Episode 36 showed the household and the law. Slavery tried to reduce people to labor and property. The people it held made cultures anyway.

## The turn

Africans arrived from many regions with different languages and religions. In the Americas they built new communities. Some were organized by African ethnic identity: Yoruba (Nagô, Lucumí), Kongo, Akan (Coromantee), and Igbo. Some were new creole cultures. Religions combined African practices with Catholic or Protestant forms. Vodou in Saint-Domingue and Haiti, Candomblé in Brazil, and Regla de Ocha (Santería) in Cuba drew heavily on West and Central African religion. Black Christian congregations in North America and the Caribbean made Christianity their own, from hush-harbor worship to the African Methodist Episcopal Church (founded 1816). Creole languages — Haitian Kreyòl, Jamaican Patois, Papiamento, Gullah — emerged. Foods, musical forms, and kinship networks, including shipmate bonds (*malungo* in Brazil, *sippi* in Suriname), made community possible.

Colonial authorities often suppressed these practices as dangerous, and they were sometimes used in revolt. The ceremony at Bois Caïman (1791; Episode 38) is part of Haitian revolutionary memory, and its details are debated. The series films these cultures as achievements of the people who made them. It does not frame them as compensation for slavery.

## After

Episode 38 turns to Saint-Domingue, where enslaved people overthrew slavery and colonial rule.

## Acts

### 1. Arriving from many places

African origins by region, from the Slave Voyages database. Ethnic labels used by traders and adopted or remade by the enslaved. The act avoids treating “Africa” as one culture.

### 2. Religion

Vodou, Candomblé, and Regla de Ocha as religions with their own theologies and communities, filmed with the consent of practitioners. Black Christianity from hush harbors to independent churches. Colonial and later suppression.

### 3. Language and food

Creole languages as new languages, not broken versions of European ones. Foodways — rice culture in the Carolina Lowcountry drawing on West African expertise (a debated argument), okra, and plantains — and the provision grounds where enslaved people grew their own food.

### 4. Music and kinship

Drumming traditions and their suppression, work songs, and spirituals. Shipmate kinship and family networks across plantations. Naming practices, including African day names among Akan-descended people in Jamaica.

### 5. Not a silver lining

A closing act in which descendant scholars and practitioners explain why survival and creativity are testimony to the people, not to the system. The act refuses the framing that slavery “gave” anything.

## Comparative note

Indigenous peoples under colonial rule (Episodes 15, 41) also kept and remade religions and languages under pressure. Cultural survival under coercion appears across the series. Its forms differed with the regime and the community.

## How we know

Colonial suppression laws. Missionary and planter accounts (hostile witnesses). Linguistic studies of creoles. Oral tradition and living practice, with consent. Scholarship by Sidney Mintz and Richard Price (*The Birth of African-American Culture*, 1976), John Thornton, Judith Carney (*Black Rice*, 2001), and Laurent Dubois on Vodou.

## Do not claim

- That slavery gave enslaved people their culture or religion.
- That African cultures survived unchanged; they were remade.
- That Vodou or Candomblé are “magic” or “superstition”; they are religions.
- That details of Bois Caïman are established fact; they are debated.
- That creole languages are broken European languages.
