## Before

Episode 65 showed revolts older than nationalism. Early nationalist associations (Episode 55) were mostly elite petitioners. Between the world wars and after 1945, anticolonial movements recruited masses.

## The turn

Mass anticolonial politics drew on new tools: political parties with branches and dues, trade unions, newspapers, schools, religious associations, and women’s organizations. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, became the largest such party in the colonial world. Its history, and Gandhi’s, belong to *Discovery of India* and are not told here. This hour compares other cases.

In Indonesia, Sarekat Islam (1912) became the first mass organization, with hundreds of thousands of members. The Communist Party (PKI, 1920) led failed revolts in 1926–27. Sukarno’s Indonesian National Party (PNI, 1927) was banned and he was exiled. In Vietnam, Hồ Chí Minh founded the Indochinese Communist Party (1930) and the Viet Minh front (1941), combining nationalism and communism. In Algeria, Messali Hadj’s Étoile Nord-Africaine (1926), founded among Algerian workers in France, called for independence. The Association of Algerian Muslim Ulama (1931), led by Abdelhamid Ben Badis, taught Arabic and Islam as national identity (“Islam is my religion, Arabic is my language, Algeria is my homeland”). Ferhat Abbas sought equality within France before turning to independence. In the Gold Coast, Kwame Nkrumah broke from the elite United Gold Coast Convention and founded the Convention People’s Party (CPP) in 1949. Its “Positive Action” campaign of strikes and boycotts (1950) led to his imprisonment, and then to electoral victory in 1951, and independence as Ghana in 1957. Women organized too. In Nigeria, Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti led the Abeokuta Women’s Union against colonial taxation and a British-backed ruler in the 1940s.

## After

Episode 67 shows the Second World War as the hinge that made these movements unstoppable.

## Acts

### 1. The tools of mass politics

Parties, unions, newspapers, and schools, compared across colonies. How colonial education and cities produced organizers. Congress named once and handed to *Discovery of India*.

### 2. Indonesia

Sarekat Islam, the PKI, and the PNI. The Youth Pledge (1928): one homeland, one nation, one language. Colonial repression and the Boven-Digoel internment camp.

### 3. Vietnam

Hồ Chí Minh’s path from Paris (1919 petition to Versailles) to Moscow to the Viet Minh. The Nghệ-Tĩnh soviets (1930–31) and their repression. Nationalism and communism fused.

### 4. Algeria’s early nationalism

Messali Hadj, Ben Badis, and Ferhat Abbas: three strands (populist, religious-cultural, and liberal). The Blum-Viollette proposal (1936) for limited citizenship, defeated by settlers. Handed forward to Episode 69.

### 5. Nkrumah and the CPP

From the 1948 Accra riots to Positive Action, prison, and the 1951 election. The CPP as a mass party with women traders (market women) as a base. Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti and women’s movements in Nigeria. The act ends with a comparative board of party, base, method, and colonial response.

## Comparative note

Mass politics took different forms under different regimes. Where colonial powers allowed elections (Gold Coast after 1951), parties could win power legally. Where they did not, or where settlers blocked reform (Algeria, Kenya), movements turned to armed struggle (Episode 69).

## How we know

Party records and newspapers. Colonial police and intelligence reports (in national archives and the UK migrated archives). Nationalist leaders’ writings. Scholarship by Takashi Shiraishi (*An Age in Motion*, 1990), William Duiker on Hồ Chí Minh, James McDougall on Algerian nationalism, Jean Allman on Ghana, and Cheryl Johnson-Odim and Nina Emma Mba on Ransome-Kuti.

## Do not claim

- That this episode tells the Indian national movement; it belongs to *Discovery of India*.
- That mass nationalist movements were unified; they had rival strands.
- That colonial powers granted independence without mass pressure.
- That women were absent from anticolonial politics.
- That nationalism and communism were always opposed in colonial movements.
