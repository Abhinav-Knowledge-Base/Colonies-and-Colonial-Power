## Before

The ships and the line gave monarchs claims. They did not pay for fleets, forts, or garrisons. Crowns were short of money, and merchants were short of protection. The chartered company was one answer.

## The turn

The Portuguese crown ran a royal trading system. The *Casa da Índia* in Lisbon controlled trade, and fortified trading posts (*feitorias*, “factories”) were run by crown officials. Castile ran the *Casa de Contratación* in Seville from 1503 to regulate trade with the Americas. Northern European states used a different tool. A monarch or estates-general granted a charter giving a group of investors a monopoly on trade to a region. The Dutch and English companies added permanent joint-stock capital, and this became a new kind of power. The English East India Company (1600) and the Dutch Verenigde Oostindische Compagnie (VOC, 1602) could maintain armies, build forts, negotiate treaties, administer justice, and wage war. They answered to shareholders and, less directly, to the state that chartered them.

Charters did not make the companies all-powerful. In Asia they were long minor players beside Mughal, Safavid, Ming/Qing, and Javanese powers. They depended on local merchants, bankers, and rulers. The form matters because it fused commerce with sovereignty. That fusion returns across the series, from Batavia to the Congo Free State.

## Acts

### 1. The feitoria

A Portuguese *feitoria* at Elmina (1482) or Cochin: a fort, a warehouse, a factor, a garrison, and a treaty with a local ruler. The crown owned the trade. The act distinguishes this royal model from later joint-stock companies.

### 2. Seville’s house

The *Casa de Contratación* licensed ships, recorded cargoes, and trained pilots. Spanish American trade was a crown monopoly organized through fleets (*flota* system). Spain did not rely on a chartered company to conquer the Americas. Conquest was carried out by licensed private expeditions under crown contracts (*capitulaciones*).

### 3. Joint stock

The VOC’s 1602 charter, its permanent capital, and its tradable shares on the Amsterdam exchange. The English East India Company’s charter of 1600 and its slower shift to permanent capital (by the 1650s). A reading of charter clauses granting the right to make war and treaties. This series does not tell the story of either company in detail here. The VOC returns in Episode 25, the English companies in Episode 17, and the East India Company’s territorial rule in Episode 21.

### 4. Many charters

The Dutch West India Company (1621), the Royal African Company (1672, successor to the Royal Adventurers of 1660), the Hudson’s Bay Company (1670), and the French Compagnie des Indes. Many failed or went bankrupt. The form was common, and success was not.

### 5. The long afterlife

19th-century chartered companies in Africa and Asia: the Royal Niger Company (1886), the British South Africa Company (1889), the Imperial British East Africa Company (1888), and the German colonial companies. The Congo Free State (Episode 29) was not a company but a personal sovereign regime run with concessionary companies. The act closes on the principle: when a company governs, profit and rule are the same ledger.

## Comparative note

Asian merchant networks — Gujarati *banias*, Hokkien traders, Armenian merchants of New Julfa — ran long-distance trade with credit and partnerships but without state charters granting sovereignty. The difference was not commercial skill. It was the grant of armed sovereignty to merchants.

## How we know

Charters and company records: the VOC archive in the Nationaal Archief (The Hague) and in Jakarta, Colombo, Chennai, and Cape Town; East India Company records within the British Library’s India Office Records; Casa de Contratación papers in the Archivo General de Indias. Economic histories of early joint-stock finance.

## Do not claim

- That the East India Company or the VOC was dominant in Asia from its founding.
- That Spain conquered the Americas through a chartered company.
- That joint-stock companies were purely economic; they made war and law.
- That the company form was invented in 1600; it had medieval and Iberian antecedents.
- That the companies’ success was inevitable; many chartered companies failed.
