## Before

Episode 18 showed colonies built to extract sugar through enslaved labor. Settler colonies wanted something else: land for settlers to farm and keep.

## The turn

Ireland is a debated early case. English plantations in Munster (1580s) and Ulster (from 1609), and the Cromwellian conquest and confiscations (1649–53), transferred land to English and Scottish Protestant settlers. Some historians treat Ireland as a colonial laboratory; others stress its place within the British and Irish kingdoms. The series presents it carefully as a debate and moves on.

In North America, Jamestown (1607) depended on the Powhatan paramount chiefdom and fought wars with it (1610–14, 1622, 1644). New England settlers fought the Pequot War (1636–38) and King Philip’s War (1675–76), in which the Wampanoag leader Metacom (called King Philip) led an alliance against colonial expansion. The war was among the deadliest in colonial North American history relative to population. Epidemic disease had already devastated coastal peoples before and during settlement.

Australia came much later. The First Fleet arrived at Sydney Cove in 1788. British law treated the continent as if no one owned it, a doctrine later called *terra nullius* and rejected by Australia’s High Court in *Mabo* (1992). Frontier wars and massacres followed for more than a century. In Aotearoa New Zealand, British sovereignty was claimed through the Treaty of Waitangi (1840). Its English and Māori texts differ significantly on sovereignty. The New Zealand Wars (1845–1872) and land confiscations followed. Each case is dated in its own century.

## Acts

### 1. Ireland, carefully

The Ulster plantation and Cromwellian confiscations as an early case of settler land transfer. The debate over whether “colonial” is the right frame. One act, not the center of the hour.

### 2. Powhatan and New England

Tsenacomoco under Wahunsenacawh (Powhatan), Jamestown’s dependence and wars. The Pequot War and King Philip’s War as evidenced conflicts, with Indigenous captives sold into Caribbean slavery after 1676. Indigenous diplomacy, alliances, and law. No “empty land.”

### 3. Disease and dispossession

Epidemics before and during settlement, and how settlers used them to claim divine sanction. Deeds, purchases, and frauds. Disease did not act alone; settlers took land by law and war.

### 4. Australia after 1788

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander nations, their law and land. *Terra nullius* as legal fiction. Frontier violence documented by the University of Newcastle’s Colonial Frontier Massacres map project. Resistance by leaders such as Pemulwuy (Eora country, 1790s). Filmed with consultation and respect for protocols about images of the deceased.

### 5. Aotearoa and the Treaty

The Treaty of Waitangi’s two texts: *kāwanatanga* (governance) versus sovereignty, and *tino rangatiratanga*. The New Zealand Wars and the confiscations under the New Zealand Settlements Act (1863). The Waitangi Tribunal (1975) as a later process. The act closes on a line the series repeats: settler colonies did not end when the settlers stopped arriving.

## Comparative note

French Algeria (Episode 28) and settler Africa (Episode 54) show settler colonies with large colonized majorities who remained. In Australia and New England, settlers became majorities. The difference shaped how, and whether, decolonization came.

## How we know

Colonial records and settler accounts (claims). Indigenous oral histories and records, with consultation. Archaeology of contact sites. Court decisions (*Mabo v Queensland (No 2)*, 1992). The Waitangi Tribunal’s reports. Scholarship by Lisa Brooks (*Our Beloved Kin*, 2018, on King Philip’s War), Henry Reynolds, Lyndall Ryan, and James Belich.

## Do not claim

- That any of these lands was empty or unowned.
- That Australia was settled in the 17th century; the First Fleet arrived in 1788.
- That disease alone explains dispossession.
- That the Treaty of Waitangi’s English and Māori texts say the same thing.
- That Ireland’s place in colonial history is settled; it is debated.
