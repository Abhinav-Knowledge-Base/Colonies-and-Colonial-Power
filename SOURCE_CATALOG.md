# Source catalog

Evidence map for *Colonies and Colonial Power* (*The Colonial Age*). It lists the classes of evidence the series uses, what each can show, and what each cannot settle. It belongs to this series only.

Series bible: [`README.md`](README.md). Production: [`PRODUCTION_GUIDE.md`](PRODUCTION_GUIDE.md). Packaging: [`SEASONS_AND_PATHS.md`](SEASONS_AND_PATHS.md). Comparison: [`COMPARATIVE_MATRIX.md`](COMPARATIVE_MATRIX.md). Clearance: [`RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md`](RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md).

## Principle

The colonial archive over-represents the state (honesty rule 13). Every evidence class below has a bias of authorship, purpose, and survival. The series uses each for what it can show and labels what it cannot settle.

## 1. Imperial state archives

**What they are.** The paperwork of empire: correspondence, laws, reports, tax and tribute rolls, court records, and censuses.

**Major holdings.** Archivo General de Indias (Seville; online via PARES); Archivo Histórico Nacional (Madrid); Torre do Tombo and Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino (Lisbon); The National Archives (Kew), including the Colonial Office (CO), Foreign Office (FO), Treasury (T), and the “migrated archives” (FCO 141); the British Library’s India Office Records, treated as the archive of an imperial company-state and not as the basis of an India series; Nationaal Archief (The Hague) for the VOC, WIC, and Dutch colonial state; Archives nationales d’outre-mer (Aix-en-Provence); Service historique de la Défense (Vincennes); Bundesarchiv (Berlin); Archives africaines and the AfricaMuseum (Brussels and Tervuren); the US National Archives (NARA).

**What they can show.** Policy, law, administrative practice, revenue, official counts, and what officials knew.

**What they cannot settle.** How colonized people understood events; what happened where officials were absent; motives officials hid; totals of the dead where the state did not count. Destruction and removal at decolonization (FCO 141 revealed in 2011) mean some gaps are permanent.

**Episodes.** Nearly all; central in 4, 6, 21, 25, 26, 28, 29, 69.

## 2. Archives in formerly colonized states

**Major holdings.** Archivo General de la Nación (Mexico); Archivo General de la Nación (Peru); Archivo y Biblioteca Nacionales de Bolivia (Sucre); Arquivo Nacional (Rio de Janeiro); Public Records and Archives Administration Department (Ghana); Archives nationales du Sénégal (Dakar), which hold the federal archive of French West Africa; Arsip Nasional Republik Indonesia (Jakarta); National Archives of the Philippines; Kenya National Archives; National Archives of Namibia; National Archives of South Africa; National Archives of Zimbabwe; Arkib Negara Malaysia; National Archives of Nigeria (Ibadan, Enugu, Kaduna); Archives nationales d’Haïti; Jamaica Archives; national archives of Korea and Vietnam.

**What they can show.** Colonial administration from the colony’s side, local courts and petitions, and post-independence records.

**What they cannot settle.** They also hold colonial-state records with the same biases. Access, preservation, and funding vary. The series credits them on screen equally with metropolitan archives.

## 3. Company archives

**What they are.** Records of the VOC, WIC, English East India Company, Royal African Company (T 70), Hudson’s Bay Company (Archives of Manitoba), the Compagnie des Indes, and 19th–20th-century concession and plantation companies (Unilever for Huileries du Congo Belge).

**What they can show.** Trade volumes, prices, profits, shipping, orders to officers, and labor contracts.

**What they cannot settle.** Company records serve shareholders. Violence appears as cost or euphemism. Enslaved people appear as cargo.

## 4. Voyage and demographic databases

**What they are.** The Slave Voyages project (Trans-Atlantic and Intra-American databases, African Origins); the Legacies of British Slavery database (UCL); indenture emigration registers; parish and tribute records compiled into population series.

**What they can show.** Scale, routes, timing, mortality patterns, and names where recorded.

**What they cannot settle.** Missing voyages are estimated. Totals are estimates (on the order of 12.5 million embarked and 10.7 million disembarked) and are revised. Deaths before embarkation (in capture and on the march to the coast) are largely unrecorded. Names are the exception.

**Episodes.** 7, 33–40.

## 5. Conquerors’ and officials’ narratives

**What they are.** Columbus’s writings (via Las Casas), Cortés’s *Cartas de relación*, Bernal Díaz, the Pizarro circle’s accounts, Zurara’s chronicle, governors’ memoirs, and explorers’ journals (Livingstone, Stanley).

**What they can show.** What their authors wanted rulers and readers to believe, and sometimes incidental detail.

**What they cannot settle.** They are claims (label **CL**). Numbers of enemies and dead are routinely inflated or minimized. Indigenous and African allies are routinely erased.

## 6. Missionary sources

**What they are.** Jesuit *Relations*, Franciscan chronicles, the Florentine Codex (compiled under Sahagún from Nahua informants), mission society archives (CMS, LMS, White Fathers, Baptist Missionary Society), missionary dictionaries and grammars, and missionary photographs (Alice Seeley Harris).

**What they can show.** Local life observed at length; languages; sometimes testimony against colonial abuse (the Congo reform campaign).

**What they cannot settle.** Missionary records are not a census of belief (honesty rule 12). Conversion counts serve fundraising. Local religion is described as error.

## 7. Writing by colonized and enslaved people

**What it is.** Nahuatl and Quechua notarial records, annals, and petitions; Guaman Poma’s *Nueva corónica*; Titu Cusi Yupanqui’s account; Afonso I of Kongo’s letters; Diponegoro’s *babad*; slave narratives (Equiano, Baquaqua, Omar ibn Said, Harriet Jacobs); freedom suits; Sokoto’s Arabic manuscripts and Nana Asma’u’s poetry; the African and Asian press; nationalist writings (Rizal, Plaatje, Nkrumah); Haitian state documents.

**What it can show.** Colonized people’s own arguments, categories, and strategies.

**What it cannot settle.** Much was written under colonial constraints, for colonial audiences, or with editors’ interventions. Some narratives have debated authorship or firsthand status (Equiano’s birthplace). Survival favors elites and the literate.

## 8. Oral history and oral tradition

**What it is.** Recorded testimony of living people (Mau Mau veterans, residential school survivors, comfort-station survivors) and traditions passed across generations (Maroon, Saramaka, Asante, Herero, Bandanese in the Kei Islands).

**What it can show.** Memory, meaning, events outside the archive, and communities’ own historical frameworks.

**What it cannot settle.** Oral tradition is labeled **MEM** and handled by its own methods (Jan Vansina). Testimony used in legal proceedings (the TRC; *Mutua v. FCO*) is cited from the public record. Communities control access to some traditions.

## 9. Archaeology and material evidence

**What it is.** Plantation and slave-quarter archaeology; maroon sites (Serra da Barriga); fort and factory sites (Elmina, Cape Coast); shipwrecks (the *San Diego*); Taíno and Kilwa sites; epidemic cemeteries (Teposcolula-Yucundaa); colonial urban archaeology; ancient DNA.

**What it can show.** Daily life, diet, housing, trade goods, and the presence of people the documents ignore.

**What it cannot settle.** Motives, names, and totals. Ancient DNA findings are recent and developing and are labeled as such. Human remains are not shown (see `RIGHTS_AND_FACTCHECK.md`).

## 10. Visual sources

**What they are.** Colonial photographs; casta paintings; Eckhout’s and Post’s Brazilian paintings; 19th-century history painting; abolitionist prints (the *Brookes* diagram); colonial film (British Pathé, Colonial Film Unit, Istituto Luce, INA, ECPAD, Eye Filmmuseum); anticolonial and independence footage.

**What they can show.** How empire represented itself and its subjects, and occasionally evidence of events (Congo photographs).

**What they cannot settle.** Images were made for purposes: ideology, science, propaganda, campaign. Casta paintings are not census data. Colonial photographs of prisoners and the dead raise consent and dignity questions.

## 11. Legal texts and court records

**What they are.** Papal bulls; treaties (Tordesillas, Waitangi, the numbered treaties, Berlin’s General Act, Évian); slave codes (Barbados 1661, Virginia 1662, Code Noir 1685); colonial statutes (Natives Land Act 1913, Indian Act 1876); abolition acts; court decisions (*Johnson v. M’Intosh*, *Worcester v. Georgia*, *Mabo*, *McGirt*, *Mutua v. FCO*); and tribunal reports.

**What they can show.** The rules, the categories, and the legal claims.

**What they cannot settle.** Law is not practice. A code that limited punishment does not show punishment was limited. Treaties may have been understood differently by their parties (Waitangi’s two texts; the numbered treaties’ oral records).

## 12. Scholarship and inquiries

**What they are.** Historians’ syntheses and debates; official inquiries (Casement 1904; the Hunter Committee 1920; the Belgian Lumumba inquiry 2001; the Church Committee 1975; the Canadian TRC 2015; the Dutch Indonesia research program 2022); truth commissions; restitution reports (Sarr and Savoy 2018).

**What they can show.** Weighed evidence, competing interpretations, and official findings.

**What they cannot settle.** Inquiries have mandates and limits. Scholarship disagrees. The series names positions (for example, Hochschild and his critics on the Congo; Elkins and her critics on Kenya; Sen and others on Bengal) rather than choosing a winner where evidence does not.

## What no source class settles

- Exact death tolls for the conquest of the Americas, the Congo Free State, Banda 1621, the Herero and Nama, the Algerian conquest and war, and the major famines.
- The pre-contact population of the Americas.
- Deaths between capture and embarkation in Africa.
- The inner lives of most enslaved and colonized people, especially women.
- The net economic effect of empire on the metropoles, measured without a verdict built in.
