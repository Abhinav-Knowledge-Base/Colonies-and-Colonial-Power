## Before

Episode 27 introduced Saint-Domingue, the richest colony in the Americas, with about half a million enslaved people, a small white population, and a large class of free people of color. Episode 37 showed the cultures enslaved people built. In 1789 revolution in France opened a crisis in the colony.

## The turn

Free people of color demanded equal rights; Vincent Ogé led a revolt in 1790 and was executed. In August 1791 enslaved people in the Northern Plain rose in a coordinated insurrection and burned plantations. Its beginnings are linked in memory to the Bois Caïman ceremony, whose details are debated. Toussaint Louverture, a formerly enslaved man who had been freed, emerged as the leading commander. Spain and Britain invaded, hoping to take the colony. The French commissioner Léger-Félicité Sonthonax declared emancipation in the north in 1793, and the French National Convention abolished slavery throughout its colonies in February 1794. Louverture joined France, defeated the British and Spanish, and by 1801 governed the colony under his own constitution, which named him governor for life.

Napoleon sent an expedition under Charles Leclerc in 1802 to restore French control and, as the restoration of slavery in Guadeloupe that year showed, slavery itself. Louverture was captured by deception and died in a French prison in 1803. Jean-Jacques Dessalines led the war that followed, which was fought with extreme violence on both sides. At Vertières in November 1803 French forces were defeated. On 1 January 1804 Dessalines declared the independence of Haiti.

In early 1804, on Dessalines’s orders, most of the remaining French population was killed. Estimates are around 3,000 to 5,000 people. This is part of the record and is stated without gore. Haiti was isolated by slaveholding powers; the United States did not recognize it until 1862. In 1825 France demanded an indemnity of 150 million francs (later reduced to 90 million) for recognition, backed by warships. Haiti borrowed from French banks to pay. Payments on the indemnity and associated loans continued into the mid-20th century.

## After

Episode 39 examines abolition as politics, in which Haiti’s example was a fear in every slaveholding empire.

## Acts

### 1. A colony in crisis

Saint-Domingue’s social order, the free people of color’s campaign, and Ogé. The French Revolution’s declarations and their limits.

### 2. The rising of 1791

The insurrection as organized action by enslaved people. Bois Caïman as memory, with its debated evidence. The burning of the plain shown through records and landscapes, not recreated fire.

### 3. Louverture

His rise, his alliances, the 1801 constitution, and his labor regime, which required former slaves to work on plantations. He is presented whole: liberator, general, and ruler who kept plantations. His capture and death.

### 4. War of independence

Leclerc’s expedition and yellow fever. Dessalines and the war’s violence. Vertières and 1804. The killing of the remaining French in 1804 stated plainly, with estimates and without imagery.

### 5. The price of recognition

The 1825 ordinance and its gunboats, the loans, and the decades of payments. Haiti as an example for enslaved people elsewhere and a fear for slaveholders. Its isolation. The act closes on the indemnity as a chain into the 20th century, and on Haitian demands for restitution.

## Comparative note

The American Revolution kept slavery (Episode 20). Spanish American independence was led by creoles (Episode 44). Haiti was the one revolution in the Americas made by enslaved people, and it abolished slavery. Simón Bolívar received aid from Haitian president Alexandre Pétion in 1816 on condition that he free the enslaved (Episode 44).

## How we know

French colonial and military archives (ANOM; Service historique de la Défense). Louverture’s correspondence and constitution. The Haitian Declaration of Independence (a printed copy found in The National Archives, Kew, in 2010). The 1825 ordinance. Scholarship by C. L. R. James (*The Black Jacobins*, 1938), Carolyn Fick, Laurent Dubois (*Avengers of the New World*, 2004), Philippe Girard, and research on the indemnity’s payments (including a 2022 *New York Times* investigation, labeled as journalism).

## Do not claim

- That the Haitian Revolution was a footnote to the French Revolution.
- That it was uncomplicated heroism; the 1804 killings and Louverture’s labor regime are part of the record.
- That the Bois Caïman ceremony’s details are established.
- That France granted freedom to Haiti; Haitians won it.
- That the indemnity’s total effect is a settled figure; estimates differ.
