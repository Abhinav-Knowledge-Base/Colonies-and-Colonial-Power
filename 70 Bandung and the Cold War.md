## Before

Episode 69 followed the wars of exit. By the mid-1950s many Asian states were independent, and Africa was about to follow. The world was dividing between the United States and the Soviet Union.

## The turn

In April 1955, the Asian-African Conference at Bandung, Indonesia, brought together 29 states, including Indonesia, India, China, Egypt, Ghana (then the Gold Coast, attending as an observer), Japan, and others. Sukarno opened it. The final communiqué condemned colonialism “in all its manifestations” — a phrase some delegates meant to include Soviet domination — and endorsed self-determination and peaceful coexistence. Bandung became a symbol of Third World solidarity. The Non-Aligned Movement was founded in Belgrade in 1961.

In 1956 Egypt’s Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal. Britain, France, and Israel invaded. US and Soviet pressure forced them to withdraw (Episode 24). In the Congo, Belgium granted independence on 30 June 1960 with almost no preparation. Within days the army mutinied. Katanga, backed by Belgian mining interests and officers, seceded. Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba appealed to the UN and then to the Soviet Union. He was deposed in September 1960, captured, and handed to Katangan authorities, and was killed on 17 January 1961 in the presence of Belgian officers. The documented record: a Belgian parliamentary inquiry (2001) found Belgium bore “moral responsibility,” and Belgian officers were present at the killing. The US Senate Church Committee (1975) documented a CIA plan to poison Lumumba that was not carried out. What is argued in the scholarship is the extent of US involvement in the actual killing. In 2022 Belgium returned a tooth, Lumumba’s only known remains, to his family. UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld died in a plane crash in 1961 during the crisis; the cause remains under UN investigation. Joseph-Désiré Mobutu seized power in 1965 with Western support.

Many new states became one-party states or military regimes within a decade: Ghana under Nkrumah (one-party by 1964, overthrown in 1966), Guinea under Sékou Touré, and Indonesia after 1965, where the army-led mass killings of alleged communists in 1965–66 (with estimates of 500,000 or more dead) brought Suharto to power. The series states this plainly, and explains causes — colonial legacies, Cold War patronage, internal conflicts — without claiming colonized peoples were unready for freedom.

## After

Episode 71 asks what the treaties left: borders, languages, economies, looted objects, and debt.

## Acts

### 1. Bandung

Delegates, speeches, and the communiqué. Richard Wright’s account (*The Color Curtain*, 1956). Solidarity and its limits: China, India, and Pakistan had deep disagreements.

### 2. Non-alignment

Belgrade (1961), Nasser, Nehru (named only as a participant; his Indian politics are *Discovery of India*’s), Tito, Sukarno, and Nkrumah. Non-alignment as strategy in a bipolar world.

### 3. Suez

Nationalization, invasion, and withdrawal. The end of Britain’s and France’s ability to act without the superpowers.

### 4. The Congo and Lumumba

Independence, mutiny, and Katanga. Lumumba’s independence-day speech. His killing with the documented record (Belgian inquiry; Church Committee) and the inferred or argued (the scope of US involvement) separated on screen. The return of the tooth in 2022. Mobutu’s coup.

### 5. Not every state was democratic

One-party states, coups, and the Indonesian killings of 1965–66, with estimated tolls. Explanation without sneering: colonial states had been authoritarian, borders were imposed, and Cold War powers backed strongmen.

## Comparative note

Latin America experienced US-backed interventions in the same era (Guatemala 1954; Chile 1973), in states independent for more than a century. Cold War intervention was not only a problem of new states; it was a problem of power.

## How we know

Bandung conference records. UN records on the Congo. The Belgian parliamentary inquiry report (2001). The Church Committee report (1975). Scholarship by Ludo De Witte (*The Assassination of Lumumba*, 1999; English 2001), Christopher Lee (ed., *Making a World after Empire*, 2010), Odd Arne Westad (*The Global Cold War*, 2005), and Geoffrey Robinson (*The Killing Season*, 2018).

## Do not claim

- That the US directly killed Lumumba as documented fact; its plan to poison him is documented, and its role in the actual killing is argued.
- That Belgium bore no responsibility; its parliament found moral responsibility.
- That new states failed because colonized peoples were unready.
- That Bandung created a unified bloc.
- That the Indonesian killings’ toll is precisely known.
