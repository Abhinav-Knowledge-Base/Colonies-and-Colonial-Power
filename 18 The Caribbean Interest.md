## Before

Episode 17 showed English power as factories on others’ coasts. In the Caribbean, England took land to settle, first islands Spain had not occupied, and then Spanish Jamaica.

## The turn

English settlers arrived on Barbados in 1627. They first grew tobacco and cotton with indentured English, Scottish, and Irish servants. In the 1640s they turned to sugar, learning techniques from Dutch Brazil, and to enslaved African labor. Within a generation Barbados was a slave society in which enslaved Africans were the majority. Its 1661 slave code became a model for Jamaica, South Carolina, and Antigua. England seized Jamaica from Spain in 1655. By the 18th century Jamaica was Britain’s richest colony, with enslaved people outnumbering whites by roughly ten to one.

Sugar money flowed to Britain. Absentee planters lived in England, bought estates, and sat in Parliament. The West India interest — planters, merchants in London, Bristol, and Liverpool, and allied members — lobbied on sugar duties, trade laws, and later against abolition. Historians still debate how far Caribbean slavery financed British industrialization. Eric Williams’s *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) argued it was central; critics have questioned the scale; newer work has revived parts of the argument. The series presents the debate, not a verdict.

## Acts

### 1. Barbados turns to sugar

Indentured servants and the “sugar revolution” of the 1640s–60s. The term is itself debated. Plantation layouts, mills, and boiling houses. The shift to enslaved African majorities.

### 2. The 1661 code

The Barbados “Act for the better ordering and governing of Negroes,” its language, and its spread. Law defined enslaved people as property and gave owners near-total power. The text is read, not dramatized.

### 3. Jamaica

The 1655 conquest and the Maroons who had escaped Spanish rule and fought the English (Episode 43). Tacky’s Revolt (1760) as an evidenced major rising. The planter class and its fear.

### 4. The interest in Parliament

The West India lobby, the Molasses Act (1733) and Sugar Act (1764), and absentee wealth in British country houses. The Legacies of British Slavery database is introduced for the compensation claims (Episode 39).

### 5. The Williams debate

Eric Williams’s thesis, its critics, and its revival. Trinidad’s first prime minister was also a historian of empire. The act closes by handing economics to Season 5 and abolition to Episode 39.

## Comparative note

French Saint-Domingue (Episode 27) and Portuguese Brazil (Episode 14) had their own sugar systems and different legal codes. Episode 35 compares the plantation regimes directly.

## How we know

Colonial statutes and Colonial Office records (The National Archives, Kew, CO series). Barbados and Jamaica archives. Plantation records and inventories. The Legacies of British Slavery database (University College London). Scholarship by Richard Dunn (*Sugar and Slaves*, 1972), Hilary Beckles, Trevor Burnard, and debates on Williams.

## Do not claim

- That indentured servitude and chattel slavery were the same condition.
- That the “Irish slaves” meme is historically accurate; indentured Irish servants were not held in hereditary racial chattel slavery.
- That Williams’s thesis is either proven or refuted; it remains debated.
- That the Middle Passage is narrated here; it is Episode 34.
- That enslaved people were passive; revolt and flight were constant.
