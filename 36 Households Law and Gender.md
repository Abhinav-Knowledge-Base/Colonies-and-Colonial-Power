## Before

Episode 35 followed plantation labor and law. Slavery also reproduced itself through law about birth and family, which fell hardest on women.

## The turn

In 1662 the Virginia assembly declared that children would be bond or free according to the condition of the mother: *partus sequitur ventrem*. Other English colonies followed. Spanish and Portuguese law, drawing on Roman precedent, had the same principle. The rule meant that a child fathered by a white owner on an enslaved woman was enslaved. Sexual coercion of enslaved women by owners and overseers was therefore both a crime against those women and a source of property for owners. It is recorded in court cases, wills, manumission records, and the diaries of owners such as Thomas Thistlewood in Jamaica, whose own record documents his assaults. The series states this as a legal and demographic fact. It does not stage it.

Enslaved women were field workers. On many Caribbean sugar estates, women made up the majority of field gangs. They were also cooks, nurses, domestic workers, and market traders. Motherhood was a site of sale — children sold away from mothers — and of resistance: women negotiated, fled with children, and in rare documented cases took drastic action, as in the case of Margaret Garner (1856). Enslaved men were not the only subjects of slavery’s history, and women were not only victims.

## After

Episode 37 looks at the cultures that survived and were built under slavery.

## Acts

### 1. The law of the mother

The 1662 Virginia statute read as text. Roman and Iberian precedents. The legal logic that made enslaved women’s bodies a site of property reproduction.

### 2. Coercion as legal fact

Court records, wills, and Thistlewood’s diary described as documents. The act names sexual violence as systemic and documented, and does not reconstruct it. No actors, no suggestive imagery.

### 3. Women in the field

Gender composition of field gangs, from estate records. Pregnancy and field labor. Planters’ “amelioration” policies after the slave trade ended, aimed at raising birth rates, as economic calculation.

### 4. Motherhood and sale

Sale records separating families. Advertisements for runaway mothers with children. Margaret Garner’s case stated from court records, without dramatization. Harriet Jacobs’s *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl* (1861) as a narrative written by an enslaved woman.

### 5. Women’s resistance and freedom

Freedom suits by women (for example, Elizabeth Freeman in Massachusetts, 1781). Market women in the Caribbean and Brazil. Women in maroon communities (Nanny of the Maroons; Episode 43). Manumission patterns that freed more women than men in Brazil and Spanish America, and why.

## Comparative note

The Indian Ocean and trans-Saharan slave systems also involved concubinage and the enslavement of women in large numbers, under Islamic and customary law that made some children of concubines free. They were different systems, named for comparison and not as a diversion.

## How we know

Colonial statutes. Court and manumission records. Plantation records by gender. Thistlewood’s diaries (Beinecke Library, Yale). Enslaved women’s narratives. Scholarship by Jennifer Morgan (*Laboring Women*, 2004; *Reckoning with Slavery*, 2021), Deborah Gray White (*Ar’n’t I a Woman?*, 1985), Saidiya Hartman, and Marisa Fuentes (*Dispossessed Lives*, 2016).

## Do not claim

- That sexual coercion was occasional or incidental; it was systemic and legally enabled.
- That the series depicts or reconstructs sexual violence; it does not.
- That enslaved women were mainly domestic servants.
- That enslaved women were only victims.
- That relationships between owners and enslaved women were consensual romances; consent was structurally impossible to establish under slavery.
