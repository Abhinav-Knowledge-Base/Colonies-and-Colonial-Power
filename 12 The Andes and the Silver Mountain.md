## Before

Episode 11 showed Mexico falling through alliance, disease, and siege. Francisco Pizarro, a veteran of Panama, reached the Peruvian coast in the late 1520s. By then epidemic disease had probably already crossed the Andes ahead of the Spaniards.

## The turn

The Inca ruler Huayna Capac died, probably of an epidemic, in the 1520s. His sons Huáscar and Atahualpa fought a civil war over succession. Atahualpa had just won it when Pizarro’s force of fewer than 200 met him at Cajamarca in November 1532. The Spanish accounts describe a sudden ambush, a friar’s speech, and Atahualpa’s capture amid a massacre of his attendants. These accounts were written by participants seeking reward and are claim-heavy. Atahualpa paid a ransom in gold and silver and was executed in 1533. Conquest took decades more. Manco Inca led a great rising in 1536, and a neo-Inca state held out at Vilcabamba until 1572.

In 1545 silver was found at Cerro Rico, Potosí, in what is now Bolivia. Viceroy Francisco de Toledo in the 1570s reorganized an Andean labor obligation into the colonial *mita*: a rotating draft of Indigenous men from a wide region, sent to work the mines. Mercury from Huancavelica in Peru, found in 1563, made the patio amalgamation process possible and poisoned the people who mined and processed it. Deaths in the mines and refineries were a labor fact, recorded in complaints and petitions. Potosí became one of the largest cities in the Americas. Its silver entered world trade, much of it eventually reaching China (Episode 13).

## After

Episode 13 follows American silver across the Pacific on the Manila galleon.

## Acts

### 1. A state in civil war

Tawantinsuyu’s recent expansion, Huayna Capac’s death, and the Huáscar–Atahualpa war. Andean politics shaped the Spaniards’ opportunity.

### 2. Cajamarca as a claim

The accounts of Francisco de Xerez, Pedro Pizarro, and others, set against later Andean narratives (Titu Cusi Yupanqui’s account dictated in 1570; Guaman Poma). The act shows how little is securely known about what happened in the square. It labels numbers of the dead as claims.

### 3. Conquest takes decades

Manco Inca’s rising (1536–37), civil wars among the conquistadors, and Vilcabamba until 1572 and the execution of Túpac Amaru I. Andean lords (*kurakas*) who allied with Spain and kept power.

### 4. Potosí and the mita

Cerro Rico, the *mita* as Toledo designed it, and the journeys of mitayos and their families. Huancavelica mercury. Death and illness appear as recorded labor facts, not as spectacle. The act includes Indigenous litigation against mita abuses. Communities sent petitions and lawsuits to the Audiencia of Charcas and to Spain.

### 5. Silver leaves

Coins minted at Potosí, the flow to Seville, and the flow via Acapulco and Manila to China. Silver as the first global commodity chain connecting the Americas, Europe, and Asia. The act hands off to Episode 13.

## Comparative note

The mita used an Andean idea of rotating labor (*mit’a*) under the Inca state and turned it to a very different purpose, for a colonial economy and on colonial terms. Using existing institutions for colonial ends appears repeatedly in the series, from indirect rule in Africa to the Cultivation System in Java.

## How we know

Spanish conquest narratives (claims). Titu Cusi Yupanqui’s *Instrucción* and Guaman Poma’s *Nueva corónica*. Mita registers and petitions in the Archivo General de Indias and the Archivo y Biblioteca Nacionales de Bolivia (Sucre). Scholarship by Jeffrey Cole on the mita, Kris Lane (*Potosí*, 2019), and Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez on global silver. Environmental studies of mercury contamination.

## Do not claim

- That Pizarro conquered Peru at Cajamarca in a day.
- That the numbers killed at Cajamarca are known; they are claims.
- That the colonial mita was the same institution as the Inca *mit’a*.
- That Indigenous Andeans accepted the mita without resistance or litigation.
- That a precise number of deaths at Potosí is known.
