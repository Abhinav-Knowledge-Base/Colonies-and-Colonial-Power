## Before

## The turn

Historians once distinguished “primary resistance,” armed opposition to conquest, from later nationalism. The line is blurred. In Natal in 1906, a poll tax sparked the Bambatha rebellion, led by the Zulu chief Bambatha kaMancinza. It was crushed, with an estimated 3,000–4,000 Africans killed, against about two dozen colonial deaths. In Sierra Leone the Hut Tax War of 1898 opposed a new tax. In southeastern Nigeria in 1929, tens of thousands of Igbo and Ibibio women protested fears of taxation on women in the Women’s War (the “Aba Women’s Riots” in colonial records). Colonial troops killed more than 50 women.

John Chilembwe, a Nyasaland Baptist minister educated in the United States, led a rising in January 1915. His grievances were forced labor on settler estates (the *thangata* system), the conscription of Africans into the First World War, and racial humiliation. The rising was quickly suppressed, and Chilembwe was killed. His letter to the *Nyasaland Times* protesting African deaths in a European war was censored. He is honored today in Malawi. Independent African churches (Ethiopianism) and newspapers — *Imvo Zabantsundu* (1884), edited by John Tengo Jabavu, and the Gold Coast press — gave educated Africans a voice. The South African Native National Congress (1912) and the National Congress of British West Africa (1920) were early nationalist associations, largely of elites, that petitioned for rights. They are the prehistory of the mass politics in Episode 66.

## Acts

### 1. Bambatha, 1906

The poll tax, the rising, and its repression. Estimated tolls from colonial and later research. Gandhi served briefly as a stretcher-bearer; that is a clause, not a subject (his life is *Discovery of India*’s).

### 2. Taxes and refusal

The Hut Tax War (1898) and the Women’s War (1929): tax as a flashpoint, and women as political actors. The colonial label “riot” examined.

### 3. Chilembwe

His education, his church at Mbombwe, his letter, and the 1915 rising. The documentary record from the colonial inquiry, and his memory in Malawi. George Shepperson and Thomas Price’s *Independent African* (1958) as the classic study.

### 4. Churches and newspapers

Ethiopianism and independent churches. The African press as a political space. Mission schools producing critics.

### 5. Early nationalism

The SANNC’s 1914 and 1919 deputations to London, the NCBWA, and Harry Thuku’s East African Association in Kenya (1921), whose arrest led to a shooting of protesters in Nairobi in 1922. The act ends with elite petition politics about to give way to mass politics.

## Comparative note

In the Americas, Indigenous and enslaved revolt long predated independence movements (Episodes 43, 65). In Asia, as in Africa, early nationalism grew from the mission and colonial schools that empire built (Episodes 59, 66).

## How we know

Colonial inquiry reports (the Nyasaland Commission of 1915; the Aba Commission of 1930). African newspapers. Church and mission archives. Oral histories. Scholarship by Shula Marks (*Reluctant Rebellion*, 1970), Shepperson and Price, Judith Van Allen and Misty Bastian on the Women’s War, and Terence Ranger on resistance.

## Do not claim

- That African resistance ended after conquest and began again only with nationalism.
- That the Women’s War was a “riot”; that was the colonial label.
- That Chilembwe’s rising was only religious fanaticism, as colonial officials claimed.
- That the Herero and Maji Maji wars are retold here; they are in Episode 30.
- That early nationalist associations represented mass movements; they were mostly elite.
