## Before

Episode 10 ended in the Caribbean, where the island populations had collapsed and Spaniards looked west. Expeditions from Cuba reached the Yucatán coast in 1517 and 1518.

## The turn

Hernán Cortés landed in 1519 against the orders of Cuba’s governor. He succeeded not through Spanish arms alone but by joining a political world already at war. Tlaxcala had resisted the Triple Alliance for decades. After first fighting the Spaniards, it allied with them. Other altepetl (city-states) that paid tribute to Tenochtitlan saw a chance to end it. The Spaniards entered Tenochtitlan in November 1519 and took Moctezuma II captive. They were driven out in June–July 1520 (the Noche Triste). A smallpox epidemic then struck the city, killing many, including Moctezuma’s successor Cuitláhuac. After a siege of about three months with tens of thousands of Indigenous allies, Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521.

Malintzin (Doña Marina) was a Nahua woman who had been given to the Maya and then to the Spaniards. She interpreted between Nahuatl, Maya, and later Spanish, and acted as negotiator. She appears prominently in Nahua pictorial sources such as the *Lienzo de Tlaxcala*. Later Mexican nationalism made her a traitor (*la Malinche*); later feminist writers reclaimed her. The series treats her as a historical actor with limited choices, not a romance or a cartoon.

## After

Episode 12 goes to the Andes, where another recent empire was divided by civil war when the Spaniards arrived.

## Acts

### 1. Tribute and its enemies

The Triple Alliance’s tribute system and its enemies — Tlaxcala, the Purépecha (Tarascan) state that it never conquered, and discontented tributaries. The act establishes a political map of central Mexico in 1519 without modern borders.

### 2. Alliance

Tlaxcala’s decision and its later rewards and disappointments under Spanish rule. The *Lienzo de Tlaxcala* (the original lost; copies survive) shows the war from Tlaxcalan eyes: as their conquest too.

### 3. Malintzin

The evidence for her life: Cortés’s letters (which barely name her), Bernal Díaz’s later memoir, and Nahua pictorial sources. What is evidenced, what is inferred, and what is later memory. No staged romance.

### 4. Smallpox and siege

The 1520 epidemic described in the Florentine Codex (Book 12, compiled under Sahagún from Nahua informants decades later). The siege, the brigantines on the lake, and the allied armies. No battle ballet. Disease, guns, and alliances all appear, and none is the whole story.

### 5. The Maya are not one battle

Yucatán conquest attempts from 1527. Montejo’s campaigns, with Mérida founded in 1542. Continuing revolts. The Itza kingdom at Nojpetén held out until 1697. The Caste War of Yucatán (1847–1901) is named forward. The Maya regions had many polities and a long resistance.

## Comparative note

Episode 12 shows a parallel: an Inca state split by civil war, with Spaniards exploiting a succession crisis. In both cases Indigenous politics shaped the outcome. In both, the Spanish accounts compress years of alliance and war into heroic scenes.

## How we know

Cortés’s *Cartas de relación* (claims written to the emperor). Bernal Díaz del Castillo’s *Historia verdadera* (written decades later). The Florentine Codex (Nahua testimony compiled by Sahagún). The *Lienzo de Tlaxcala* and other Nahua pictorial histories. Scholarship by Matthew Restall (*Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest*, 2003), Camilla Townsend (*Malintzin’s Choices*, 2006; *Fifth Sun*, 2019), and Ross Hassig.

## Do not claim

- That a few hundred Spaniards conquered Mexico alone.
- That the Mexica believed Cortés was the god Quetzalcoatl; that story appears mainly in post-conquest sources and is doubted.
- That Malintzin was a traitor to a Mexican nation that did not yet exist, or a romantic heroine.
- That smallpox, guns, or alliances alone explain the fall of Tenochtitlan.
- That the Maya were conquered in one campaign.
