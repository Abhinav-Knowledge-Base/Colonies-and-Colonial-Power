## Before

Episodes 25–26 followed the Dutch. France entered overseas empire in North America and the Caribbean in the 17th century and built a second empire in Africa and Asia in the 19th.

## The turn

New France began with Quebec (1608). It depended on alliances with Wendat (Huron), Innu, Algonquin, and later Anishinaabe nations, and on the fur trade. It was a small settler population, with missions, and with Indigenous allies who shaped French policy. France lost New France to Britain in 1763. In the Caribbean, Martinique, Guadeloupe, and above all Saint-Domingue became sugar and coffee colonies worked by enslaved Africans. By the 1780s Saint-Domingue produced a large share of the world’s sugar and coffee, with roughly half a million enslaved people. It was the richest colony in the Americas. Louis XIV’s Code Noir (1685) regulated slavery. It gave owners broad power, set punishments, required Catholic baptism, and nominally limited some abuses.

France sold Louisiana to the United States in 1803, after its army failed to retake Saint-Domingue (Episode 38). Its second empire began with the conquest of Algiers in 1830 (Episode 28) and grew in West and Equatorial Africa, Madagascar, and Indochina. Its ideology of *mission civilisatrice* claimed to spread French civilization. In practice most colonial subjects were *sujets*, not citizens, and were governed by the *indigénat*, a code of summary administrative penalties. The inhabitants of Senegal’s Four Communes were an exception with voting rights. Blaise Diagne was elected to the French parliament in 1914.

## After

Episode 28 goes to Algeria, the settler colony France declared part of itself.

## Acts

### 1. New France

Quebec, the fur trade, and alliances with Indigenous nations. Jesuit *Relations* as a source that is also missionary propaganda. The Great Peace of Montreal (1701) as Indigenous diplomacy.

### 2. Saint-Domingue

Sugar and coffee, the plantation regime, and a colonial society of whites, free people of color (*gens de couleur libres*), and a large enslaved majority. The act establishes the colony’s wealth and violence, and hands off to Episode 38.

### 3. The Code Noir

The 1685 text read, with its articles on punishment, marriage, religion, and manumission. What it permitted, what it forbade, and how it was enforced or ignored. The act avoids both “it protected slaves” and “it was only cruelty”: it was law making human beings property.

### 4. Louisiana and the pivot

Louisiana’s French and Spanish periods, its enslaved population, and its sale in 1803. The link to Haiti’s victory is made explicit.

### 5. The second empire and its mission

The *mission civilisatrice* as ideology in speeches and schoolbooks. The *indigénat* as practice. The Four Communes and Blaise Diagne. French West Africa (1895) and Indochina (1887) as federations. The act refuses a Paris travelogue; the camera stays in the colonies and their archives.

## Comparative note

Britain separated self-governing dominions from ruled colonies (Episode 22). France claimed a single Greater France with a hierarchy of citizens and subjects. Both architectures excluded most colonized people from full rights, in different legal languages.

## How we know

Archives nationales d’outre-mer (Aix-en-Provence). The Code Noir text. Jesuit *Relations*. Saint-Domingue plantation and notarial records. Scholarship by Laurent Dubois, Gilles Havard on New France, Alice Conklin (*A Mission to Civilize*, 1997), and Gregory Mann on the *indigénat*.

## Do not claim

- That the Code Noir protected enslaved people in practice.
- That the *mission civilisatrice* describes what colonial rule did.
- That all French colonial subjects were citizens.
- That New France was a peaceful partnership; it was an alliance system with its own wars.
- That Saint-Domingue’s story ends as a French footnote; it is Episode 38.
