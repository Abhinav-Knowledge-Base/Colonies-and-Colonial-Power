## Before

Season 8 has covered footholds, China, Southeast Asia, the Philippines, and Japan’s colonies. A season on Asia under empire would normally put British India at its center. This series does not.

## The turn

British rule in South Asia was the largest single colonial system in Asia. Its political and social history — regions, religions, caste, economy, the national movement, Gandhi and his contemporaries, Partition, and the republic — belongs to *Discovery of India*, a separate documentary project with its own episodes, honesty rules, and archive. *Colonies and Colonial Power* already filmed the Raj once as an imperial system: revenue, army, law, and land (Episode 21). It will not narrate the subcontinent’s history a second time.

What this series can do is show how British India worked inside Asian empire as a whole. That means three connections. First, opium grown under Company and Crown monopoly in Bengal and Bihar was sold to China (Episode 58). Second, the Indian Army was deployed across Asia and Africa — to China in 1840 and 1900, Burma, Malaya, Mesopotamia, and East Africa — and Indian police served in Hong Kong, Shanghai, and Singapore. Third, indentured Indian laborers went to Malaya, Burma, Ceylon, Fiji, Mauritius, the Caribbean, and South Africa (Episodes 40, 59). These are comparative threads for this series. Their South Asian side is *Discovery of India*’s.

## After

Episode 63 turns to the Second World War in Asia: occupation, famine, and 1945.

## Acts

### 1. The refusal, stated

A title card and a plain statement: the history of the subcontinent under British rule and after belongs to *Discovery of India*. The act explains why. A series about colonial power as a world system would be swallowed by any one region told in full. Another project exists to tell this one properly.

### 2. What Episode 21 did

A short recap of Episode 21’s system view — revenue, army, law, land — and its stopping point in 1858.

### 3. Opium, troops, and indenture

A segment of about ten minutes. Opium to China: the monopoly and the trade. Indian troops and police in other colonies. Indentured migrants in Southeast Asia, the Indian Ocean, and the Caribbean. Each thread is tied to an episode of this series where the receiving colony is filmed.

### 4. Comparisons, not narration

How British India compares with Dutch Java, French Indochina, and Japanese Korea on revenue systems, armies, and labor export. A comparison board, not a history lesson.

### 5. Here the series stops again

A closing card repeats the handoff. The hour is shorter than usual, and that is deliberate. The remaining time is given to a short preview of Episode 63.

## Comparative note

The Dutch East Indies Army (KNIL) and French colonial troops (from Senegal and elsewhere) were also used across empires. Imperial armies recruited in one colony to police another were a system-level feature of colonial power.

## How we know

For this hour’s comparative threads: opium trade records, military deployment records, and indenture emigration registers, drawn from the imperial archive system including the India Office Records at the British Library, treated as imperial rather than regional sources. Scholarship on these threads includes Carl Trocki (*Opium, Empire and the Global Political Economy*, 1999), Thomas Metcalf (*Imperial Connections*, 2007), and Sunil Amrith.

## Do not claim

- That this series covers the history of India under British rule; *Discovery of India* does.
- That this episode narrates 1857, Gandhi, the national movement, Partition, or the republic.
- That British India was only an instrument of empire; its people had their own history, told elsewhere.
- That Indian soldiers abroad were simply imperial tools; they had their own motives and grievances.
- That the comparative threads replace a regional history.
