## Before

Episode 12 followed silver from Potosí. Some went to Seville. Much of the rest was drawn toward China, where Ming tax reforms had made silver the standard of payment. Spain needed a Pacific route.

## The turn

Miguel López de Legazpi’s expedition reached the Philippines in 1565. The same year Andrés de Urdaneta found the return route (*tornaviaje*) across the northern Pacific to Mexico. Legazpi took Manila in 1571 from Rajah Sulayman and allied Muslim rulers. Manila became the hub of the galleon trade. Chinese junks from Fujian brought silk, porcelain, and goods to Manila, and galleons carried them to Acapulco in exchange for silver. The trade tied the Americas, Asia, and Europe into one commercial system for 250 years.

Manila depended on Chinese merchants and artisans, known as Sangleys, who far outnumbered the Spanish. Spanish authorities confined them to the Parián district and taxed them, and feared them. In 1603, after rumors of a Chinese invasion and a Chinese uprising, Spanish forces with Filipino and Japanese auxiliaries killed perhaps as many as 20,000 Chinese residents. The figure comes from contemporary accounts and is uncertain. Further massacres or expulsions followed in 1639, 1662, 1686, and 1762. These were violence with causes: fear, dependence, debt, religious suspicion, and Spanish weakness. They are not a footnote.

## Acts

### 1. The tornaviaje

Urdaneta’s route and the Kuroshio current. Galleons as the largest ships of their day. Crossings of four to six months with high mortality among crews, many of them Filipino and Mexican sailors.

### 2. Manila, 1571

Legazpi, Rajah Sulayman, and the Muslim polities of Luzon. The founding of Intramuros. Filipino communities, the *principalía*, and the friars who became powers in the islands (Episode 60).

### 3. Silver meets silk

Ming China’s demand for silver, Fujian merchants, and the goods that crossed to Acapulco. Chinese porcelain found in Mexican archaeological sites. Manila as one link in a chain, not its center.

### 4. The Sangleys and 1603

The Parián, Chinese Catholic converts and non-converts, and the 1603 massacre with its causes and its uncertain toll. Later massacres and expulsions. The Ming court’s restrained response. No massacre montage; documents and places only.

### 5. The trade and its end

Mexican independence and the end of the galleon in 1815. Asians in colonial Mexico — Filipinos and others, often called *chinos*, including enslaved people from Asia. The act closes on the Pacific as a colonial space the Atlantic story usually forgets.

## Comparative note

The Dutch in Batavia also depended on Chinese residents and massacred them in 1740 (Episode 25). The pattern of economic dependence, fear, and massacre recurs in colonial port cities with large Chinese populations.

## How we know

Spanish colonial records in the Archivo General de Indias (Filipinas section). Chinese sources, including Ming records and Fujianese materials. Archaeology of galleon wrecks (the *San Diego*, 1600) and of Chinese porcelain in the Americas. Scholarship by William Lytle Schurz, Arturo Giráldez, Birgit Tremml-Werner, and Tatiana Seijas (*Asian Slaves in Colonial Mexico*, 2014).

## Do not claim

- That the galleon trade was only Spanish; Chinese, Filipino, and Mexican participants were essential.
- That the 1603 death toll is known precisely.
- That the massacres were spontaneous with no causes.
- That the Philippines was an empty or stateless archipelago in 1565.
- That Manila was the center of the silver trade; China’s demand drove it.
