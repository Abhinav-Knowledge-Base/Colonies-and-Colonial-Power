## Before

Episode 43 showed the limits of colonial power. In 1808 Napoleon invaded Spain and deposed the king. The crisis opened a question: who governed the Americas without a king?

## The turn

Juntas formed across Spanish America in 1808–10, claiming to govern in the captive king’s name, and moved toward independence. In New Spain, the priest Miguel Hidalgo launched a mass revolt with Indigenous and mestizo followers in 1810 (the *Grito de Dolores*). It was crushed, and Hidalgo was executed in 1811. José María Morelos continued it until 1815. Mexico’s independence came in 1821 through a conservative settlement led by Agustín de Iturbide. In South America, Simón Bolívar led campaigns in Venezuela, New Granada, Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia. José de San Martín crossed the Andes to liberate Chile (1817–18) and entered Lima (1821). The battle of Ayacucho (1824) ended Spanish power on the mainland.

The new republics were led mostly by creole elites. Many kept slavery for decades. Chile abolished it in 1823, Mexico in 1829, and Venezuela, Peru, and Argentina only in the 1850s. They abolished Indigenous tribute in law but often reimposed it or attacked communal land. Bolívar received aid in 1816 from Alexandre Pétion, president of Haiti, on condition that he free enslaved people. He did so only partially. Haiti was excluded from the Congress of Panama (1826). The United States’ revolution (Episode 20) influenced some leaders, but so did Spanish constitutional thought (the Cádiz Constitution of 1812), the French Revolution, and local traditions. 1776 was not a template.

## Acts

### 1. 1808

Napoleon’s invasion of Spain and the juntas in the Americas. The Cádiz Constitution (1812) and American deputies in Spain.

### 2. Hidalgo and Morelos

The 1810 revolt as a mass movement with Indigenous and mestizo followers. Violence on both sides, including the massacre at the Alhóndiga de Granaditas in Guanajuato. Morelos’s *Sentimientos de la Nación* (1813), calling for the abolition of slavery and castes. The conservative settlement of 1821.

### 3. Bolívar and San Martín

The campaigns, the Jamaica Letter (1815), Pétion’s aid, and Bolívar’s partial emancipation. San Martín’s Andean crossing. Black and Indigenous soldiers who fought on both sides, often promised freedom.

### 4. Who became free

Timetables of abolition across the republics. Indigenous tribute and communal land after independence. Creole republics as heirs of colonial hierarchy.

### 5. Comparing revolutions

Haiti, the United States, and Spanish America side by side, with no single model. Haiti’s isolation at Panama in 1826. The act closes with a map of new republics whose borders followed colonial administrative lines (*uti possidetis*), shown as drawn.

## Comparative note

Decolonization in Africa and Asia in the 20th century (Season 9) also produced states led by educated elites, and they also often kept colonial borders and hierarchies. The comparison is structural, not a claim that the cases were the same.

## How we know

Revolutionary documents (Hidalgo’s decrees, Morelos’s *Sentimientos*, Bolívar’s letters). Juntas’ records. Cádiz constitutional debates. Abolition laws. Scholarship by John Lynch, Jaime Rodríguez O., Eric Van Young (*The Other Rebellion*, 2001), Marixa Lasso on race and patriotism in Colombia, and Ada Ferrer on Haiti’s influence.

## Do not claim

- That Latin American independence abolished slavery everywhere at once.
- That creole independence liberated Indigenous peoples.
- That the United States was the sole model.
- That Bolívar freed all enslaved people as promised to Pétion.
- That new national borders were natural; they followed colonial administrative lines.
