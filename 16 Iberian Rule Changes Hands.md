## Before

Episodes 9–15 showed Iberian empires at their widest. They were never alone. Other Europeans ignored Tordesillas, and colonized people resisted from within.

## The turn

When the Portuguese king Sebastião died without heir at Alcácer Quibir in Morocco (1578), Philip II of Spain claimed the Portuguese throne in 1580. The Iberian Union lasted until Portugal restored its own dynasty in 1640. During the union, Spain’s enemies became Portugal’s. The Dutch Republic, at war with Spain, attacked Portuguese positions. The VOC took Malacca (1641) and most Portuguese forts in Ceylon and on the Malabar coast by the 1660s. The Dutch West India Company held Pernambuco and northeastern Brazil (1630–54) until Luso-Brazilian forces, including Indigenous and Black troops, drove them out. England and France seized Caribbean islands Spain could not defend.

Spain’s mainland American empire endured until the wars of independence of 1808–1826 (Season 6). The 18th-century Bourbon reforms tightened control and provoked revolts, including Túpac Amaru II’s in 1780 (Episode 65). In 1898 the United States defeated Spain and took Cuba (as a protectorate), Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines (Episodes 32 and 60). This hour previews those endings. It does not narrate them.

## After

Season 3 turns to Britain, beginning with English companies that set up “factories” before they claimed flags (Episode 17).

## Acts

### 1. One king, two empires

Philip II’s succession and the formal separation of the two administrations. The Portuguese empire kept its own councils. Union made it a target of Spain’s enemies.

### 2. The Dutch intrusion

VOC attacks on Portuguese Asia. The WIC’s Brazil under Johan Maurits (1637–44), with its artists and its sugar mills worked by the enslaved. The WIC’s capture of Elmina (1637) and Luanda (1641–48) to supply enslaved Africans. The Dutch are not liberators; they are another company empire (Season 4).

### 3. Brazil takes itself back

The Pernambucan insurrection (1645–54): Luso-Brazilian planters, Indigenous leaders such as Filipe Camarão, and the Black commander Henrique Dias. Their later place in Brazilian national memory is labeled as later memory.

### 4. The Bourbon squeeze

Eighteenth-century reforms: intendants, new taxes, and the 1767 expulsion of the Jesuits. Creole grievances and Indigenous revolts. A preview of Season 6 and Episode 65, with no narration of independence here.

### 5. 1898, previewed

The loss of Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States. A title card points to Episodes 32 and 60. The act closes Season 2 with Iberian rule changing hands — to rival empires, to creole republics, and to a new imperial power.

## Comparative note

Imperial rivalry transferred colonies without asking the colonized, as in Dutch Brazil, English Jamaica (1655), and US Puerto Rico. The same pattern appears in the Treaty of Paris (1763), the Congress of Vienna, and the post-1919 mandates.

## How we know

Iberian and Dutch state and company archives (Arquivo Histórico Ultramarino; Nationaal Archief; Archivo General de Indias). Dutch Brazil records and art (Frans Post, Albert Eckhout, whose paintings are labeled as colonial imagery). Scholarship by Charles Boxer, Evaldo Cabral de Mello, and John Lynch.

## Do not claim

- That Portugal ceased to exist as a political entity during 1580–1640.
- That the Dutch in Brazil were milder colonizers; the series compares institutions.
- That Eckhout’s paintings are ethnographic truth; they are commissioned colonial images.
- That Latin American independence is narrated here; it is Season 6.
- That 1898 ended colonialism in those territories; it changed the colonizer.
