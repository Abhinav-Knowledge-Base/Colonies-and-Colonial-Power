## Before

Episode 23 showed the empire straining under war. Television and popular history have often looked back on the empire with nostalgia: railways, cricket, parliamentary democracy, and uniforms. This hour replaces that with a ledger.

## The turn

The empire moved wealth in many forms: plantation profits, tribute through taxation, company dividends, interest on colonial loans, and cheap commodities. How much, and how much it mattered to Britain’s economy, is debated. Some estimates of “drain” are large and contested. Some economic historians argue the empire’s net benefit to Britain as a whole was smaller than assumed, while elites profited greatly. The series presents the debate without choosing a single number.

Some things were not returned at independence. Borders drawn by colonial officials stayed. English remained the language of law and administration in many former colonies. Colonies had accumulated large sterling balances — money owed by Britain for wartime goods and services — which Britain released slowly and on its own terms after 1945. Objects taken in war and collecting stayed in British museums (Episode 71). The nostalgia genre is itself a historical artifact to be examined, not a lens to use.

## Acts

### 1. What was extracted

Plantation wealth (Episode 18), company dividends, colonial tax revenue spent on imperial armies, and “home charges” paid from colonial revenues to London. The drain debate is presented as a debate with named positions.

### 2. Borders and language

Colonial borders that became national borders. English as the language of courts and elite schools. What independence kept from empire, and why new states often chose to keep it.

### 3. Sterling balances

A short, clear explanation, not a spreadsheet lesson. Colonies lent to Britain through the war. Britain repaid slowly, controlling access to the funds and thereby shaping postwar development. The act uses one colony’s case (for example, Egypt or the Gold Coast) as illustration.

### 4. The nostalgia genre

Clips from nostalgic empire programs and newsreels, examined critically. The visual grammar of golden light, orchestral music, and the absence of the colonized is taken apart. The act states the series’ refusal.

### 5. Suez, 1956, as a late scene

Britain, France, and Israel invade Egypt after Nasser nationalizes the Suez Canal. US financial pressure forces withdrawal. The act marks Suez as a late scene showing the empire’s limits, and hands the wider story to Episode 70.

## Comparative note

France’s postwar attempt to hold its empire through the French Union and war (Episodes 64, 69) and the Netherlands’ in Indonesia (Episode 64) show other ledgers. The question of what was not returned applies to all.

## How we know

Treasury and Bank of England records on sterling balances. Colonial budgets and home-charges accounts. Scholarship by Patrick O’Brien and Avner Offer on empire’s economics, Utsa Patnaik’s drain estimates (contested), and critiques of nostalgia (for example Paul Gilroy, *Postcolonial Melancholia*, 2005).

## Do not claim

- That a single number measures what the empire extracted.
- That railways, cricket, or parliaments prove the empire was beneficial.
- That the empire made Britain rich in a simple way, or made no difference.
- That independence returned what was taken.
- That Suez was the end of the British empire; it was a late scene.
