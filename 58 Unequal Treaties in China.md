## Before

Episode 57 showed Europeans on the edges of Asian empires. The Qing empire in 1800 was vast and wealthy, and it limited European trade to Canton (Guangzhou) under the Canton system. British merchants bought tea with silver and wanted something to sell. They found opium, grown in British-controlled Bengal.

## The turn

The East India Company’s opium, sold through private traders, flowed into China in growing volumes despite Qing prohibitions. In 1839 the imperial commissioner Lin Zexu confiscated and destroyed more than 1,000 tons of opium at Humen. Britain went to war (1839–42). The Treaty of Nanjing (1842) ceded Hong Kong Island, opened five ports, and required an indemnity. Supplementary treaties granted extraterritoriality, under which foreigners were tried by their own consuls, and most-favored-nation status. The Second Opium War (1856–60), fought by Britain and France, ended with the looting and burning of the Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan) and the Treaty of Tianjin and Convention of Peking. These opened more ports, legalized the opium trade, and allowed missionaries into the interior. Russia took large territories in the northeast in 1858–60. Chinese later called these the “unequal treaties.”

The Taiping war (1850–64) was a civil war led by Hong Xiuquan, who believed he was the brother of Jesus. It had roots in Chinese social crisis, famine, ethnic tensions, and population pressure, as well as in missionary influence. It was not caused by the West alone. Deaths are estimated at 20–30 million or more, an estimate. Qing regional armies defeated it, with some foreign help. The Qing attempted reform through the Self-Strengthening Movement and later the Hundred Days’ Reform (1898). The Boxer movement (1899–1901) attacked missionaries and Chinese Christians. The Qing court backed it, and eight foreign powers occupied Beijing. The Boxer Protocol (1901) imposed an indemnity of 450 million taels. The Qing dynasty fell in 1911–12.

## After

Episode 59 turns to Southeast Asia, divided among European empires.

## Acts

### 1. Tea, silver, and opium

The Canton system, the silver drain, and opium from Bengal. The act names the Company and British traders (Jardine Matheson) as the sellers. The Bengal opium economy is a clause here; its South Asian history belongs to *Discovery of India*.

### 2. Lin Zexu and the First Opium War

Lin’s letter to Queen Victoria, the destruction of opium, and the war. The Treaty of Nanjing read as text. Parliamentary opposition in Britain (Gladstone’s 1840 speech).

### 3. Treaty ports and extraterritoriality

Shanghai’s international settlement, consular courts, and customs under foreign management (Robert Hart’s Imperial Maritime Customs). A semi-colonial order with Chinese sovereignty compromised but not replaced.

### 4. The Taiping and the Qing response

Hong Xiuquan’s movement, its program, and the war. Death estimates with ranges. The Self-Strengthening Movement and the Qing’s own agency. “Backward China” rejected as an explanation.

### 5. Boxers and the fall

The Boxer uprising, the siege of the Beijing legations, and the eight-nation occupation with its looting and killings. The indemnity, partly remitted later by the US to fund scholarships. The 1911 revolution. The act closes on the “century of humiliation” as a later national narrative, labeled as later memory.

## Comparative note

Japan faced similar unequal treaties from 1854–58 and revised them by 1911 after rapid state-building, then became an imperial power (Episodes 32, 61). Siam negotiated unequal treaties and kept independence (Episode 59). China’s outcome reflected its size, the Taiping catastrophe, and the timing of foreign pressure, not cultural backwardness.

## How we know

Qing archives (First Historical Archives of China). Treaty texts. British Parliamentary Papers and Foreign Office records. Missionary and consular reports. Scholarship by Jonathan Spence (*God’s Chinese Son*, 1996), Stephen Platt (*Autumn in the Heavenly Kingdom*, 2012), Julia Lovell (*The Opium War*, 2011), and Joseph Esherick (*The Origins of the Boxer Uprising*, 1987).

## Do not claim

- That China was a colony.
- That Qing China was “backward” and that this explains its defeats.
- That the Taiping war was caused by the West alone.
- That Taiping death tolls are known precisely.
- That the opium trade was a Chinese problem; it was a British commercial policy enforced by war.
