## Before

Episode 50 showed anti-slavery becoming a reason for intervention. In the mid-19th century missionaries and explorers went inland in greater numbers. Their work produced knowledge, converts, schools, and claims.

## The turn

Protestant and Catholic missions expanded across Africa in the 19th century: the Church Missionary Society in Sierra Leone, Nigeria, and Uganda; the London Missionary Society in southern Africa; the White Fathers in East and Central Africa. African Christians led much of the growth. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a Yoruba man freed from a slave ship as a boy, became the first African Anglican bishop in 1864. His later marginalization by European missionaries showed the racial limits of mission ideals. Mission schools produced clerks, teachers, and later critics of colonial rule.

European explorers — Livingstone, Richard Burton, John Hanning Speke, Henry Morton Stanley — traveled along routes Africans and Swahili-Arab traders already used, with African guides, porters, and interpreters. Their maps and books presented them as discoverers. Quinine prophylaxis, used successfully on William Balfour Baikie’s Niger expedition in 1854, sharply reduced European deaths from malaria and made inland penetration easier. Explorers and agents signed “treaties” with African rulers. Many were signed under unequal conditions, in languages the rulers did not read, with terms that differed from what was explained. The Rudd Concession (1888), signed by the Ndebele king Lobengula, was the basis of the British South Africa Company’s claims, and Lobengula repudiated it. Maps and treaties became paper claims presented at Berlin (Episode 52).

## Acts

### 1. Mission stations

Stations, schools, printing presses, and translations. African converts and catechists as the main agents of Christian expansion. Missions as landlords and sometimes as refuges.

### 2. Crowther

His life from enslavement to bishop, his Niger mission, and his forced marginalization in the 1890s. An African Christian leader, not a missionary’s helper.

### 3. Explorers and their guides

Livingstone’s journeys and his African companions, including Chuma and Susi, who carried his body to the coast in 1873–74. Stanley’s violent expeditions. Burton and Speke’s journey depended on the caravan leader Sidi Mubarak Bombay. Explorers’ maps as claims built on African knowledge.

### 4. Quinine and the “opening”

Baikie’s 1854 expedition, and quinine as a tool of empire. The phrase “opening Africa” examined as a colonial phrase. Quinine as science belongs to *The Last Five Hundred Years*; here it is a tool of rule.

### 5. Treaties on paper

The Rudd Concession and Lobengula’s repudiation, and Stanley’s treaties for Leopold. How treaties were made, translated, and used. The act closes with a stack of treaties ready for Berlin.

## Comparative note

In Asia, missionaries worked within states that remained sovereign or semi-sovereign (Qing China, Japan, Siam), and their work was tied to unequal treaties (Episode 58). In Africa, treaties signed by missionaries and agents became direct claims to territory.

## How we know

Mission society archives (CMS at the University of Birmingham; LMS at SOAS; White Fathers in Rome). Explorers’ journals and published books (claims). Treaty texts. African accounts of explorers, where recorded. Scholarship by J. F. Ade Ajayi, Andrew Porter, Johannes Fabian (*Out of Our Minds*, 2000), and Stephen Rockel on caravan porters.

## Do not claim

- That European explorers discovered places Africans already knew.
- That Livingstone narrates Africa.
- That missionaries were only agents of empire, or only humanitarians.
- That treaties signed with African rulers represented informed consent to cession.
- That quinine alone explains conquest.
