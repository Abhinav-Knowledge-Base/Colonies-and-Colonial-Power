## Before

Episodes 1–4 established that “colony” covered many arrangements. Without a vocabulary the series would blur a Barbadian plantation, a Dutch post at Deshima, and a farm in Kenya’s highlands into one thing.

## The turn

Historians often distinguish settler colonies, where the colonizers came to stay and wanted land, from extractive or exploitation colonies, where a small colonial class governed a large colonized population to extract labor, taxes, or commodities. Between these sit trading-post empires (forts and factories on coasts, with little territorial rule), plantation colonies (land worked by imported coerced labor, often with few settlers), and protectorates (indirect rule through local rulers who kept titles and some authority).

These are models. Real colonies moved between them. The Cape began as a VOC refreshment station and became a settler colony (Episode 25). Algeria was a conquest colony that became a settler colony claimed as part of France (Episode 28). Kenya was a protectorate and colony with a settler minority (Episode 54). The distinction is useful because each type tends to produce different violence, different law, and different endings. It is not a law every colony fits.

## Acts

### 1. Four photographs, four types

Four images, each captioned with a date and archive: a Caribbean plantation great house and mill; a fortified factory on a coast; a settler homestead; a sultan’s palace under a British Resident. The act names each type and says where the series returns to it.

### 2. Settler colonies

Land is the object. Settlers aim to replace Indigenous sovereignty with their own. The act names Patrick Wolfe’s argument that settler colonialism follows a “logic of elimination” and that invasion is “a structure, not an event” (Wolfe, 2006). The claim is presented as an argument that has shaped the field, and critics who say it can flatten Indigenous survival and variation are named too. Cases the series will cash: North America (Episode 19), Australia and Aotearoa (Episode 19), Algeria (Episode 28), Southern Africa and Kenya (Episode 54).

### 3. Extractive and plantation colonies

Labor and commodities are the object. A small colonial class, large colonized or enslaved populations. The Caribbean sugar islands (Episodes 10, 18, 35), the Cultivation System in Java (Episode 26), the Congo Free State (Episode 29). Plantation colonies with few settlers and many enslaved people can be both extractive and violent at the scale of the household.

### 4. Trading posts and protectorates

Forts and factories: Elmina, Macao, Deshima, Surat. Protectorates and princely arrangements: the Malay sultanates, Morocco after 1912, Buganda. Indirect rule as a policy with a name — Lugard’s *The Dual Mandate* (1922) — is previewed for Episode 56.

### 5. When models fail

Cases that move between types or fit none: the Philippines under Spain (Episode 60), Brazil (Episode 14), Ireland as a debated case (Episode 19). The act ends with a rule for every later hour. Name the type as a model, then show the specific institutions.

## Comparative note

The Russian empire’s expansion over land (Episode 31) produced settler colonization without an ocean. The United States was a settler state that also held overseas extractive possessions (Episode 32). The categories cross the maritime/land divide.

## How we know

Colonial legislation on land and labor. Settlement and census records. The historiography of settler colonial studies (Wolfe, Lorenzo Veracini) and its critics, and comparative economic history on institutions. Indigenous scholarship on sovereignty and survival.

## Do not claim

- That every colony was either settler or extractive.
- That Wolfe’s “logic of elimination” is a proven law; it is an argument.
- That extractive colonies were milder because fewer settlers came; the series compares institutions, not moral rank.
- That protectorates left local rulers fully sovereign.
- That settler colonies ended with independence from the metropole (Episode 72).
