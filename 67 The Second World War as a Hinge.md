## Before

Episode 66 showed mass movements growing. In 1939 Europe went to war. France and the Netherlands were occupied in 1940. Britain fought on, depending heavily on its empire.

## The turn

Colonial soldiers fought on every front: the Indian Army (Episode 23), the King’s African Rifles and Royal West African Frontier Force in East Africa and Burma, the *tirailleurs sénégalais* and North African troops who made up a large share of the Free French forces, and the KNIL in the Dutch Indies. In December 1944 French troops fired on West African soldiers who protested unpaid back pay at Thiaroye, near Dakar. The official toll was 35 dead, and historians argue the real number was higher; the archive is incomplete.

In August 1941 Roosevelt and Churchill issued the Atlantic Charter, which declared “the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live.” In September Churchill told Parliament it applied to Europe under Nazi occupation, not to the British empire. Colonized nationalists quoted it anyway. It was hypocritical and useful. Japan’s conquest of Southeast Asia in 1942 (Episode 63) showed that European powers could be defeated. France’s Brazzaville Conference (1944) promised reform but rejected independence. On 8 May 1945, VE Day, a nationalist demonstration at Sétif in Algeria led to killings of Europeans and a massive French repression. The Algerian toll is disputed, with figures from about 1,000–1,500 (French official) to 45,000 (Algerian official), and historians generally estimate several thousand or more. The Fifth Pan-African Congress in Manchester (October 1945), with Nkrumah, Jomo Kenyatta, and W. E. B. Du Bois, demanded independence. The war ended with European empires financially weakened, the United States and the Soviet Union opposed to formal colonialism for their own reasons, and a new United Nations whose charter included self-determination.

## After

Episode 68 examines the forms independence took: constitutions, partitions, federations, and dominions.

## Acts

### 1. Colonial soldiers

Soldiers from India, Africa, the Caribbean, and Southeast Asia on many fronts. Their letters, their pay, and their expectations. Thiaroye with its disputed toll.

### 2. The Atlantic Charter

The text, Churchill’s September 1941 qualification, and how nationalists used it. A document that was hypocritical and useful at once.

### 3. 1942

The fall of Singapore and the Dutch Indies from a colonial perspective. European prestige broken. A cross-reference to Episode 63, not a retelling.

### 4. 1945: Sétif and Manchester

Sétif and Guelma with disputed tolls and no imagery of massacre. The Manchester Pan-African Congress and its demands. Two faces of 1945.

### 5. A new order

The UN Charter’s Chapter XI on non-self-governing territories. US and Soviet positions. European weakness. The act closes by marking the war as a hinge, not a cause: movements already existed, and the war changed the balance.

## Comparative note

The First World War also produced colonial soldiers’ claims and broken promises (Episode 23; the “Wilsonian moment” of 1919). The difference in 1945 was that European powers were much weaker and the movements much stronger.

## How we know

Military records. The Atlantic Charter and parliamentary debates. French records of Thiaroye and Sétif, some only recently opened. The Pan-African Congress record (George Padmore, ed., 1947). Scholarship by Ashley Jackson (*The British Empire and the Second World War*, 2006), Martin Thomas, Armelle Mabon on Thiaroye, Jean-Pierre Peyroulou on Sétif-Guelma, and Erez Manela on 1919.

## Do not claim

- That the Second World War caused decolonization by itself.
- That the Atlantic Charter was meant to apply to colonies by its authors.
- That the Thiaroye or Sétif death tolls are settled.
- That colonial soldiers fought only because they were forced, or only from loyalty.
- That this episode is a history of the Second World War.
